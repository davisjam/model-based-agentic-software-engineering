\section{The question I asked}\label{heading:the-question-i-asked}

In the Preface, I asked:

% <!-- inline-quote -->

\begin{quote}
How do we safely grant autonomy to commodity intelligence?
\end{quote}

Software engineering provided a concrete setting for the question: if an agent can perform consequential work at extraordinary speed, what must the surrounding environment provide before we can safely grant that capability useful autonomy?

The seven chapters developed an answer. \magevalue{chapter:problem} identified two engineering problems arising from the delegation of implementation: Representation and Enforcement. Chapters \magevalue{chapter:modeling|num} and \magevalue{chapter:alignment|num} developed a repertoire of engineering models and governance mechanisms, and \magevalue{chapter:one-problem-many-models} showed how I used them while building a system. \magevalue{chapter:software-factories} examined the software factories that such engineering can produce, while \magevalue{chapter:theory} considered their economics. \magevalue{chapter:profession} explored what these developments might mean for the engineering profession.

I developed this answer through software engineering because that is where my engineering expertise lies. The underlying question reaches beyond software and perhaps beyond the conventional engineering disciplines. Its answer in another domain will depend on the representations, evidence, and controls available there.

\section{The core of MAGE}\label{heading:the-core-of-mage}

MAGE rests on two principles, Modeling and Alignment. Through their repeated application, engineers can develop an environment that preserves useful engineering judgment for subsequent work.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Model what matters.} Choose the reduction that makes the engineering question answerable without carrying unnecessary detail. A purposeful model can expose properties that humans and agents would otherwise have to reconstruct from implementation, making them available for analysis, review, and evidence. Build the model when those benefits repay its cost, and maintain the correspondences on which its use depends.
\item
  \textbf{Enforce what must hold.} Guidance influences behavior; enforcement determines which actions or results the environment will accept. Some obligations can be enforced directly through types or permissions that restrict actions or artifacts. Where tests or validators check an obligation, a gate can reject work when the check fails. Others become decidable only after a suitable representation exposes the relevant semantics. Leave consequential decisions to human judgment when the environment lacks the meaning or evidence needed to decide them adequately.
\item
  \textbf{Make useful judgment reusable.} When a recurring or consequential engineering decision is worth carrying forward, convert it into a model, mechanism, architecture, procedure, or body of evidence that later work can inherit. Such structures become engineering capital when they improve later work. Reusable machinery can also make it less expensive to establish further models and controls. The engineering environment preserves the consequential concern while allowing the particular structure that carries it to be corrected, replaced, or retired.
\end{enumerate}

% <!-- index-def: governance-as-design-patterns -->

Together, these moves produce the \textbf{governed engineering environment}. Models represent consequential knowledge, controls constrain actions or evaluate results, and evidence supports engineering judgments. People remain responsible for the resulting system. In software, the representations include requirements, architectures, behavioral models, dependencies, provenance, and measurements; the governance mechanisms are constraints, sensors, and gates. Other domains will supply different representations and different means of evidence and control.

That is Model-Based Agentic Engineering.

\section{The New Engineering Opportunity}\label{heading:what-changes-next}

When commodity intelligence makes realization cheaper, the cost of the surrounding engineering work becomes more important. If a software factory can produce changes faster than engineers can establish requirements, evaluate evidence, or maintain control over the resulting system, improving implementation further does not solve those problems. Engineering attention moves to them.

% <!-- point: neither-problem-has-a-fixed-solution | As agents improve, they can help construct models and engineering machinery; the environment governing their work can become more capable too. -->
% <!-- point: richer-machinery-is-the-opportunity | Commodity intelligence may make model-based engineering practical at smaller organizational scales by reducing the cost of constructing and maintaining its machinery. -->
% Evidence: E01 | source: book/data/local-evidence/evidentiary-basis-11202026/integration/records/E01.md | kind: author-account
But commodity intelligence also gives us new means of addressing those problems. Engineers can use agents to construct models, develop analyses, generate implementations, and build the machinery that evaluates and enforces engineering obligations. As we saw in \magevalue{chapter:profession}, these capabilities may make model-based engineering practical at a much smaller organizational scale. I was able to construct a modeling and governance environment suited to my own engineering problems while building and operating the product it supported. I could then use that environment to reason about DocAble, investigate alternative designs, and govern subsequent changes.

The opportunity is therefore greater than inexpensive implementation. We can reconsider which engineering techniques we can afford to employ, which tools we can afford to build, and which systems we can undertake to engineer.

\section{How much specification, and of what kind?}\label{heading:how-much-specification-and-of-what-kind}

% <!-- point: the-encoding-limit-has-two-implications | Making intent explicit can support both realization and assurance: helping an agent produce the intended result and helping the engineer establish that it has done so. -->
In \magevalue{chapter:problem}, I asked how much direction, and of what kind, an agent needs to produce an acceptable result. We can now distinguish two reasons for making engineering intent explicit: to help the agent realize it, and to help the engineer establish that it has been realized.

% <!-- point: two-open-questions-how-much-and-in-what-form | How much intent to make explicit and in what representation can have different answers for realization and assurance. | terms: specification-frontier -->
% <!-- point: commodity-intelligence-moves-the-frontier | Better interpretation can reduce the need for explicit instructions while precise representations remain useful for mechanical analysis, generation, and assurance. | terms: specification-frontier -->
As agents become better at interpreting incomplete descriptions, engineers may need to specify less to obtain a useful result. But we may still want precise representations of selected properties so that we can analyze designs, generate parts of implementations, or evaluate the resulting systems mechanically. The degree of semantic commitment appropriate for realization need not be the degree appropriate for assurance.

Agents can also reduce the cost of constructing and maintaining those representations and their associated machinery. How much we choose to model, and how much of Alignment we choose to mechanize, will depend on the capabilities of the agents, the engineering obligations, and the economics of the available alternatives.

\section{The Authority and Responsibility of the Engineer}\label{heading:the-part-that-stays-yours}

% <!-- point: no-stable-set-of-human-only-tasks | The engineer's role need not rest on a fixed collection of human-only tasks; what persists as capabilities change is informed control over consequential work. -->

I would not define the engineer by a permanent collection of tasks that only humans can perform. The boundary will move as capabilities improve. Engineering competence will change with it, as it has changed with earlier technologies. What persists is the need for informed control over consequential work: understanding what the engineering arrangement can establish, recognizing where its evidence is insufficient, and accepting responsibility for the decisions made under its authority.

% <!-- point: responsibility-extends-to-the-structures-that-decide | Responsibility need not mean making every decision personally; MAGE moves suitable decisions and decision-making processes into engineered structures, and the engineer's responsibility extends to designing, governing, and revising them. -->

That responsibility need not mean personally making every decision. Indeed, MAGE is an argument for moving suitable decisions and decision-making processes into engineered structures so that they can operate more reliably, consistently, and economically. The engineer\textquotesingle s responsibility extends to the design and governance of those structures, including the judgment to revise them when their assumptions no longer hold.

The question from the Preface was how to grant autonomy safely. My answer is to engineer the environment in which autonomous work takes place. We can establish representations through which agents reason, mechanisms that protect the obligations we have established, and evidence through which we evaluate their work. As commodity intelligence becomes more capable, we can use it to improve that environment too. The responsibility for establishing and maintaining informed control remains ours.

MAGE worked for me. I hope it proves useful in your engineering practice, too. If you try it, I would be glad to hear what works, what does not, and what you discover along the way.
