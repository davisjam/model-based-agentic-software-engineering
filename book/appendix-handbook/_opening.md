A service has the right address but lacks permission to call its dependency. A document repairer needs freedom to choose a correction without unrestricted authority to rewrite the artifact. A new reasoning engine could improve cost or quality, but replacing it should not require rebuilding the document machinery. An engineering agent reports completion, yet the engineer still needs evidence of what landed and what can safely be recovered. A validation suite is costly to repeat, but skipping checks requires a defensible account of what a change can affect. These are five reasons to construct MAGE machinery.

Each blueprint follows a common format, drawing on the design-pattern tradition exemplified by Gamma, Helm, Johnson, and Vlissides [cite: gof1994]. *Problem* identifies the failure or capability that motivates the arrangement. *Conceptual Design* explains how its representations and mechanisms interact. *Engineering Models* identifies the representations, their meanings and authority, the questions they answer, and their consumers. *Realization* describes construction and operation, including the verification and evaluation needed to establish that the mechanisms work. *Adaptation and Limits* explains where the arrangement applies and what it does not establish. [ref:chapter:appendix-a-designing-your-own-blueprint] applies this format to a new engineering problem.

The designs are grounded in DocAble, with construction advice for analogous arrangements in other systems. The engineer chooses the obligations, establishes what each mechanism can decide, and retains judgments for which the available evidence is insufficient. Appendix B complements these blueprints with practical guidance for choosing where to begin, migrating an existing environment, operating it, and measuring whether it pays.
