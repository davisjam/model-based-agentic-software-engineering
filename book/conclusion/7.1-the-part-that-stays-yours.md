## The question we asked

In the Preface, we asked:

> How do we safely grant autonomy to commodity intelligence?

The question was deliberately not "How do we generate more code?"
Commodity intelligence had already made implementation abundant enough to expose the harder problem.
If an agent can produce and modify software at extraordinary speed, what must the surrounding engineering environment provide before we can safely grant that capability useful autonomy?

Six Parts later, the book has an answer.

Give humans and agents representations suited to the questions they have to answer.
Give important obligations authority where the environment can evaluate them honestly.
When experience reveals knowledge or judgment that future work should not have to repurchase, convert the lesson into durable engineering structure—and maintain that structure only while it continues to earn its keep.

Modeling extends what can be reasoned over.
Alignment determines what the environment will require or permit.
Governance conversion lets useful judgment survive the task that produced it as engineering capital.

But that answer raises a converse question, and it is the one on which this book should end:

**What cannot be delegated?**

## The answer in three moves

Strip MAGE to three engineering moves and this is what remains.

1. **Work at the right representation.** Do not force a reasoner to reconstruct every system property from implementation when a smaller, purposeful model exposes the property directly. Model where the representation repays its cost, and keep honest the correspondence the model claims.

2. **Put authority where the obligation is decidable.** Guidance aims the work; authority binds it. Some obligations can be enforced directly through types, permissions, tests, validators, or gates. Others become decidable only after the right abstraction exposes the relevant structure. Leave genuinely semantic or consequential decisions with judgment rather than pretending machinery has settled them.

3. **Make useful judgment durable.** When a recurring or consequential engineering decision is worth carrying forward, externalize it into a representation, mechanism, architecture, procedure, or body of evidence that later work can inherit. Treat that structure as capital, not scripture: maintain it, reconcile it, and retire it when its return no longer pays its carrying cost.

<!-- index-def: governance-as-design-patterns -->
Together, those moves produce the **governed engineering environment**: representations carry what must be understood; mechanisms carry what can be decided repeatably; evidence exposes what happened; and human judgment remains responsible for what the machinery cannot honestly settle.

That is Model-Based Agentic Software Engineering.

## What it asks of you

### If you are an engineer

Start with the places where judgment is being purchased repeatedly.

Where do you repeatedly reconstruct the same map of the system? Where does the same defect class return? Which review repeatedly asks the same question? Which obligation is clear but lives only in someone's memory? Which task still consumes human attention because it genuinely requires judgment—and which does so merely because nobody has engineered a better representation or boundary?

Do not begin by adopting the entire MAGE apparatus. Pick one recurring cost. Ask whether the missing asset is a representation, an authoritative mechanism, or both. Make one durable improvement, observe what changes, and let the next bottleneck determine the next investment.

That is MAGE in miniature: **engineer the environment rather than merely ask the next agent to try harder.**

### If you lead engineers

Do not begin with a leaderboard of coding assistants or a mandate to deploy one agent architecture everywhere. Begin with the engineering environment.

Where does important engineering knowledge live? Which obligations are repeatedly re-explained or re-reviewed? Where does human attention remain in the loop because the property is genuinely judgment-laden, and where only because the organization has not engineered a better boundary? Which models, controls, and evidence-producing mechanisms are earning their maintenance cost, and which survive only because nobody owns their retirement?

Different organizations will answer those questions differently. The industrial cases in this book did. The lesson is not to copy their platforms. It is to identify the recurring reasoning, risk, and coordination costs that your own environment is failing to carry.

### If you are learning engineering

Do not define engineering competence by the tasks that happen to require human implementation today. That boundary is moving too quickly to build a profession around it.

Learn instead to choose useful abstractions, reason about systems at more than one level, distinguish evidence from assertion, decide what can honestly be automated, recognize what remains a matter of judgment, and understand why a system should be trusted before accepting it. Learn enough implementation to understand what realization means and enough engineering to know that realization is not the whole job.

Commodity intelligence changes which activities are expensive. It does not remove the need to know what should be built, what properties matter, what evidence is sufficient, or who is responsible for the result.

## The part that stays yours

We began with a machine capable of building almost anything you describe—and only what you describe.
That capability is both the opportunity and the engineering problem.

You cannot make a probabilistic reasoner deterministic by wishing harder at the prompt.
But you can decide what world it reasons through, what actions it may take, what evidence its work owes, which obligations the environment can hold, and which decisions it never gets to make alone.

The enduring profession therefore cannot be defined as whatever tasks the machine still fails at this year.
Those tasks will move.

The durable boundary is responsibility.

**You decide what should exist and what "correct" means for it. You remain responsible for the abstractions through which the system will be understood. You decide what evidence is enough, which obligations deserve authority, and which tradeoffs remain matters of judgment. You change the environment when its assumptions fail. And you remain answerable for the resulting system after the last keystroke has been delegated.**

Implementation became cheaper. None of that did.

The future of software engineering is therefore not merely faster implementation. It is a more recognizably engineering arrangement of the work.

Classical definitions of software engineering remain substantially right. The discipline was never defined as typing code, but by systematic, disciplined approaches to developing, operating, and maintaining software. What changed is the economics of realization. As implementation becomes abundant, representation, architecture, evidence, validation, governance, and judgment become relatively more valuable.

Other engineering disciplines have long separated engineering responsibility from physical realization. Software is not identical to them: it remains unusually mutable, and many of its constraints are authored rather than imposed by physics. But the professional shape increasingly rhymes.

Commodity intelligence did not abolish software engineering. By making implementation cheap, it may finally force us to practice more of it.
