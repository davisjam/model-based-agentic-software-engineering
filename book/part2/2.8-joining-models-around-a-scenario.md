# Joining Models Around a Scenario

## The scenario: what the system does for someone

> #### Scenario / joined view · Behavioral
>
> - **Engineering question** — Does the whole system still accomplish the user's goal through valid states once the separate models are composed?
> - **Model** — one goal walked end to end across the other models — the join an engineer performs, not a seventh mega-model the system stores.
> - **Property** — the goal completes only through a sequence of valid states, with every cross-cut — ownership, authorization, a measured bound, provenance — satisfied at each hop.
> - **Quality attribute** — end-to-end correctness, the composite of what the other models each guarantee.

<!-- point: the-scenario-walks-a-goal-and-holds-the-models-honest | The scenario walks a goal and holds the models honest. | terms: scenarios-view -->
<!-- index-def: scenarios-view -->
*Does a real goal actually work, end to end, across everything the separate models describe on
their own?* A **scenario** — a use case, a journey — walks a real goal end to end, and in walking it
exercises the other models and confirms they hold up. A structural model says what the system is, a
behavioral model what it does over time, an ownership model who is responsible, a deployment model
where it runs — each a still portrait of the same connected system. The scenario sets them in
motion.

**The scenario is where the models are held honest.** The separate models are not parallel
documentation; they are a reasoning system projected from one connected representation, and the
scenario removes the burden of validating each model in isolation by exercising all of them through
a single engineering goal.

<!-- point: a-user-journey-anchors-the-scenario | A user journey anchors the scenario; models enter where used. | terms: user-journey-model -->
One type anchors the walk. A **user journey** names the interfaces a user moves through and the
actions they take to accomplish something of value. Each model in this chapter serves that walk, and
each is introduced where it enters. The chapter builds to a flagship demonstration — four models
joined on a single fact, so a product goal reaches all the way to how the deploy rations its tests.

## The user-journey model {#user-journey-model}

<!-- section-terms: user-journey-model -->

<!-- point: the-user-journey-model-copies-nothing-and-the-others-join-to-it | The user-journey model copies nothing; the other models join to it. | terms: user-journey-model, dependency-correctness, join-key -->
<!-- index-def: user-journey-model -->
The user-journey model makes the product's journeys first-class typed entities — each an actor
pursuing a goal through ordered steps, every boundary-crossing step joined to the endpoint it calls.
It assesses **dependency correctness**: does every step a journey declares have a real call site, and
is every real call declared? A call-site drift lint checks both directions. The idea that makes it
the join's entry point is that the journey *copies nothing* — a step's endpoint references the
service-flow model, its call-site anchor references the real code, and those two references are the
keys the coverage and placement models reach through. **Name the journey once and the other models
join to it.**

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [user-journey model](appendix-b-user-journey-model.html).

The same walk turns on the fleet as well as the product: pointed at the agents that build the
software rather than the users it serves, the scenario becomes **method parity** — the substrate
that produces the software is governed by the same machinery pointed at the product. Part IV takes
that up where it operates the fleet; the appendix carries its
[full model](appendix-b-agent-orchestration-model.html).

## The feeder models {#feeder-models}

<!-- section-terms: journey-criticality-test-placement, coverage-model-mapping, node-coverage, journey-task-closure -->

Three small models feed the flagship, each answering one question about a journey's tests. They are
named here with the single property each contributes; their full construct-and-invariant treatments
are in the appendix, and the join below shows each doing its part.

<!-- point: a-journeys-criticality-derives-its-test-tier-never-stored | A journey's criticality derives its test tier, never stored. | terms: journey-criticality-test-placement, placement-soundness, derivation-direction -->
<!-- index-def: journey-criticality-test-placement -->
- **Journey-criticality → test-placement** — the **Selector** in the join. It derives which host tier
  a journey's tests run in from the journey's criticality: a major part earns the fast local tier and
  the full staging matrix, a minor part runs staging only. It assesses **placement soundness**, and its
  property is that *the tier is derived, never stored* — a stored tier literal is banned, so you cannot
  quietly push a major path off the slow local gate; to move it you must demote it to minor, a visible
  edit. [Learn more](appendix-b-journey-criticality-test-placement.html).

<!-- point: projecting-coverage-onto-nodes-makes-an-untested-invariant-visible | Projecting coverage onto nodes makes an untested invariant visible. | terms: coverage-model-mapping, node-coverage, invariant -->
<!-- index-def: coverage-model-mapping -->
<!-- index-def: node-coverage -->
- **Coverage → model-node mapping** — projects the test suite's coverage onto the model's *nodes* —
  states, seams, invariants, journey endpoints — instead of its lines. It assesses **node-coverage
  adequacy**, and its property is that *an untested invariant cannot hide inside a 95% line number*,
  because the invariant is a node, and a node is either covered or a visible gap.
  [Learn more](appendix-b-coverage-model-mapping.html).

<!-- point: task-closure-types-what-the-terminal-assertion-must-mean | Task-closure types what the terminal assertion must mean. | terms: journey-task-closure, closure-strength, flow-vs-task -->
<!-- index-def: journey-task-closure -->
- **Journey task-closure** — the three models above ask whether a journey's test *exists*, *runs*, and
  runs *in which tier*; none asks what the test asserts at the end. Split the pair it turns on: a *flow*
  assertion says the journey ran; a *task* assertion says the user got the thing they came for. **A test
  built on flow alone can green while the task is broken** — the artifact never painted, the file never
  downloaded. Task-closure promotes the journey's terminal "what *works* means" into a typed `closure`
  post-condition — a small boolean expression over reusable observable predicates, each preferring an
  accessibility observable because that is how the user perceives the task is done. A pure function then
  derives a **closure-strength** (`TASK_CLOSED`, `FLOW_ONLY`, or `DISABLED`), stored nowhere by hand; a
  `FLOW_ONLY` closure on a major journey is a build finding — the mechanical form of "a journey test
  should have caught it." [ref:task-closure-tree] draws the expression tree and the grade it derives.
  [Learn more](appendix-b-journey-task-closure.html).

<!-- label: task-closure-tree -->
<!-- figure: assets/task-closure-tree.svg | *Task-closure as an expression tree.* A boolean `closure` is an AND over observable predicates — the artifact rendered, a control is inspectable, no error-fallback shown. Two **flow-only** leaves (navigated to a URL; a route returned 2xx) are the weak ones: they prove a flow ran, not that the task closed. A pure `closure-strength()` reads the leaves and emits exactly one grade — `TASK_CLOSED`, `FLOW_ONLY`, or `DISABLED`. A `FLOW_ONLY` grade on a MAJOR journey is a build finding. -->

## Worked join — the journey↔coverage↔deploy-policy composition {#worked-join}

<!-- section-terms: models-join-not-repeat, join-key -->

<!-- point: four-models-join-on-one-fact-with-nothing-said-twice | Four models join on one fact, nothing said twice. | terms: models-join-not-repeat, join-key, traceability -->
<!-- index-def: models-join-not-repeat -->
**The compositional question:** *watch one fact — a journey is* major — *travel through four models
with nothing said twice, until a product goal reaches all the way to how the deploy rations its
tests.* The other model pages each show a single model; this one shows them **joining, not
repeating.** Four models compose on one shared key — the journey and its endpoints:

- the **user-journey model** (M13) names the journey and its endpoints;
- the **coverage-to-node map** (M5) asks whether those endpoints are tested at all;
- the **criticality-to-placement Selector** (M6) derives which host tier each test runs on from the
  journey's criticality;
- the **execution-policy Scheduler** (M7) decides *how* each host runs the tests it was handed — fan
  out, or serialize.

No model owns another's facts; each joins on the journey and its endpoints.

### The three questions the join answers

Three questions the raw deploy scripts and test config cannot answer on their own.

- **Goal-to-gate coverage**: *does a green local run mean every major journey actually ran?* The
  criticality floor makes "local-green implies every major path was exercised locally" a checked
  property, not a hope — and journey task-closure sharpens what *ran* means, proving the terminal
  assertion closes the user's task rather than merely reaching the last flow step.
- **Placement soundness**: *is any major journey mis-filed as staging-only?* Because the host tier
  is derived from criticality and stored nowhere by hand, you cannot quietly shove a major path off
  the local gate; you must demote it to minor, a visible edit.
- **Rationing correctness under cost and resource pressure**: *does the deploy fan tests out when
  it safely can, and serialize them only when it must?* The Scheduler honors a cost gate everywhere
  but rations concurrency only where a scarce box demands it, so a single-worker host serializes
  while an elastic host fans out, from one profile table.

### The composite picture

Everything so far has examined models individually. This figure is the first time they
cooperate. The join has no single appendix diagram, so this composite is built from the four real panels,
adding only the join edges between them; read it left to right, in [ref:join-composite].

### The invariants the join spans

The join's invariants span all four models, collected in [ref:scenarios-invariants]. All are safety properties; the liveness that concurrency hands the fleet is stated and routed with the behavioral model, whose form-match rule sends a liveness invariant to a temporal checker.

<!-- label: scenarios-invariants -->
<!-- table: Invariants of the Scenarios view — the properties the journey-to-test join spans and how each is checked. [short: Invariants of the Scenarios view] -->
| Invariant | How it is checked |
|---|---|
| Every major journey-part has a test in the fast local tier | Coverage-floor lint (M6) walks the model; a major part with no local test is a finding. |
| A journey's declared deps match its real call sites, both ways | Call-site drift lint (M13): every declared dep has a real call site, every call site is declared. |
| Every declared journey endpoint is exercised by some test | Undertested-journey audit (M5): join coverage to the endpoint nodes; an uncovered endpoint is a gap. |
| The host tier is a pure function of criticality | Derive-and-assert (M6): a stored tier literal is banned; the lint recomputes the derivation. |
| No deploy edge carries the `LOAD` intent | Load-edge lint (M7) reads the graph and the Scheduler's graph-resident intents; a `LOAD` edge is a finding. |
| Staging's test set is a superset of local's and of prod's | Containment property test plus a live-model lint (M6): recompute containment by calling the selector, not auditing a matrix. |

### The Selector and Scheduler in code

Two pieces of real policy code make the join concrete — the **Selector** derives a host's test
roster from the journey model, and the **Scheduler** rations that roster per host. The full listing
is in the [invariant-DAG execution policy](appendix-b-invariant-dag-execution-policy.html) and
[journey-criticality](appendix-b-journey-criticality-test-placement.html) appendix entries; the join
needs only the Scheduler's call and its result:

```python
plan = plan_for(host_profile)   # (permits, honor_cost_gate), from the profile alone
```

The three named hosts show the whole behavior in one table:

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — inline host→plan mapping strip making the Scheduler behavior scannable, a reference not a numbered cross-referenced float -->
| Host | Ceiling | Budget | Derived plan | Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `local` | 1 | 100.0 | 1 permit, honor cost gate | **serialize** — scarce VM, cost gate + load gate both apply |
| `staging` | `None` (elastic) | ∞ | all permits, cost gate relaxed | **fan out** — no box scarce, no budget |
| `prod` | 4 | 50.0 | 4 permits, honor cost gate | low finite smoke ceiling |

Moving the stress burst between hosts is a one-cell edit to the profile table, not to the deploy
graph — which is why `LOAD` stays out of the DAG.

The join keys chain, and a reader can walk them against the figure: the **endpoint** ties M13 to M5,

## The joined model

### The models pay off when they join

<!-- point: the-models-are-worth-more-joined-than-apart | The models are useful apart; their real value appears when they join. | terms: model-zoo -->
Each model earns its keep separately. The structural model answers a dependency question, the
behavioral model a concurrency one, the ownership model a responsibility one, the deployment model a
placement one, and the scenario a goal. Each is a projection: it keeps the entities and relations its
question needs and drops the rest. But a real engineering task rarely stays inside one model. It
starts in one and reaches across the others. The larger value shows up there — not in any single
projection, but in what the projections do when they meet.

### A request walks the connected slice

<!-- section-terms: scenarios-view -->

<!-- point: a-request-traverses-the-models-along-one-connected-slice | One engineering question traverses the models along a single connected slice. | terms: scenarios-view, system-knowledge-graph -->
Trace a single request. A user asks for a feature. The scenario names the journey that delivers it.
That journey names endpoints, and the endpoints resolve to components and the dependencies among
them. Those components run as services and concurrent workers, ship inside packages someone owns, and
land on deployment targets. Tests, invariants, ownership, and policy attach along the walk. The agent
never carries the whole codebase. It carries the connected slice the question actually touches, and
the walk is what assembles that slice.

### The graph is what lets the projections meet

<!-- point: the-graph-lets-the-projections-meet-on-stable-identity | The system knowledge graph joins the projections on stable identity, not on hand-kept consistency. | terms: system-knowledge-graph -->
The walk works only because every model names the same entities. A service named in the structural
model is the same service the behavioral model schedules and the deployment model places. They agree
because they cite one identity, not because someone kept five diagrams consistent by hand. That is
the work the system knowledge graph does. The graph is not a sixth picture standing beside the
models. It is what lets the projections meet on stable identity. Each projection discards what its own
question ignores; the joins recover exactly the neighboring facts the next question needs. Five
independent diagrams drift independently. Five projections over shared entities join, reuse facts, and
expose a contradiction the moment one appears.

## Where Modeling meets Alignment

<!-- point: an-explicit-relation-can-carry-a-predicate | Once a relation is written down explicitly, the environment can attach a predicate to it. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, map-and-territory -->
This is also where the Modeling Thesis meets the Alignment Thesis. While a relation lives only in
someone's head — service A consumes queue B, journey J reaches endpoint E, worker W may run beside
worker X — nothing can check it. Write the relation down explicitly, and the environment gains
something to check against: only a worker of this class may consume that queue; every endpoint this
journey names must exist; these two workers must never hold the same lock. **Making the relation
explicit is the Modeling move. Attaching the predicate is the Alignment move.** The richer the
representation, the more of engineering judgment shifts from probabilistic reasoning toward a check
the build can run on its own.

<!-- point: a-model-earns-its-place-twice | A model earns its place twice — it compresses the system for reasoning, and it creates a surface for obligations. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
So a model earns its place twice. It compresses the system into a slice a bounded reasoner can hold,
and it creates a surface on which an obligation can become executable. The first payoff is why this
Part built the models. The second is why the next Part can act on them.

Both payoffs turn on the same discipline of selection: **choose the representation at the abstraction
where the question becomes answerable.** Pick the grain too coarse and the question has no purchase;
too fine and the reasoner drowns in detail the question never asked. The next Part states the twin of
this line for enforcement.

<!-- point: this-part-supplied-the-world-the-next-part-gives-it-authority | This Part supplied the structured world; the next Part gives that world authority. | -->
This Part supplied the world the fleet reasons through; the next Part gives that world authority. It
takes the relations this Part made explicit and teaches the environment to insist on them.

## The mental toolbox

<!-- point: the-mental-toolbox-is-the-parts-carry-away | The mental toolbox is the Part's carry-away: a model, a property, a quality per concern. | terms: model-zoo, scope-of-modeling, thesis-modeling -->
The Part opened with one question and promised a table at its close. Here it is. You came into
Part II able to say why models matter; you leave it able to *choose* one. We did not pick these
models because a methodology prescribed five boxes to fill — we began with what an engineer needed
to know, and each need selected a representation. Work back through the models this Part built on
DocAble — structure and ownership, behavior and execution, measurement and documentation — and a
small, reusable toolbox falls out, collected in [ref:mental-toolbox]. For each engineering concern
it gives the model that answers it, the property the model then lets you state, and the quality
attribute that property serves. The columns run in the order the opener named: name what you need to
reason about, reach for the model, read off the property, and land on a quality an engineer already
knows how to want.

|---|---|---|---|
| what exists, and what may depend on what | structural / component model | only permitted component and service edges exist; every in-scope directory is owned once | modularity, maintainability, security |
| which service may act on or reach which resource | decision / policy — the service-flow model | only declared service-to-resource relationships are legal | security, least-privilege, architectural integrity |

Six rows, five classes. Concurrency and ownership are not a sixth kind of model; they are a
specialization — an owner and a lease laid over the behavioral machine, and the queue structures
the owner claims from added to the structural one. The test of the Part is not whether you can
recite these rows for DocAble. It is whether you can fill the same table for a system of your own.

<!-- point: the-scenario-is-the-join-you-perform-across-the-toolbox | The scenario is the join you perform across the six models, not a seventh model the system stores. | terms: scenarios-view, models-join-not-repeat -->
The flagship earlier in this chapter walked the join move on the fleet's own models. The same move
composes the six product models above into a single end-to-end scenario: a document arrives, is
planned and split, remediated under a single owner with every change stamped, validated and merged,
its evidence derived, and delivered — one goal walked across all six, each hop governed by a different
model. The system keeps the six models separate, each pinned to its code by a drift check; the
scenario is the join *you* perform across them, not a seventh mega-model it stores.

## What each model hands forward

<!-- point: part-2-owns-the-chain-through-the-property-part-3-gives-authority | Part II owns the chain through the property; the next Part gives each property authority. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
The join showed the models meeting; the hinge above showed why an explicit relation can carry a
predicate. Here is what that leaves for the next Part, per model. Part II owns the chain through the
property and stops there on purpose — stating "only declared edges are legal" is a different act from
refusing the undeclared one, and refusing it is the next Part's work. So each model hands forward one
property, collected in [ref:ii-iii-handoff]. The next Part will build the authority that makes each
property bite, out of four archetypes: a **constraint** that makes the violation unexpressible, a
**sensor** that reads reality back out of the code, a **validator** that compares reality to the
model, and a **gate** that blocks the divergence before it ships. This Part names the property; it
does not design the mechanism.

<!-- label: ii-iii-handoff -->
<!-- table: What each model hands to Alignment — the property this Part establishes and hands to the next. The authority that will enforce each is the next Part's work, not pre-built here. [short: What each model hands forward] -->
| Model | Property it hands to Alignment |
|---|---|
| **Structural / component** | only permitted edges exist; each in-scope directory owned by exactly one component |
| **Decision / service-flow** | only declared service-to-resource edges are legal, each with its declared authentication |
| **Behavioral (state machine)** | illegal transitions unreachable; terminal states do not re-enter processing |
| **Concurrency / ownership** | at most one active owner or lease; a crash before ack re-delivers, never drops; failure never requeues except preemption |
| **Measurement** | latency, cost, and queue-depth ceilings stay within bounds |
| **Documentation / provenance** | generated docs and provenance stay consistent — every mutation stamped, every insertion recorded and surviving, the changelog a loss-free projection of the stamps |

<!-- point: a-modeled-property-with-no-authority-yet-is-where-the-work-is | A modeled property whose authority does not yet exist is exactly where the next Part begins. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
Two of these properties are modeled but not yet governed, and the honesty is the point. The
measurement model is fully built and instrumented, yet its production cost ceiling is report-only —
sensed, logged, never enforced. And the end-to-end scenario has no single gate: its correctness is the
conjunction of the six per-model properties, asserted together only by a whole-pipeline smoke, with no
typed state-graph walked to prove every path reaches completion through legal states. A property an
engineer can state, and a build cannot yet enforce, is not a hole in the book; it is the book pointing
at where the next Part, and the real work, begins.

## The scope of modeling

<!-- index-def: scope-of-modeling -->
<!-- point: this-book-is-an-introduction-the-doorway-outward | This chapter is the doorway outward. | terms: scope-of-modeling, model-zoo -->
MAGE does not replace established modeling disciplines. The models in this Part are deliberately
lightweight — enough structure to answer recurring engineering questions, support agent reasoning,
and permit mechanical checks. When the engineering question requires stronger analysis, use the
established formalism built for it.

<!-- point: graduate-when-the-goal-sharpens-past-the-sketch | Graduate tools when the quality goal sharpens into a measurable constraint. | terms: scope-of-modeling -->
There is a moment when a working sketch stops carrying its weight. **A sketch should become an
analytic model when the quality goal hardens from a soft preference into a constraint you can
measurably fail.** "Fast is good" is a preference a napkin sketch carries fine; sharpen it to "every
frame must finish inside 16 milliseconds" and you have a real-time system that needs schedulability
analysis, not a vibe about which parts look slow. The concern did not change; the preference became a
constraint, and the day a constraint can fail is the day the sketch has to graduate. That is the same
motion the Part's ladder describes from the other side: you climb a rung when a recurring engineering
question makes the next capability worth its cost.

<!-- point: a-protocol-property-needs-a-temporal-logic-model-checker | A protocol property needs a temporal-logic model checker. | terms: protocols-and-tla, temporal-logic, liveness-property -->
<!-- index-def: protocols-and-tla -->
> ### Inset — Protocols and TLA+ {#inset-tla}
>
> When a property is not about one component but a *protocol* — many actors interleaving over time, each taking steps in an order you do not control — a single state assertion is not enough, and even a bounded walk of one component misses the cross-actor races. You specify the protocol in a **temporal-logic language** (TLA+ is the best-known), stating the actors, their steps, and the safety and liveness properties the interleaving must satisfy. A model checker then explores every interleaving the actors can produce *under the stated model and bounds* and either establishes the properties for that model or hands you a counterexample trace. This is the heaviest tool the Part points to, reserved for the invariant whose failure lives in a schedule no example test will ever pick — a distributed reservation, a lease-and-preempt loop, a two-phase hand-off between services.

<!-- point: model-at-the-coarsest-level-that-answers-the-question | Model at the coarsest level that answers the question; descend only when it forces you to. | terms: scope-of-modeling, reasoning-horizon, map-and-territory -->
<!-- index-example: reasoning-horizon -->
One scaling move underlies all of this. The models tier — system, subsystem, component, module,
implementation — and a finite reasoner enters at the level its task needs, descending only as far as
the work forces. Every tool this chapter points to keeps some model honest against some territory at
the coarsest grain the task allows. **Model at the coarsest level that answers the engineering
question; descend toward implementation only when the question requires it.**

## Can alignment exist without models?

<!-- point: alignment-without-models-yields-weak-authority | Implicit representations support only weak authority; explicit models are the substrate for stronger guarantees. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
One question sits under everything this Part has built, and it is worth answering before the
next Part takes these models up as authority: can alignment exist without models at all?

Yes. Natural-language prompts, briefs, examples, and supervision guide agents successfully,
sometimes spectacularly. MAGE does not claim otherwise. A prompt is a representation too, only
a weak one: implicit, local, untyped, mostly opaque to tools, and hard to compose, validate,
or keep true as the system moves. It can steer a build. It cannot, on its own, state,
compose, validate, and enforce the obligations engineering assurance rests on.

So the claim is narrow, and worth stating exactly. Implicit or weakly structured
representations support correspondingly weak forms of authority. Explicit models are not a
prerequisite for useful software; they are the substrate on which progressively stronger
guarantees become possible. **Without explicit models, alignment cannot become
authoritative.**

Read the whole argument as one line.

> Natural-language intent can guide autonomous work.
> Explicit models transform that intent into an engineering substrate.
> Alignment transforms that substrate into authority.

That honest reading closes the argument. This Part walked each kind of model on real code and
showed the drift gate that holds each one to the code, so the map cannot quietly rot away
from the territory. What it has not shown is how you reach that state from a codebase that
carries none of it. The models are built; the next Part gives them authority.
