# Modeling Structure and Ownership

Before an agent changes a system it has to answer a handful of questions that raw code answers
slowly and inconsistently: *What is this made of? What depends on what? Who owns this piece? What
may cross this boundary? What third-party material does the build rest on?* An engineer can
reconstruct each answer by reading the tree — once. A fleet reconstructs it every task, and each
reconstruction is a chance to get it subtly wrong.

Structural models settle these questions once and hold the answer true. Each is a projection over
the connected system model that keeps the entities and relations bearing on structure and drops the
rest. Two of the classical architectural views live here — the **Logical view**, the functional
structure of what the system is, and the **Development view**, how the source is packaged and
owned — but the reader cares about the questions, not the Kruchten boundary, so this chapter is
organized by what you want to know.

## What the system is made of, and what depends on what

<!-- section-terms: service-flow-model, executable-source-of-truth, data-flow-diagram -->

> #### Service-flow model · Decision
>
> - **Engineering question** — Which service may call which, and reach which resource?
> - **Model** — a typed catalog of every service, its endpoints, their authentication, and the wires that join a caller to a callee.
> - **Property** — only declared service-to-service edges are legal, and each carries its declared authentication posture. One node, the public web tier, faces the outside; every other node is internal.
> - **Quality attribute** — security, least-privilege, architectural integrity.

A system of services grows edges the way a garden grows weeds. A quick call from one service to
another, added under deadline, becomes a permanent, undocumented dependency. Nobody decided the
graph should have that edge; it appeared. Under audit, *who can reach the billing service?* has no
answer you can trust. The first structural question is compositional, and it is this graph you need
to make answerable.

<!-- point: the-service-flow-model-answers-what-the-system-is-made-of | The service-flow model answers what the system is made of and what depends on what. | terms: service-flow-model, wiring -->
The first question is compositional: what services exist, and which one is allowed to call which?
Deploy config and handler code each hold a fragment of the answer, and neither can be queried for
the whole. The **service-flow model** holds it in one place — a typed catalog of every service,
its endpoints, their authentication, and the **wires** that join a caller to a callee.

Read the permitted calls as a declared graph — a short list of arrows. The quality service may ask
the generation service. The conformance service may ask the validator. The worker may reach exactly
five downstream services; the merge stage mirrors the worker. Only the web tier is public — it is
the one node that faces the outside, and everything else sits behind an internal, authenticated
door. That allow-list is six or so nodes, one of them public, and every edge that is *not* drawn is
forbidden.

Job: the reader understands service connectivity is an explicit allow-list, not an emergent property of HTTP calls. Question: Which service connections are authorized? -->

<!-- point: one-service-graph-generates-policy-and-reconciles-the-call-graph | One service graph generates the access policy and reconciles the real call graph. | terms: wiring, access-policy, model-to-code, model-from-code -->
What the model lets you establish is that declared architecture and running implementation cannot
silently diverge, and it earns that in **both directions**. The network access policy is *generated*
from the wires (model-to-code), so what a service may reach stays equal to what the model says it
may. The real call graph is *read back* and reconciled against the wires (model-from-code), so a
call nobody declared surfaces as a finding. A drawing of a service architecture goes stale the
afternoon someone adds an endpoint; this graph fails the build instead. [ref:service-model-structure]
shows the model feeding its generators on one side and its parity gates on the other.

<!-- label: service-model-structure -->
<!-- figure: assets/service-model-structure.svg | The service model feeds generators and parity gates. It is authoritative for what it generates and reconciled against reality for what it checks; either side diverging blocks the build. -->

One illustrative invariant carries the shape: **every declared wire resolves to a real cross-service
call, and every real cross-service call resolves to a declared wire.** A call-graph parity lint holds
both halves — an undeclared call is an ungoverned one, and it is exactly the thing the check refuses.
The natural drawing is a **data-flow diagram**: the model on the left, the generators and parity
gates it feeds on the right, each edge labeled with what flows across it rather than what decides.

The move to carry away is general. A decision model answers *who may do what.* Represent the
permissions explicitly — as data, not scattered call sites — and two powers follow. You can *check*
actual behavior against the declared policy, and you can encode part of the policy in a type so a
violation never compiles. Separate the *permission* from the *action*, write the permission down, and
let something compare the written policy to the running system. Network policy, firewall rules,
service-mesh authorization, and cloud access management are all this one model; the variation across
shops is only the enforcement rung — a firewall at runtime, a policy check at build, a type at
compile — not the shape of the model.

## Who owns each piece, and what may cross a boundary

<!-- section-terms: component-zone-model, code-zone, boundary-kind -->

> #### Component-and-zone model · Structural
>
> - **Engineering question** — Where am I, what may this file touch, and what senses a boundary violation?
> - **Model** — a typed catalog that gives every component a code zone: its directories, its tags, its boundary kind, the external seams it may cross — with one edge drawn forbidden.
> - **Property** — every in-scope source directory is owned by exactly one component, and only a sanctioned seam crosses a boundary. In particular, no ordinary code touches a powerful format library directly; all of it routes through one structured model per format.
> - **Quality attribute** — modularity and maintainability first; corruption-resistance (correctness) is the sharp secondary; keeping dangerous construction in one audited place serves security besides.

A large system rots along its dependency edges. Any file imports any other. A convenient shortcut in
one corner reaches across three subsystems, and six months later no one can say what depends on what.
DocAble's version of the failure is sharper than generic tangle. A powerful document library — the
kind that can rewrite a document's internal object structure — is a loaded weapon. Call it from the
wrong place and you can silently destroy the very accessibility structure the product exists to
build: the delivered file still opens and renders, but its remediation is gone, and the customer
finds out only with a screen reader.

<!-- point: the-component-zone-model-makes-ownership-a-queried-fact | The component-zone model makes ownership and boundary a queried fact. | terms: component-zone-model, ownership-correctness, boundary-soundness -->
<!-- index-def: component-zone-model -->
The next question is the one an agent asks before it edits a line: *where am I, and what may this
file touch?* The **component and zone model** answers it — a typed catalog that gives every
component a code zone (its focus directories, its tags, its boundary kind, the external seams it may
cross). "Which component owns this file" becomes a lookup, not a per-tool guess that each lint,
dispatcher, and context injector re-derives on its own and drifts on its own.

Draw the system as zones — a web tier, a worker tier, a cluster of small services, the storage and
queues, and the core that holds the rule engine and the per-format repair logic — and let dependency
edges run between them. One edge is drawn in red and forbidden: *ordinary code may not reach the raw
format library.* Every mutation of a format passes through one structured model dedicated to that format.
The model is the only door; the raw library lives behind it — a single sanctioned door with the
forbidden edge drawn around it.

Job: the reader understands ordinary remediation code reaches the format library only through a typed seam. Question: Where may document mutation occur? -->

<!-- point: ownership-and-boundary-become-lint-findings-not-slow-erosion | Ownership and boundary reaches become findings, not slow erosion. | terms: reverse-mapping, boundary-soundness -->
What the model lets you establish is that ownership and boundaries hold as the tree moves under
them. A reverse-mapping test reconciles the declared zones against the real directory tree in both
directions; a boundary lint refuses a reach outside a component's sanctioned seams. The illustrative
invariant: **every in-scope source directory is owned by exactly one component.** The test runs
reality-⊆-model over the declared source surface — the repository minus the paths that belong to no
component — so a moved or unowned directory is a finding while a vendored tree is exempt.
[ref:component-model-structure] shows the registry feeding every tool that reads ownership, with a
reverse-mapping test joining it back to the real tree.

<!-- label: component-model-structure -->
<!-- figure: assets/component-model-structure.svg | The component registry feeds the boundary lints, dispatch, and context injection — every tool reads the same ownership answer — and a reverse-mapping test joins it to the real tree in both directions. -->

A word on honesty of scale. The production structural model carries roughly a hundred and twenty
components. The teaching view keeps about seven boxes and that single forbidden edge; the production
model adds accounting buckets, deployment linkage, and the fan-out of front-end sub-parts. Strip
them. A model leaves things out because leaving things out is what makes the relevant property
visible — here, *which dependencies are legal, and what catches a violation.*

The move to carry away: find the one resource powerful enough that uncontrolled access to it is
dangerous — a raw database handle, a filesystem, a format library, a credential — and route all
access through a single typed seam. Now the model states a property worth defending: *the dangerous
thing is reachable from exactly one place.* That serves maintainability first — a preservation fix
made once at the seam holds for every caller — and correctness right behind it, because the
corrupting operation becomes unreachable from ordinary code.

This is the layered-architecture and dependency-rule tradition, sharpened: a structural model earns
its keep when it names an edge that must not exist, at a resolution precise enough to police.

### Example — Spotify
Spotify externalizes its component topology and keeps it current by construction. A service catalog and
a System Model span thousands of repositories; ownership and dependency edges are written down, and the
diagrams stay synced because a tool generates them from the same source the services declare. What the
estate does not carry is a blocking gate. The model describes the topology faithfully, yet nothing fails
a build when a service drifts from it. This is the component-and-zone model's own function — topology made
an explicit, agent-facing fact — realized without the drift gate.

### Example — Cloudflare
Cloudflare leads with policy, not system structure, and still externalizes its topology in a catalog of
the same shape. The reach of the pattern is the point: even a shop whose signature representation is a
curated policy corpus writes its component estate down where an agent can resolve it. Structure gets
externalized wherever a fleet has to navigate it. The variation is only how far the model is pushed toward
enforcement.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble takes the same topology model one rung further and gates it against the real tree. A reverse-mapping
check reads ownership back from the directory structure and set-diffs it against the declared zones in both
directions, so a moved or unowned directory fails the build instead of drifting in silence. Same fact
everyone externalizes — component ownership — but held equal to the code by a check rather than trusted to
stay current.

<!-- takeaway -->
Externalizing the component topology is common; gating it against the real tree is the variation that turns
a faithful drawing into a fact that cannot silently drift.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

> **Handoff to Alignment** — where am I and what may this file touch → the component-and-zone model → every in-scope directory is owned by exactly one component, and only a sanctioned seam crosses a boundary → modularity, maintainability, security → in Alignment the property acquires authority: a sensor scans the tree and re-induces ownership from it; a validator set-diffs that against the declared zones in both directions; a boundary lint is the constraint that refuses an out-of-seam reach — including raw access to a powerful format library outside its one sanctioned model; a gate fails the build on a moved or unowned directory.

## Some facts describe; others declare

<!-- point: descriptive-facts-are-derivable-intent-bearing-facts-are-authored | Descriptive facts are derivable; intent-bearing facts are authored. | terms: descriptive-vs-prescriptive, model-from-code, boundary-kind -->
<!-- index-def: descriptive-vs-prescriptive -->
The component and zone model is the cleanest place to see a distinction that runs through every
model in this book. **Some model facts describe what exists; others declare what is allowed.**

- **Descriptive facts** mirror the system. Whether a declared focus directory exists, and whether
  every in-scope directory is owned once, can be read straight off the tree. A drift gate derives
  them and checks them; if the model disagrees with the code, the model is wrong.
- **Intent-bearing facts** — the strong form is a **prescriptive** or **blueprint** fact — declare
  what *ought* to be. *Which* component owns a directory, what boundary kind it is, which seams it
  may cross: the code cannot derive any of these. Someone authored them. The model records the
  intent and a lint defends it.

The distinction is not academic bookkeeping, and it becomes load-bearing later. A drift gate proves
the model and the code *agree* — but agreement is not correctness. A descriptive model can faithfully
mirror a wrong system. Only an independently authored *ought* gives the implementation something to
be wrong against. That authored intent is the seam a structural model hands forward to Alignment:
the facts that describe can be checked against reality, and the facts that declare are the ones a
guardrail is there to enforce.

## The facts every consumer joins to

<!-- section-terms: domain-registries, rule-metadata-registry, executable-source-of-truth -->

> #### Domain registries · Structural
>
> - **Engineering question** — Which slow-changing facts are read everywhere, and how do I keep every reader agreeing?
> - **Model** — each such fact frozen in one table that every consumer joins to by key instead of copying.
> - **Property** — every consumer reads the fact through the registry key; no reader keeps a private copy.
> - **Quality attribute** — fact consistency, maintainability.

<!-- point: frozen-registries-are-the-single-source-every-consumer-joins | Frozen registries are the single source every consumer joins by key. | terms: domain-registries, join-key, fact-consistency -->
Some facts change slowly and are consulted everywhere — the file types a system handles, the
governance rules it enforces. A fact read in many places and revised rarely is exactly the fact
that drifts when each reader keeps its own copy. The **domain registries** freeze each such fact in
one table, and every consumer joins to it by key instead of copying it. Disagreement becomes a build
failure rather than a latent inconsistency.

Concretely, the file-type registry lists the formats the system remediates. Three consumers read it
by key — the customer-facing comparison doc, the internal coverage table, and the code that switches
on file type — so a format added in one place appears in all three or the build fails. Copying
`"pptx"` into a handler instead of joining to the registry is the move the checker rejects. The
quality property is **fact consistency**, and the join key is the registry key itself.

<!-- point: the-rule-metadata-registry-is-the-self-referential-slice | The rule-metadata registry is the self-referential slice; the book is generated from it. | terms: rule-metadata-registry, round-trip -->
<!-- index-def: rule-metadata-registry -->
One slice turns the lens on the governance itself. The **rule-metadata registry** keeps each rule's
metadata beside the rule it describes, and the published rule index is generated by reading every
such block — one row per rule. A completeness lint fails a rule whose block omits a required field;
a freshness lint regenerates the index and fails the build if the committed rows differ. Because the
description lives next to the rule, it cannot rot into a separate document nobody updates. This
book's own catalogue is built this way: the row you would find in its index is generated from the
block on the entry's page, and the same freshness gate holds them equal.

> The registry schemas, the join key each consumer reads by, and the completeness and freshness
> invariants live in the appendix. [appendix: appendix-g-domain-registries-reference]

> **Handoff to Alignment** — which slow-changing fact is read everywhere → the domain registries → every consumer joins by key and none keeps a copy → fact consistency, maintainability → in Alignment the property acquires authority: the registry key is the constraint every reader joins through; a completeness sensor reads each metadata block; a freshness validator regenerates the derived index and diffs the committed rows; a gate fails the build on a copied-not-joined fact or a stale index.

## What third-party material the build depends on

<!-- section-terms: bill-of-materials, dependency-manifest -->

> #### Bill of materials · Structural
>
> - **Engineering question** — What third-party material does the build rest on, and will a fresh checkout resolve it?
> - **Model** — the dependency graph of the build, every third-party package direct and transitive: the SBOM lens on the code.
> - **Property** — every package the code or a quality gate imports appears in the matching manifest.
> - **Quality attribute** — reproducibility, supply-chain integrity, maintainability.

<!-- point: a-bill-of-materials-holds-the-dependency-manifest-complete | A bill of materials holds the dependency manifest complete on every build. | terms: bill-of-materials, sbom, dependency-manifest -->
<!-- index-def: bill-of-materials -->
The last structural question looks outward: what does the build pull in that nobody in this
repository wrote? A **bill of materials** captures it — the dependency graph of the build, every
third-party package direct and transitive, the SBOM lens on the code. A dependency is a packaging
fact, not a runtime one: it decides what a fresh checkout must resolve before anything runs.

What the model lets you establish is that a fresh checkout never breaks on a resolve the author
never saw fail. The illustrative invariant: **every package the code or a quality gate imports
appears in the matching manifest.** A build-time completeness gate holds it, and its companion
discipline closes the obvious gap — an out-of-band install an agent runs mid-task must land in the
same change that relies on it, or it stays invisible to the manifest, and invisible is where a
supply-chain surprise lives. The bill of materials is not a document someone maintains; it is the
manifest the gate holds complete on every build. [appendix: appendix-g-bill-of-materials-reference]

> **Handoff to Alignment** — what third-party material the build rests on → the bill of materials → every imported package appears in the matching manifest → reproducibility, supply-chain integrity → in Alignment the property acquires authority: the same-change discipline for an out-of-band install is the constraint; a build-time sensor and validator diff the imported packages against the manifest; a gate fails a fresh checkout that would break on an unresolved dependency.

do when many of them run at once, and where each one runs. That is the next question.
