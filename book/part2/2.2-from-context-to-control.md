# From Context to Engineering Model

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, structured-relationships -->

<!-- point: an-edge-gains-authority-one-property-at-a-time | An edge gains authority one property at a time: type, invariant, derivation, gate. | terms: structured-relationships, thesis-modeling, drift-gate -->
Suppose the graph records that a worker consumes a queue. As context, that edge helps an agent find the
relevant code. Add to it and it does more. Give the edge a type and properties, and a tool can reason
over it. Attach an invariant — only workers of class X may consume queues of class Y — and the edge now
carries engineering intent, not just a fact. Derive the graph from the implementation, or generate the
implementation from the graph, and its claims become checkable. Put a gate across divergence, and the
edge becomes part of the governed environment. The same edge that began as context has acquired
authority, one property at a time.

<!-- point: one-artifact-serves-three-roles | A structured representation serves three increasingly strong roles: context, engineering model, governed environment. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
A structured representation can serve three increasingly strong roles. As **context**, it helps an agent
recover the facts a task touches. As an **engineering model**, it supports reasoning about the system's
structure, behavior, intent, or evidence. When mechanisms enforce obligations expressed over it, it
becomes part of the **governed environment**. Each role keeps the earlier ones — a governed model is
still legible context — while adding a stronger claim on top. Modeling and Alignment are not two concerns
bolted together; they are the low and high ends of one representation getting richer. This chapter reaches
the middle role. The next Part builds the third.

<!-- point: what-makes-a-representation-a-model | A representation becomes a model when it is reduced, machine-readable, and mechanically kept related to the system. | terms: thesis-modeling, structured-relationships -->
The middle role deserves a definition, because "model" is used loosely. A **model** is a deliberately
reduced, machine-readable representation of a system. It earns the name here only when it supports
reasoning, derivation, or checking and is mechanically kept related to the system it represents — a
picture no one can act on, or one that has quietly drifted from the code, is not yet a model in this
sense. The word doing the work is **structured**: an explicit, declared shape a machine can read and
validate — a schema, not prose. The structure is what admits the machine. Prose can be read; only a
structured representation can be traversed by a tool, checked against the code, and gated for drift.

## The capability ladder

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: the-ladder-orders-capabilities-not-organizations | The capability ladder orders representational capabilities, not organizations. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, structured-relationships -->
<!-- index-def: capability-ladder -->
<!-- index-def: structured-relationships -->
Naming the steps from context to control gives the rest of this Part its organizing model. The lower
steps help the reasoner: select the right context, externalize durable knowledge, structure it into typed
relations, add the semantics of a system model. The upper steps govern the artifact: state invariants
over the model, derive analysis from it, trace its elements to the code, and refuse the drift that would
let model and territory silently diverge. [ref:capability-ladder] draws all eight.

The one framing to hold onto is what the ladder is *not*. **It is a capability ladder, not a maturity
model. It orders representational capabilities, not organizations. Higher steps make additional
engineering questions answerable; they do not imply every system should reach every step.** A stronger
representation tells you what you *could* do, not that you are behind for not having built it. You
strengthen a representation when a recurring engineering question is worth the capital, and not before.

The evidence bears the caution out. Docker sits low on modeling and high on authority: it sandboxes
actions and runs tests against raw artifacts with no invariant-bearing system model at all. That is a
legitimate place to stop, and the visible proof that the steps order capabilities rather than rank
companies. The transition reads in one line — the lower steps help the reasoner; the turn in the middle
makes intent explicit; the upper steps give the environment authority over the artifact.

## The turn from modeling to alignment

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, determinization -->

<!-- point: knowledge-rep-makes-legible-executable-modeling-makes-analyzable | Knowledge representation makes the system legible; executable modeling makes it analyzable. | terms: thesis-modeling, structured-relationships, executable-source-of-truth -->
The two sides of the ladder are one motion seen twice. Knowledge representation makes the system legible;
executable modeling makes it analyzable. Better representations improve reasoning first. As they acquire
semantics, they also expose more of engineering judgment to deterministic enforcement: the service edge
that helped an agent navigate becomes a predicate a gate can check; the state a comment described becomes
a transition a model can declare legal or illegal.

<!-- point: modeling-makes-intent-explicit-alignment-makes-it-binding | Modeling makes intent explicit; Alignment makes it binding. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
That motion is the hinge between this Part and the next. **Modeling makes intent explicit; Alignment
makes it binding.** First write down what matters in a form both people and machines can read; then teach
the environment to insist on it.

<!-- point: representation-alignment-proposition | Representation–Alignment Proposition: more explicit structure makes more obligations eligible for deterministic enforcement. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, determinization -->
> **Representation–Alignment Proposition.** As engineering intent becomes more explicit and structured,
> more obligations become eligible for deterministic enforcement.

The more of the system you write down in a checkable form, the more of how it must behave the environment
can hold on its own, instead of leaving it to human vigilance. The closing chapters make this precise;
here it is the through-line every model below follows — each starts as context an agent reasons through
and ends as a surface a gate can hold the code to.

## Where to start

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, brownfield-migration -->

<!-- point: start-at-the-lowest-rung-that-solves-a-real-problem | Start at the lowest step that solves a real reasoning problem; strengthen the model when a recurring question justifies it. | terms: thesis-modeling, brownfield-migration, capability-ladder -->
The ladder answers a question every real team asks: where do I start? Not by building a complete
executable model on day one. Start at the lowest step that solves a reasoning problem you actually have,
then strengthen the representation when a recurring engineering question makes the next step worth its
cost. A catalog that ends the daily "who owns this?" rediscovery is a cheap step well spent. An invariant
is worth stating the third time the same class of bug ships. The work is paced by the judgments you want
to retire, not by an ambition to reach the top.

<!-- worked-examples: thesis-modeling -->
### Example — Siemens
Siemens shows the far side of the ceiling. An engineer states intent in plain words — "validate this part
under 200 lb in the Y-direction" — and an agent reads the CAD geometry, sets up the finite-element model,
calls a deterministic solver, and returns a validated result. The model is the work surface; the code is
one downstream artifact the model generates; a solver, not a reviewer's opinion, decides whether the
answer holds. This is what mature engineering looks like once the representation is executable rather than
merely legible.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble crosses the same boundary in software, one implementation of the move. It makes an ordinary
codebase queryable through structured models an agent reasons over and a gate checks, so a system built
from source gains the property Siemens's models already have: the representation is analyzable, not just
readable.

<!-- takeaway -->
Cross from a representation an agent can read to one a machine can analyze, so engineering intent is
settled by a solver or a gate instead of trusted. The model-first world lives past that line already;
software can reach it.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

<!-- point: a-representation-becomes-a-model-when-it-carries-intent | A representation becomes an engineering model when it carries semantics and intent, not merely facts. | terms: thesis-modeling, structured-relationships -->
A representation becomes an engineering model when it carries semantics and intent, not merely facts. The
edge that only recorded *worker consumes queue* now says which workers *may*, and a machine can act on the
difference. From here, each model is a slice of the system knowledge graph, structured until it answers an
engineering question the raw code cannot. The first question every one of them must answer: what entities
and relations does it represent?
