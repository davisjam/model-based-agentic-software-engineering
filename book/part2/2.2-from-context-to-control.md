# From Context to Engineering Model

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, structured-relationships -->

<!-- point: an-edge-gains-authority-one-property-at-a-time | An edge gains authority one property at a time: type, invariant, derivation, gate. | terms: structured-relationships, thesis-modeling, drift-gate -->
Suppose the graph records that a worker consumes a queue. As context, that edge helps an agent find
the relevant code. Add a little and it does more. Give the edge a type and properties, and a tool
can reason over it. Attach an invariant, say that only workers of class X may consume queues of
class Y, and the representation now carries engineering intent, not just a fact. Derive the graph from the
implementation, or generate the implementation from the graph, and its claims become checkable.
Put a gate across divergence, and the model becomes part of the governed environment. The same edge
that began as context has acquired authority, one property at a time.

<!-- point: one-artifact-serves-three-roles | The same artifact serves three roles: knowledge graph, engineering model, governed model. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
The representation has crossed a boundary, and one artifact now serves three roles at once:

- **Knowledge graph:** what must the intelligence know to reason about this system?
- **Engineering model:** what does the engineer assert must be so?
- **Governed model:** what will the environment refuse to let become false?

The same graph answers all three questions as it grows stronger. That is why Modeling and Alignment
are not independent concerns bolted together. They are the low and high ends of one representation
getting richer.

> ### Inset — The three constraints {#inset-three-constraints}
>
> **Context helps the reasoner. Models constrain the reasoning problem. Governance constrains the resulting action.**
>
> MAGE moves engineering knowledge steadily outward: first from the agent's transient reasoning into a durable representation, then from the representation into explicit properties, and finally from those properties into mechanisms the environment enforces. Each move keeps the earlier ones: a governed model is still context, and still legible, while adding a stronger claim on top.

## The capability ladder

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: the-ladder-orders-capabilities-not-organizations | The ladder orders representational capabilities, not organizations. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, structured-relationships -->
<!-- index-def: capability-ladder -->
<!-- index-def: structured-relationships -->
The climb from context to control has rungs, and naming them gives the rest of this Part its shape.
The lower rungs help the reasoner: select the right context, externalize durable knowledge,
structure it into typed relations, add the semantics of a system model. The upper rungs govern the
artifact: state invariants over the model, derive analysis from it, trace its elements to the code,
and refuse the drift that would let model and territory silently diverge. [ref:capability-ladder]
draws all eight.

The one framing to hold onto is what the ladder is *not*. **It is a capability ladder, not a
maturity model. The ladder orders representational capabilities, not organizations. Higher rungs
make additional engineering questions possible; they do not imply that every system should climb
every rung.**

*In other words:* the ladder tells you what a stronger representation would let you do, not that
you are behind for not having built it. You climb a rung when a recurring engineering question is
worth the capital, and not before.

The evidence bears the caution out. Docker sits low on modeling and strong on authority: it
sandboxes actions and runs tests against raw artifacts without an invariant-bearing system model at
all. That is a legitimate stopping point, and it is the visible proof that the rungs order
capabilities rather than rank companies. The transition across the ladder reads in one line: *rungs
one through four mostly help the reasoner; the turn at rung five makes intent explicit; rungs five
through eight give the environment authority over the artifact.*

## The turn from modeling to alignment

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, determinization -->

<!-- point: knowledge-rep-makes-legible-executable-modeling-makes-analyzable | Knowledge representation makes the system legible; executable modeling makes it analyzable. | terms: thesis-modeling, structured-relationships, executable-source-of-truth -->
The two axes of the ladder are one motion seen from two sides. Knowledge representation makes the
system legible; executable modeling makes it analyzable. Better representations improve reasoning
first. As they acquire semantics, they also expose more of engineering judgment to deterministic
enforcement. The service edge that helped an agent navigate becomes a predicate a gate can check;
the state a comment described becomes a transition a model can declare legal or illegal.

<!-- point: modeling-makes-intent-explicit-alignment-makes-it-binding | Modeling makes intent explicit; Alignment makes it binding. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
That motion is the hinge between this Part and the next. **Modeling makes intent explicit;
Alignment makes it binding.**

*In other words:* first write down what matters in a form both people and machines can understand,
then teach the engineering environment to insist on it.

<!-- point: representation-alignment-proposition | Representation–Alignment Proposition: more explicit structure expands what can be enforced deterministically. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, determinization -->
There is a formal statement of why the turn happens, and the closing chapters of this book prove it
as a result. Named here as a signpost:

> **Representation–Alignment Proposition.** As engineering intent becomes more explicit and
> structured, the set of obligations that can be enforced deterministically expands.

*In other words:* the more of the system you write down in a checkable form, the more of "how it
must behave" the environment can enforce on its own, instead of leaving it to human vigilance.

That is the through-line the rest of this Part builds toward. Each model below starts as context an
agent reasons through and ends as a surface a gate can hold the code to.

## Where to start

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, brownfield-migration -->

<!-- point: start-at-the-lowest-rung-that-solves-a-real-problem | Start at the lowest rung that solves a real reasoning problem; climb when a question justifies it. | terms: thesis-modeling, brownfield-migration, capability-ladder -->
The ladder answers a question every real team asks: where do I start? Not by building a complete
executable model on day one. Start at the lowest rung that solves a reasoning problem you actually
have, then strengthen the representation when a recurring engineering question makes the next rung
worth its cost. A catalog that ends the daily "who owns this?" rediscovery is rung two well spent.
An invariant is worth stating the third time the same class of bug ships. The climb is paced by the
judgments you want to retire, not by an ambition to reach the top.

<!-- worked-examples: thesis-modeling -->
### Example — Siemens
Siemens shows the far side of the ceiling. An engineer states intent in plain words — "validate this
part under 200 lb in the Y-direction" — and an agent reads the CAD geometry, sets up the
finite-element model, calls a deterministic solver, and returns a validated result. The model is the
work surface and the code is one downstream artifact the model generates; a solver, not a reviewer's
opinion, decides whether the answer holds. This is what mature engineering already looks like once the
representation is executable rather than merely legible.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble crosses the same boundary in software, one implementation of the move. It makes an ordinary
codebase queryable through structured models an agent reasons over and a gate checks, so a system
built from source gains the property Siemens's models already have: the representation is analyzable,
not just readable. That is the ambition of this whole Part — to give software the executable model a
model-based discipline takes for granted.

<!-- takeaway -->
The shared move is executable *modeling*: cross from a representation an agent can read to one a
machine can analyze, so engineering intent is settled by a solver or a gate instead of trusted. The
model-first world lives past that line already; this Part is about helping software reach it.
