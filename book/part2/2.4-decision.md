# Decision Models: What Should Happen Next?

The preceding models already expressed obligations: a user interface must reach its database through the service layer, and a job must pass through `Running` before `Succeeded`. A decision model makes explicit how to choose among alternatives: whether a result is acceptable, whether the evidence is sufficient, what to do after failure, or which strategy to try next. Its answer may be to accept, retry, repair, escalate, or stop.

A behavioral model describes possible states and transitions. A decision model states how to choose among them using the available evidence, the cost of further work, and the attempts remaining. For example, a repair process may allow another attempt, escalation, or termination. A decision table can state when the observed failure justifies another attempt, when more evidence is needed, and when the remaining budget requires stopping. The model supplies criteria for the choice; a separate mechanism carries it out.

## The Canonical Move: Choosing Among Alternatives

<!-- point: permission-has-many-canonical-forms-unified-by-mood-not-shape | Decision tables, rule systems, and constraint systems make choices explicit; policy is one kind of decision model, expressed through access-control matrices, policy graphs, and feature models as well as those general forms. -->

Decision tables map conditions to choices; rule systems state how evidence affects a decision; constraint systems distinguish admissible alternatives. A policy is one kind of decision model: it specifies which alternatives an engineer permits, requires, or forbids. Access-control matrices record which subject may act on which object, [cite: lampson1974] policy graphs declare permitted relationships, and feature models constrain valid combinations of options.

<!-- point: same-arrow-different-mood-is-the-sharpest-case-of-topology-not-fixing-meaning | An observed-call edge and a permitted-call edge can look identical while answering different questions; in the illustrated models, a missing edge means no recorded call or no permitted call respectively. -->

The meaning of an edge depends on the model’s purpose. In an observed call graph, `A → B` records that A calls B; in a policy graph, the same arrow means that A *may* call B. An absent observed edge means only that no such call was recorded within the observation’s coverage. An absent policy edge can mean that the call is prohibited, provided the policy declares a complete set of permitted relationships within its scope. These are different claims, even though both models use the same graphical notation. Comparing them lets engineers ask whether the implementation respects the declared permissions.

<!-- point: a-declared-policy-turns-is-this-allowed-into-an-evaluation | Once permission is declared, the claims a policy designer wants become expressible — permitted/forbidden, mutual exclusion, prerequisites, consistency, completeness, least privilege — and "is this allowed?" becomes an evaluation rather than an argument. -->

Once permission is declared, the claims a policy designer wants become expressible: which alternatives are permitted or forbidden, which are mutually exclusive, which imply prerequisites, whether the policy is internally consistent, whether it is complete over the cases that can arise, and whether it achieves least privilege. The analyses follow — evaluating a rule against a case, checking or solving constraints, detecting conflicts between rules, testing completeness and consistency. A declared policy turns "is this allowed?" into an evaluation rather than an argument.

## Compare the Decision with the Facts

<!-- print-columns-start: observed-vs-declared -->

In a hypothetical system, a public web interface may call a report service, which may in turn call a renderer. The declared policy permits those relationships but prohibits the web interface from calling the renderer directly. [ref:observed-vs-declared] compares that policy with an observed call graph. The observation contains a direct web-to-renderer call that the policy does not permit. Comparing the graphs therefore identifies a violation of the declared architectural boundary.

<!-- label: observed-vs-declared -->
<!-- figure: assets/observed-vs-declared.svg | *Observation compared with declared permission.* In this hypothetical example, the observed web-to-renderer call has no corresponding permission in the declared policy. <!-- print-columns-end: observed-vs-declared -->

The comparison asks whether observations conform to the declared policy, not whether the policy itself is correct. A policy could permit an undesirable relationship, and an implementation could faithfully follow it. Conversely, the absence of an observed call does not establish that the call can never occur; the observation may have incomplete coverage. Engineers must therefore evaluate both whether the policy expresses the intended architecture and whether the available evidence establishes conformance to it.

## Preserve the Cases a Decision Must Distinguish

The input representation can make a decision wrong before any rule is evaluated. Return to the prepaid and unlimited subscriptions introduced earlier. For a simple policy with one applicable entitlement, [ref:simple-admission-policy] states when to admit a request and whether to charge finite credit.

<!-- label: simple-admission-policy -->
<!-- table: *Admission under one entitlement.* Unlimited entitlement admits covered work without drawing down finite credit; a finite balance must cover the requested work. [short: Admission under one entitlement] -->
| Entitlement | Requested work | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Unlimited | Any amount covered by the subscription | Admit without drawing down finite credit |
| Finite balance | Enough credit for the request | Admit and charge the required credit |
| Finite balance | Insufficient credit for the request | Reject for insufficient credit |

The table needs the entitlement kind as well as the balance. If a previous step passes only a number, zero cannot distinguish an exhausted finite allowance from unlimited use. The decision code cannot recover a distinction that its input has discarded.

### When the Decision Depends on Context

<!-- point: image-purpose-and-uncertainty-govern-the-decorative-decision | An image's informational role depends on context; DocAble uses a VLM classification with an explicit conservative policy that retains content when the classification is uncertain. -->

Some decisions cannot be made from the object alone. Accessibility standards distinguish informative images, which need a text alternative, from decorative images, which assistive technology can ignore [cite: w3c2018wcag21]. A blue square might decorate a restaurant menu but be the subject of a lecture on modern art.[^blue-monochrome-context] What matters is the image’s contribution to the document, not merely what it depicts.

DocAble’s slide classifier gives a vision-language model (VLM) a rendered slide so it can judge each image or shape in context. The VLM returns a classification: clearly decorative, uncertain, or informational. That judgment supplies evidence; the decision policy determines what to do with it. For content-bearing images, only a clearly decorative verdict permits suppressing the description. An uncertain or missing verdict preserves the image’s informational role. The asymmetry is deliberate: an unnecessary description is audible, while wrongly hiding information can leave a screen-reader user unaware that anything was omitted.

An evaluation found that earlier classifiers had marked content-bearing images as decorative, particularly in visualization-heavy documents. We tightened the policy by representing uncertainty explicitly and making its conservative interpretation common to the classification paths. The decision model now states how uncertain evidence affects remediation without prescribing which VLM must supply that evidence. A different VLM can make the semantic judgment; it does not redefine the policy for acting on it.

DocAble supplies two worked applications of permission: a declared service-call policy and an upload-admission failure that required a richer decision model.

> **Which entitlement authorizes an upload?**
>
> <!-- point: the-entitlement-model-was-born-from-a-production-failure | The service-flow policy was declared in advance; DocAble's upload-admission decision acquired its model in the opposite order, after a production failure exposed a representation that could no longer express the decision. -->
>
> The service-flow policy states an architectural decision. A different decision, whether a user may upload a document, exposed the cost of losing a domain distinction as the product changed.
>
> <!-- point: upload-entitlement-flows-through-groups | A user receives upload entitlement through groups — finite-credit or unlimited — so the admission decision depends on two facts: how many credits remain and whether any entitlement is unlimited. -->
>
> DocAble admits an upload only when the user is entitled to it. A user receives that entitlement through groups. A group may carry a finite credit balance, drawn down as pages are processed, or it may provide unlimited use. The admission decision therefore depends on two different facts: how many finite credits are available, and whether any applicable entitlement is unlimited ([ref:entitlement-domain]).
>
> <!-- label: entitlement-domain -->
> <!-- figure: assets/entitlement-domain.svg | *The upload-entitlement domain.* A finite balance alone cannot determine admission: the model must also preserve whether any applicable group grants unlimited use. -->
>
> <!-- point: the-scalar-was-adequate-until-the-domain-changed | The original credit comparison was understandable directly in code; unlimited entitlement made the policy harder to follow across implementation sites and motivated a separate decision model. -->
>
> The original client upload rule was easy to understand from the code: if the user’s credits covered the upload cost, allow the upload; otherwise, reject it. Box (1) in [ref:entitlement-model-evolution] shows this balance-based check. A separate decision model would have added little to understanding that simple rule.
>
> Unlimited groups changed what the upload decision had to account for. As box (2) shows, a group could authorize an upload without contributing finite credits to the balance. The server’s balance calculation, the client’s check before sending an upload, and the server admission gate represented entitlement in different ways. Understanding and updating the intended policy now required checking how each part of the implementation handled both finite and unlimited entitlement.
>
> <!-- point: the-old-reduction-observed-the-distinction-and-discarded-it | The finite-credit reduction excluded unlimited groups, while the client upload check ignored a separate unlimited-status signal and rejected the upload before the correct server gate was reached. -->
>
> Box (3) traces the production failure. In August 2026, a user whose personal group provided unlimited use was blocked from uploading a thirty-eight-page presentation: *the upload needs 38 credits, and you have 0*. The legacy server function read each group’s unlimited flag twice: once to exclude unlimited groups from the credit sum, and once to compute a display class. For this user it returned zero credits and the class *individual*. The login response omitted the detailed group records but carried a separate unlimited-status flag. The client upload check ignored that flag, compared the zero balance with the required 38 credits, and rejected the upload before sending the file. The server’s admission logic, which handled this entitlement correctly, was never reached. The failure came from using the finite-credit total as an admission decision even though other system representations retained the unlimited entitlement.
>
> <!-- point: the-production-failure-produced-the-model | After fixing the client to read the existing unlimited signal, the team made the more complex entitlement policy explicit in a decision model and checked it with property tests, shared policy cases, and a shape check. -->
>
> The immediate fix made the client consult the existing unlimited-status flag it had been ignoring. We then introduced the entitlement decision model shown in box (4) to state the policy explicitly: given the user’s group entitlements and the requested work, should this upload be admitted, and under which entitlement? Property tests checked the model, and a cross-runtime parity corpus checked shared policy cases in the server and client. A further check validated the model’s shape. The display code derived its values from the model, and the server admission code delegated the policy decision to it.

>
> Box (4) separates the admission decision from the display projection. For the same unlimited group, with no per-user cap, the model admits the 38-page upload and records the authorizing group and an *admit_unlimited* plan step. The group’s finite credit balance is still zero. The display projection, however, returns a special numeric value marking unlimited use; it returns a finite credit sum only when all of the user’s groups are finite. That display value is explicitly excluded from the admission decision. The two outputs answer different questions, and each preserves the information its consumer needs.

>
> A note in the first person: I changed the domain in one place — groups could now be unlimited — and did not carry the change into every representation of the same decision. An explicit model would not have made the change automatically correct; it would have made the inconsistency visible as an engineering obligation. When the domain contains a consequential distinction, every representation used to reason about that decision must be capable of expressing it. {{chapter:alignment}} gives the recurring pattern here — a failure converted into explicit structure that later work inherits — a name.
>
> The two models give a decision an explicit domain: permitted service pairs in the first case, distinct entitlement alternatives in the second. That makes the decision inspectable and checkable. It also exposes what must change when the intended policy or the product's domain changes.

Decision models make the criteria for a choice explicit, whether the choice concerns permission or what to do next. The final section steps back from one representation to ask how a family of models can answer questions together.
