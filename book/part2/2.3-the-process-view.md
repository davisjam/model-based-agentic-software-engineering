# The Process View: what runs at once

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | The Process View — the chapter front-loads five concept-primer insets (automaton, state-vs-sequence assertions, safety-vs-liveness, bounded model checking) that the reader needs before any Process-view model; the insets are self-contained boxed primers, not a wall of prose to break -->

<!-- point: the-process-view-asks-what-runs-at-once | The Process view asks what runs at once. | terms: process-view, concurrency -->
<!-- index-def: process-view -->
Reasoning about a concurrent system by simulating its interleavings in your head is the
burden the Process view removes: it names the states, the locks, and the invariants that must
hold across *every* interleaving, so a race is a checked property rather than a thought
experiment. The Logical view named the parts. The Process view asks the question the logical view cannot:
what happens when many parts run at the same time? Concurrency turns a correct-in-isolation
design into a source of races, deadlocks, and torn writes, and the more parts run at once, the
more interleavings the design must survive. The Process view is the lens for that — the states
a system moves through, the locks it takes, and the invariants that must hold across every one
of those interleavings. Those invariants are not comments: a checker holds the
running system to them, so a race cannot reach production unseen — the Alignment
Thesis, read at the grain of concurrency.

<!-- point: here-invariants-become-predicates-over-an-order-of-events | Here invariants become predicates over an order of events. | terms: invariant, temporal-logic, state-machine -->
This is the chapter where invariants stop being predicates over one state and become
predicates over an *order of events*. That step-up pulls in a cluster of ideas: what an automaton is, safety versus liveness, bounded
model checking, and the temporal-logic step from a state assertion to a sequence assertion. This
chapter defines each as it is first needed, and [The Book's
Language](0.3-the-books-language.html) collects them for quick reference. Two shapes of concept live
here — the **state machine** and the **automaton with a formal invariant**. **Three** real models
embody the view: the **synchronization model**, the **mediator registry**, and the
**single-writer registry** — all resting on the **formal-invariant-verification backbone**. That
backbone is not a model of the view but the shared *checker-router* the three hang their invariant
tables on: it takes an invariant's temporal shape and routes the exhaustive checker that verifies
it. It represents no slice of the system, so it is trunk machinery, not a counted model.

<!-- point: an-automaton-is-states-and-legal-transitions | An automaton is states and their legal transitions. | terms: state-machine -->
<!-- index-def: state-machine -->
> ### Inset I4 — What is an automaton / state machine? {#inset-automaton}
>
> An **automaton**, or **state machine**, is a finite set of states and the labeled
> transitions between them. It models computation as "which state am I in, and which moves are
> legal from here." A leased job is *queued*, *leased*, *running*, or *reclaimed*; a worker taking
> the lease moves *queued → leased*, a lease expiring moves *leased → queued*, completion moves
> *running → reclaimed*. Drawing it makes the illegal states and the missing transitions visible:
> if a path leads back to *running* from *reclaimed*, the diagram shows the double-execution bug
> before the code does. A state machine is the logical spine the Process view's invariants attach
> to — the invariant is a predicate over the states, and the checker walks the transitions.
>
> *You can now read a Process-view model as states + legal transitions, and see an invariant as a predicate the checker evaluates while walking them.*

<!-- point: claims-about-order-over-time-need-temporal-logic | Claims about order over time need temporal logic. | terms: temporal-logic, safety-property, liveness-property -->
<!-- index-def: temporal-logic -->
> ### Inset I2 — State properties versus sequence properties {#inset-ltl}
>
> Some properties are about a **single state**: "a job's status and its lease-owner never disagree
> — a *leased* job has an owner, a *queued* job has none." You check one by visiting every reachable
> state and evaluating the predicate there. Other properties are about **order over time**: "a
> preempted job eventually re-runs." No single state settles that — it is a claim about the
> *sequence* of states. Claims about order need **temporal logic** (LTL), which adds operators over
> sequences: *always* (*□*), *eventually* (*◇*), and *leads-to* (*↝*). The two kinds route to
> different checkers — a state search for the first, a temporal model checker for the second.
>
> The four operators the rest of this chapter leans on all describe the shape of a *run* — a path
> the system walks, one state after another. Read them first as **shapes of behavior**; their formal
> semantics matter when you choose the checker:
>
> - **Always (□P)** — P holds at every step of every run: nothing bad ever slips through.
> - **Eventually (◇P)** — some step of the run satisfies P: the good thing arrives, sooner or later.
> - **Leads-to (P ↝ Q)** — every step where P holds is followed by a later step where Q holds: once P, then Q eventually.
> - **Until (P U Q)** — P holds at every step until a step where Q becomes true, and Q does become true.
>
> *You can now tell a state assertion from a sequence assertion, and know that the difference decides which checker verifies it.*

<!-- point: the-temporal-shape-derives-the-checker | An invariant's temporal shape derives its checker. | terms: safety-property, liveness-property, temporal-logic -->
<!-- index-def: safety-property -->
<!-- index-def: liveness-property -->
> ### Inset I6 — Safety versus liveness {#inset-safety-liveness}
>
> Two shapes cover most invariants. **Safety** says *a bad thing never happens* — commonly written
> *□P*. "A *leased* job always has exactly one owner" is safety. **Liveness** says *a good thing
> eventually happens* — a common shape is *P ↝ Q*. "A preempted job eventually re-runs" is liveness.
> The shape is not decoration: it **derives the checker**. For the `□P` state predicates this book
> checks, a safety property is settled by a state-space search for any reachable bad state; a
> liveness property needs a temporal model checker that reasons about cyclic behaviors. (The
> trace-property theory is Baier & Katoen [cite: baier-katoen2008]; here we use only the routing it
> justifies.) Declare the shape wrong — route a liveness property to a safety runtime — and the
> checker structurally cannot see the violation, and reports green. So the shape is checked too: a
> lint asserts the declared temporal form matches the routed checker.
>
> *You can now name an invariant safety or liveness from its shape, and know that a mis-declared shape is a silent gap the match-lint closes.*

<!-- point: model-checking-explores-exhaustively-where-a-test-samples | Model checking explores exhaustively where a test samples. | terms: bounded-model-checking -->
<!-- index-def: bounded-model-checking -->
> ### Inset I3 — What does bounded model checking establish? {#inset-bmc}
>
> A test *samples* the input space — it runs the cases you thought of and sails past the one
> adversarial schedule you did not. **Model checking** instead *exhaustively explores* the state
> space: it visits every reachable state, or every interleaving of a concurrent system, and either
> shows the invariant holds across all of them or hands you a concrete **counterexample trace** —
> the exact sequence of steps that reaches the bad state. **Bounded** model checking does this out
> to a fixed depth: it checks every behavior up to depth *k*. Within the bound there is no
> counterexample under the model and the encoding; a bug that needs *k+1* steps, or one the model
> abstracts away, is out of scope — the honest limit you trade for a decidable check. Why it matters
> for a fleet of concurrent agents: a distributed race has failure traces no hand-picked example
> hits, and only an exhaustive walk of the interleavings finds them. A green sampled test over that
> race means far less than an exhaustive check; a clean bounded model check means no bad state is
> reachable within the bound and the model.
>
> *You can now state what a clean bounded check does and does not establish: no reachable violation within k steps under this model — not a proof for all depths, and not for behavior the model omits.*

The general type behind these ideas — an **automaton with a formal invariant** — gets no
separate model page; it is introduced here as trunk machinery. So is the backbone that operates on
it: **formal invariant verification** is not one of the view's models but the method the three
models share — it takes an invariant's temporal shape and routes the exhaustive checker that proves
it.

## Formal invariant verification: the verification backbone {#formal-invariant-verification}

<!-- section-terms: formal-invariant-verification, temporal-logic -->

<!-- point: an-invariants-temporal-form-routes-the-exhaustive-checker | An invariant's temporal form routes its exhaustive checker. | terms: formal-invariant-verification, safety-property, liveness-property, bounded-model-checking -->
<!-- index-def: formal-invariant-verification -->
Formal invariant verification is the backbone the three models hang their invariant tables on — a
governance method over the models, not a model of the running system. It assesses **invariant
soundness under interleaving** — whether a property holds on every reachable state, not just the
schedules a test happened to try — and answers it by an exhaustive check that either **establishes
that no modeled reachable state violates the predicate** or returns a concrete counterexample
trace. The
distinctive move: the invariant's *temporal form is a routing input, not a label*. A
safety form (*□P*) routes to a state-space search; a liveness form (*P ↝ Q*) routes to a
temporal model checker. Get the form wrong — declare a liveness property as safety — and the
safety runtime structurally cannot see the violation and reports green. So a lint checks the
form against the checker that ran: the shape is consumed, and a mis-routed invariant is a build
failure rather than a false all-clear. Its full construct-and-invariant treatment is in the
appendix; the temporal operators it routes on are defined above.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [formal invariant verification](appendix-b-formal-invariant-verification.html).

That backbone proves invariants once you can state them. The next three models are about the
invariants concurrency hands you: the ones nobody writes down until an inverted lock ordering
deadlocks the build. Start with the locks themselves.

## The synchronization model {#synchronization-model}

<!-- section-terms: synchronization-model, concurrency -->

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | The synchronization model — a model page rendered from the fixed five-field (a)-(e) template plus its ordering-lint code sample; the fields are one indivisible reference unit a reader scans by reflex across every model -->

<!-- point: the-sync-model-declares-every-lock-and-its-ordering | The sync model declares every lock and its ordering. | terms: synchronization-model, lock-ordering, concurrency -->
<!-- index-def: synchronization-model -->
*A typed registry that models the system's synchronization behavior — every OS-level lock,
which shared resource it guards, and the required acquisition ordering — so concurrency
contracts are declared and checkable, not tribal.*

<!-- point: it-assesses-lock-coverage-and-deadlock-freedom | It assesses lock coverage and lock-order consistency. | terms: lock-coverage, lock-ordering, concurrency -->
**(a) Quality property it helps assess.** Two, both locally visible but globally difficult to
establish.

- **Lock coverage**: *is every OS lock in the codebase declared, and is what it guards known?*
  An undeclared lock is one nobody can reason about; the coverage lint makes it a build failure.
- **Lock-order consistency**: *does any acquisition invert a declared ordering?* The declared
  ordering graph lets a lint answer "which locks, in what order" before the code runs, so an
  inverted acquisition fails at author time instead of hanging in production. This prevents the
  deadlocks that arise from **declared-order inversions** — it does not, by itself, prove global
  deadlock-freedom, which would need cycle detection over the whole ordering graph.

**(b) Constructs and relations.** Three record kinds compose the registry.

- **`SyncLock`** — one OS primitive: its lock-file path, its cap (1 for a mutex, M for a
  semaphore), the resource it guards, its bypass-env, its audit-log.
- **`LockAcquirer`** — one declared acquisition site: where in the code a lock is taken, or a
  declared "takes none" with a rationale.
- **`LockOrdering`**: a before/after constraint between two locks, with a rationale. The set of
  these is the ordering graph the deadlock lint walks.

One `SyncLock` is acquired at many `LockAcquirer` sites and constrained by many `LockOrdering`
edges.

crow's-foot cardinality. Reused from the model's appendix Structure slot:

**(d) Invariants, and how they are checked.** A coverage lint and an ordering lint, the second
doing genuine graph reasoning, collected in [ref:process-invariants]:

<!-- label: process-invariants -->
<!-- table: Invariants of the Process view — each with its temporal shape and the check that holds it. [short: Invariants of the Process view] -->
| Invariant | Temporal shape | How it is checked |
|---|---|---|
| Every real lock call site is a declared acquirer | *□P* (safety) | Coverage lint scans the lock call sites; each must be declared or carry a "not a sync lock" rationale. |
| No acquisition inverts a declared ordering | *□P* (safety) | Ordering lint walks each code path's acquisition sequence against the ordering graph; an out-of-order acquire is a finding. |
| Every exempt site carries a rationale | *□P* (safety) | The closed set of exempt rationales — an unexplained exemption fails the coverage lint. |

The ordering lint walks a lock-acquisition sequence against the declared graph and fails on an
inversion, so a deadlock-inducing order is caught at author time. The shape, in schematic
pseudocode (the production checker reasons over transitive orderings and per-path acquisition, and
lives in the appendix):

```python
# Schematic — illustrates the inversion check on one acquisition path.
# The production lint reasons over the transitive ordering graph; see appendix.
import sys

# Declared ordering: a lock in `before` must be acquired before its `after` lock.
ORDERINGS = [("db-lock", "cache-lock")]      # db before cache, always

def ordering_lint(acquire_sequence: list[str]) -> list[str]:
    """A held-lock acquiring one that must precede it is an inversion (deadlock risk)."""
    findings, held = [], []
    for lock in acquire_sequence:
        for before, after in ORDERINGS:
            if lock == before and after in held:
                findings.append(f"acquired '{before}' while holding '{after}' — order inverted")
        held.append(lock)
    return findings

if __name__ == "__main__":
    findings = ordering_lint(walk_acquisitions())   # a code path's acquisition order
    for f in findings:
        print(f"LOCK-ORDER: {f}")
    sys.exit(1 if findings else 0)
```

<!-- point: the-sync-model-derives-from-code-via-lock-path | The sync model derives from code by lock path. | terms: model-from-code, join-key, lock-coverage -->
**(e) Traceability and derivation direction.** *Model-from-code.* The coverage lint scans the
real lock call sites and requires each to be a declared acquirer, so the code is the ground
truth and the model is the checked view. The join key is the lock `path` (which lock a site
takes) and the acquirer `site` (where the acquisition happens).

*Also seen in:* Physical (a lock is held per-host, so placement matters). Rendered in full here;
its higher-level siblings, the mediator and single-writer registries, sit beside it below.

## Two ways to make a concurrent write safe: the mediator and the single-writer registries

<!-- section-terms: mediator-registry, single-writer-registry, concurrency -->

<!-- point: capping-how-many-and-allowing-exactly-one-fail-differently | Capping how-many and allowing exactly-one fail differently. | terms: mediator-registry, single-writer-registry, concurrency -->
The synchronization model catalogs the raw locks. Two higher-level registries sit above it, and
together they draw a distinction: **there are two ways to make a concurrent
write safe, and they fail differently.** You can *cap how many* run at once, or you can insist
that *exactly one* may write. A mediator does the first; a single-writer contract does the
second. Confusing them is how a torn write hides behind a semaphore that was only ever rationing
load.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [synchronization model](appendix-b-synchronization-model.html).

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [concurrency contracts](appendix-b-concurrency-contracts.html).

### The mediator registry {#mediator-registry}

<!-- section-terms: mediator-registry -->

<!-- point: the-mediator-registry-caps-how-many-run-at-once | The mediator registry caps how many run at once. | terms: mediator-registry, mediation-coverage, resource-contention -->
<!-- index-def: mediator-registry -->
The mediator registry is the *how-many* half. It declares which heavy or shared-resource
subprocess invocations must run through a serializer — the test runner, the build tool, the
whole-repo lint mutex — and at what concurrency cap (1 for a mutex, M for a semaphore). Its
quality property is **mediation coverage**: the serializer enforcer can only guard a call that
reaches it, so a newly-added raw call that skips the serializer is invisible to the enforcer.
The coverage lint closes exactly that blind spot: an unmediated call to a mediated class fails
the build at author time rather than surfacing as a host-trampling race in production. The failure it prevents is
*resource contention*: too many heavy processes on one box. Its full construct-and-invariant
treatment is in the appendix.

### The single-writer registry {#single-writer-registry}

<!-- section-terms: single-writer-registry -->

<!-- point: the-single-writer-registry-allows-exactly-one-writer | The single-writer registry allows exactly one writer. | terms: single-writer-registry, torn-write, concurrency -->
<!-- index-def: single-writer-registry -->
The single-writer registry is the *exactly-one* half, and its failure mode is what makes it a
separate model. A mediator caps how many may run; a single-writer contract says one function,
and only one, may write a given piece of shared state. The failure it guards against is a
*torn or interleaved write* — two writers corrupting the same state — which a concurrency cap
of two would happily permit, because two mediated processes racing on the same record is still
within cap. Its quality property is **single-writer coverage**: every mutator of shared state
has a declared monopoly, and a second discovered writer of that state is a finding.

**Concurrency control answers HOW MANY. The registry answers WHO.** A mediator bounds
contention; the single-writer registry bounds ownership — and only naming both keeps a
corruption bug from hiding inside a load limit.

This one cost me a day of chasing a corrupted registry file before I saw that two "safely rationed"
writers were the whole problem: the semaphore was doing its job and guarding nothing that
mattered. Its full construct-and-invariant treatment is in the appendix.
