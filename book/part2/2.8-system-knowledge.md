# System Knowledge: Models as a System

<!-- point: system-knowledge-is-a-substrate-of-typed-models-not-a-seventh-class | The six preceding examples are not fragments of one universal model; they are a family of structured models joined where useful. System knowledge is the substrate that connects them, not a seventh class. | terms: system-knowledge, model-classes -->
The six preceding chapters each drew a different model of the same product. It would be natural to read
them as six views onto one large model that holds structure, behavior, ownership, policy, measurement,
and provenance together. DocAble has no such model. Nor does MAGE require one. What it has is a **family
of structured models**, each built for a different engineering question, joined where a join earns its keep.
System knowledge is not a seventh class of model. It is the **substrate** that lets the six classes work
as a system.

## A Layered Modeling Substrate

<!-- section-terms: system-knowledge, modeling-substrate -->

<!-- point: docable-has-a-family-of-typed-models-not-one-representation | DocAble's models differ in shape by design — registries, state machines, contracts, quantitative relations — and share identities and query surfaces rather than one universal representation. | terms: modeling-substrate, representation-form -->
<!-- index-def: modeling-substrate -->
DocAble's models differ in shape on purpose. Some are registries of components and their relations.
Some represent state transitions. Some encode contracts or decisions. Some capture quantitative
relationships. They share identities, derivations, query surfaces, and traceability where those help,
but nothing forces them into a single representation. Read the collection as a **modeling substrate**,
not as one system-knowledge graph.

<!-- point: the-substrate-is-layered-typed-data-decoupled-from-the-code-it-describes | The substrate is layered structured data, deliberately decoupled from the code it describes so the two can be reconciled. | terms: modeling-substrate, map-and-territory -->
The substrate is concrete, and it is larger than any one chapter. In DocAble it holds on the order of
ninety structured model modules plus several data dialects, organized in layers: a component-identity layer
names what exists; a per-component metadata layer annotates each one; orthogonal meta-files sit across
the top for deployment topology, inter-service edges, data flow, required environment, state machines,
and the governance graph itself. One rule governs all of it — the models are **data, not code**, and
nothing in the substrate imports the system it describes. That decoupling is the point. A model that
*is* the implementation cannot drift from it, and cannot be checked against it either. Holding the map
separate from the territory is what makes reconciliation mean something. [ref:modeling-substrate] draws
the layers and the shared spine that joins them.

<!-- label: modeling-substrate -->
<!-- figure: assets/modeling-substrate.svg | The DocAble modeling substrate: heterogeneous structured models — structural registries, behavioral models, decision contracts, ownership views, measurement and provenance models — connected through shared identities, traceability, and query surfaces rather than fused into one representation. -->

<!-- point: the-unity-is-semantic-not-syntactic | The models cohere semantically, not syntactically: the same entity named in one model resolves to the same entity in another. | terms: modeling-substrate, join-key -->
The unity that matters is semantic, not syntactic. A service named in a structural registry is the same
service a flow model references. A work item in an ownership view resolves to the lifecycle and the
measurements for that work. A provenance event identifies the operation whose effect it records. Nothing
demands the models share a file format or a metamodel; they need only agree on **who is who**.

<!-- point: derive-and-join-do-not-repeat | A model joins to its neighbours on a shared key rather than restating them; a fact copied into two models is two facts to keep in sync by hand. | terms: derive-and-join, join-key -->
<!-- index-def: derive-and-join -->
That agreement follows one discipline: **derive and join; do not repeat.** A model joins to its
neighbours on a shared key, and it never keeps a second copy of a neighbour's facts. The service-flow
model *is* the endpoint list the access policy is generated from and the call graph is checked against;
it holds no private duplicate of the endpoints. A journey model *joins* to those endpoints by call site
rather than copying them. Restate a fact in two models and you own two copies to reconcile by hand —
the exact cost the substrate exists to remove. Derivation and join are the cure.

<!-- point: an-agent-enters-through-the-object-its-task-names-and-traverses-outward | Semantic unity lets an agent enter the substrate at the entity its task names and follow relations outward, taking only the connected slice the question needs. | terms: modeling-substrate, join-key -->
Semantic unity buys a way in. An agent — or an engineer — enters the substrate through the object its
task names and traverses outward, following identities across models rather than loading everything.
A canonical query surface makes the traversal a query instead of a grep: name a component and ask what
it owns, what it depends on, which flow reaches it, what its lifecycle looks like. The agent does not
need *the whole model.* It needs the connected slice that answers the engineering question in front of
it. [ref:substrate-traversal] draws that entry and outward walk.

<!-- label: substrate-traversal -->
<!-- figure: assets/substrate-traversal.svg | Task-driven traversal: entering the substrate at the entity a task names, then following shared identities outward into structure, behavior, ownership, and onward to decision, measurement, and provenance — retrieving only the connected slice the question requires. -->

<!-- point: the-substrate-is-a-model-of-models-that-self-classifies-into-families | The substrate classifies its own members into families by failure mode — state-machine, structural-registry, contract/decision — each with its own verification discipline. | terms: modeling-substrate, representation-form -->
The substrate even models itself. DocAble's structured models self-classify into a few families keyed by the
failure they guard against: **state-machine** models of async lifecycles, whose failure mode is a race
or bad interleaving; **structural-registry** models of components and topology, whose failure mode is
model-to-code drift; **contract/decision** models of an option space and a selection function, whose
failure mode is an incomplete option set or a stale measurement. Each family carries its own
verification discipline. The diversity is not untidiness to be resolved into one graph — it *is* the
system knowledge. Different questions want different reductions, and the substrate lets each have the
one it needs.

## Purpose and Representation Shape

<!-- section-terms: representation-form, model-classes -->

<!-- point: purpose-and-representation-shape-are-two-independent-axes | A model has a purpose — the engineering question it answers — and a representation shape — the notation it happens to use; these are independent axes. | terms: representation-form, model-classes -->
A model sits on two independent axes, and separating them prevents a common confusion. The **purpose**
axis is the engineering question a model answers — the six classes of this Part: structure, behavior,
ownership, decision, measurement, provenance. The **representation-form** axis is the shape the model
takes on the page — a registry, a state machine, a contract, a quantitative relation, a table. Purpose
and shape do not line up one-to-one, as [ref:purpose-perp-shape] shows.

<!-- label: purpose-perp-shape -->
<!-- figure: assets/purpose-perp-shape.svg | Purpose and representation shape are orthogonal axes: one engineering purpose can be served by several notations, and one notation can serve several purposes. The shape is chosen to fit the question, not the reverse. -->

<!-- point: a-shape-is-chosen-when-it-is-the-right-reduction-not-because-it-is-fundamental | A state machine matters when legal transitions are the right reduction for the question, not because "state machine" is a fundamental kind of knowledge; structure alone does not make a model. | terms: representation-form, thesis-modeling -->
A state machine is not important because "state machine" ranks among some fundamental kinds of
knowledge. It earns its place when state and legal transition are the right reduction for the question
at hand. The same runs the other way: a file of structured YAML does not become a model merely by being
structured. **Purpose comes first. Representation follows.** Choose the shape that fits the question,
and let the question, not the notation, decide.

<!-- point: purpose-first-keeps-mage-lighter-than-a-comprehensive-mbse-methodology | Leading with purpose keeps MAGE lighter than a full MBSE methodology: it borrows the model-based instinct without forcing every concern into one metamodel or notation. | terms: representation-form, modeling-substrate -->
Leading with purpose keeps MAGE deliberately lighter than a comprehensive model-based-systems-engineering
methodology. It borrows the model-based instinct — represent the concern, then reason and check over the
representation — without the commitment that every engineering concern inhabit one metamodel or one
notation. The substrate takes the model-driven pattern and stops where the pattern stops paying: at the
first shape that answers the question.

## Keeping the Models Connected to the System

<!-- section-terms: map-and-territory, traceability, drift-gate -->

<!-- point: a-family-of-models-risks-a-second-system-of-record-that-diverges | A family of models invites an obvious danger — a second system of record that quietly diverges from the first — and the answer is to engineer the relationship between map and territory, not to stop modeling. | terms: map-and-territory, drift-gate -->
<!-- index-def: map-and-territory -->
A family of models raises an obvious danger: a **second system of record** that quietly diverges from
the first. The answer is not "never model." It is to engineer the relationship between map and territory
so divergence is caught rather than assumed away. Which machinery you reach for depends on which side is
authoritative.

<!-- point: name-the-direction-of-derivation-up-front | A model and the code stay equal by derivation, and derivation runs in one of two directions; the machinery that keeps them equal depends on which. | terms: derivation-direction, model-from-code, model-to-code -->
<!-- index-def: derivation-direction -->
A model and the system stay equal by **derivation**, and derivation runs in one of two directions. Name
the direction up front, because the machinery that holds each honest is different. [ref:derive-generate-trace]
lays the three cases side by side.

<!-- index-def: model-from-code -->
<!-- index-def: model-to-code -->
- **Where the implementation is authoritative, derive the model.** The code is the truth; the model is
  a projection of it, reconciled at build time. A component-zone registry reads the real directory tree;
  a journey's dependency list is induced from its real call sites. The drift check is a **reconciler** —
  it re-derives the model from the code and fails on divergence. This is the usual case for a codebase
  that pre-dated its models, which most of Part II's do.
- **Where the model is authoritative, generate the downstream artifact.** The model is the truth; code,
  configuration, or documentation are emitted from it. The access policy, the service catalog, and the
  wire-contract types are generated from the service-flow model. The drift check is a **freshness and
  provenance check** — the generated artifact carries a header, and a hand-edit or a stale regeneration
  is a finding.

<!-- label: derive-generate-trace -->
<!-- figure: assets/derive-generate-trace.svg | Three ways to keep map and territory equal, chosen by which side is authoritative: derive the model where the implementation leads, generate the artifact where the model leads, and where neither fully derives the other, hold traceability and check the decidable predicates. -->

<!-- point: where-neither-is-fully-derivable-maintain-traceability-and-check-decidable-predicates | Where neither side can be fully derived from the other, maintain traceability between them and check the predicates that are mechanically decidable. | terms: map-and-territory, traceability, drift-gate -->
<!-- index-def: traceability -->
Where neither side can be fully derived from the other, the move is **traceability plus drift checking**.
Relate each model element to the implementation that realizes it, then check the correspondences a
machine can decide. Keep that relation *live*: resolve the target against current code when the check
runs, rather than storing a line number or a symbol name that is itself another snapshot able to drift.
A deleted target then surfaces as a finding instead of a stale green edge. In DocAble a traceability
gate resolves each model-to-code anchor through a language-aware resolver — one for each source
language — and reports every anchor that no longer resolves. Before it existed, most such drifts had no
mechanical detection at all; a rename anywhere could silently strand a model's citation.

<!-- point: two-layers-cover-the-relationship-mechanical-and-judgment | Two layers cover the map-territory relationship: a mechanical layer that re-derives or checks claims, and a judgment layer for semantics no predicate yet captures. | terms: drift-gate, map-and-territory, model-drift -->
No single check covers the whole relationship, so read it as two layers. The **mechanical layer** handles
claims a machine can re-derive or test: an anchor still resolves, a declared edge still exists, an
observed dependency still belongs to the permitted set. The **judgment layer** handles the semantics no
predicate yet captures: prose whose meaning has quietly moved, an architectural distinction the schema
cannot express, an obligation whose reading stays contextual. A human, or another probabilistic reasoner,
can inspect the second layer — but that inspection is not deterministic coverage.

<!-- point: two-gaps-remain-the-semantic-miss-and-the-unmodeled-region | Two gaps survive the net: a semantic miss, where the mechanical relation resolves but its meaning changed, and an unmodeled region, where no relation exists to check. | terms: model-drift, drift-gate, map-and-territory -->
Two gaps survive the net, and both mark where judgment is filling in for representation. A **semantic
miss** is a mechanical relation that still resolves while its meaning has changed — the pointer is live,
the intent is not. An **unmodeled region** has no relation to check at all. A recurring gap is a
candidate for governance conversion later: add a model, refine the abstraction, or promote the recurring
judgment to a check once retiring it is worth the cost. [ref:model-sync-two-layer-net] draws the net and
the two ways through it.

<!-- label: model-sync-two-layer-net -->
<!-- figure: assets/model-sync-two-layer-net.svg | **Figure 2.8-1: The two-layer net.** Mechanical checks cover the modeled-and-decidable surface; judgment covers the semantics the representation does not yet make checkable. A semantic miss can slip through the first layer; an unmodeled property has no coverage at all. Read the net as coverage, not a guarantee. -->

<!-- point: an-executable-model-is-its-schema-plus-its-checked-predicates | Operationally, an executable model is its structured schema plus the predicates a machine can check over that schema; a schema without checked predicates is only half the pattern. | terms: drift-gate, thesis-modeling -->
This is why an executable model is best read as **its schema plus its checked predicates**. The schema
gives the entities, fields, edges, and states. The predicates state what must remain true over that
structure, each named by a stable id and held true at its declared cadence by a checker. Draw the schema
and stop, and it can rot the first time the code moves past it. The predicates are not invented for
neatness — they come from failures the system actually met. In the DocAble build, the gaps the models
caught were **violated-but-unstated invariants**: a property the system had always needed, never written
down, promoted to a checked predicate the moment it broke. [ref:coherence-triangle] draws that
relationship.

<!-- label: coherence-triangle -->
<!-- figure: assets/coherence-triangle.svg | The coherence triangle: a structured model **declares** invariants (predicates with stable ids), which an **invariant checker** — a drift lint or a model check — verifies at its declared cadence. The interior states the operational meaning: an executable model = its schema + its checked predicates. Each gap the fleet found was a violated-but-unstated invariant. -->

<!-- index-def: invariant -->
> #### Inset — What is an invariant? {#inset-invariant}
>
> An **invariant** is a property intended to hold over a defined model domain. In a behavioral state
> machine, it is often a predicate that must hold in every reachable state; in a structural model, that
> every observed dependency lies in a permitted set; in a measurement model, that a quantity stays below
> a bound. State both the property *and* the domain the checker claims to establish it over.
>
> Take a behavioral illustration. A job carries two fields at once — a `status` of `free`, `leased`, or
> `done`, and a `lease_owner` that names the holding worker or is empty. Different steps write the two
> fields, so they *can* fall out of agreement, which is exactly why you write the invariant down. "A job
> is leased if and only if it has an owner" is a predicate over the pair:
> `(status == "leased") == (lease_owner is not None)`. A `free` status with a lingering `lease_owner` is
> a stale lease; a `leased` status with no owner is a lost lease. Both become reachable the moment the
> two writes separate, and both break the predicate. What makes this more than a comment is that the
> predicate is *checked*, exhaustively, by a tool:
>
> ```
> STATES = product({"free", "leased", "done"}, {None, "worker-1", "worker-2"})
> def invariant(s):                                    # leased iff owned
>     return (s.status == "leased") == (s.lease_owner is not None)
> assert all(invariant(s) for s in reachable_states())  # check every reachable state
> ```

<!-- point: no-synchronization-mechanism-proves-semantic-correctness | No synchronization mechanism proves universal semantic correctness; a model can be perfectly in sync and still express the wrong architecture. | terms: map-and-territory, drift-gate, thesis-modeling -->
None of this proves the model *right*. No synchronization mechanism establishes universal semantic
correctness. A model can be structurally in sync and still express the wrong architecture. A pointer can
resolve while its meaning has changed. A schema and its generated client can agree while a live producer
emits something else. The guarantee the machinery offers is narrower, and worth stating plainly:

> **For the surface the model claims, check the correspondence that can actually be decided.**

<!-- point: the-narrow-guarantee-is-the-boundary-part-iii-begins-at | The bounded guarantee — decide the decidable over the claimed surface — is the boundary Part III opens on, where agreement is distinguished from correctness. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, map-and-territory -->
That boundary returns in Part III, where agreement and correctness part company. Synchronization buys
agreement between map and territory over a decidable surface. It does not buy correctness of the map's
claims. Holding the two apart is what keeps the substrate honest about what it has, and has not, earned.
[ref:joined-by-identity] draws the whole: heterogeneous models joined on a stable identity, not fused
into one graph.

<!-- label: joined-by-identity -->
<!-- figure: assets/joined-by-identity.svg | **Figure 2.8-2: Models are joined by identity and traceability, not forced into one universal graph.** A shared stable identity connects heterogeneous representations; the connection derives where it can, traces where it must, and checks only what is decidable. -->

## Modeling Creates Surfaces for Alignment

<!-- point: a-model-does-not-govern-by-existing-it-makes-a-property-explicit | A model does not govern the system by existing; each class can name a property — a forbidden dependency, an illegal transition, a budget, an evidence requirement — without preventing its violation. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
A model does not govern the system merely by existing. The structural model can *name* a forbidden
dependency without preventing it. The recovery model can name an illegal transition without rejecting it.
The measurement model can represent a budget without stopping the work that exceeds it. The provenance
model can specify the evidence an operation should leave without requiring the operation to leave it.
Each class makes a property explicit; none, by itself, gives that property force.

<!-- point: modeling-makes-a-property-explicit-alignment-gives-selected-properties-consequence | That separation is deliberate: modeling makes an engineering property explicit, and Alignment gives selected properties consequence — a constraint, a sensor, or a validated gate. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
The separation is deliberate. **Modeling makes an engineering property explicit. Alignment gives
selected properties consequence.** A property, once represented, becomes a **surface** something can act
on: a constraint that forbids the violation, a sensor that reports it, a validator whose gate blocks on
it. The model supplies the property; Alignment decides which properties carry weight and by which
mechanism. [ref:modeling-creates-surfaces] draws the whole path, from territory to consequence.

<!-- label: modeling-creates-surfaces -->
<!-- figure: assets/modeling-creates-surfaces.svg | **Modeling creates surfaces for Alignment.** Abstraction reduces the territory to a model; the model makes an engineering property explicit; the property becomes a surface Alignment can act on through a constraint, a sensor, or a validator and its gate, and only then does a violation carry consequence. -->

<!-- point: the-richer-the-representation-the-richer-the-properties-available-to-govern | The richer the representation, the richer the properties available to govern; local syntactic properties need little modeling, but architecture, temporal obligations, distributed ownership, and end-to-end policy need semantics before they can be judged. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, map-and-territory -->
The richer the representation, the richer the properties available to govern. A local static check can
constrain a local, syntactic property with almost no explicit modeling. Architectural boundaries,
temporal obligations, distributed ownership, provenance requirements, and end-to-end policies each need
more semantics on the table before they can be judged at all. This is why the Part's rhythm ran from
architecture through time, ownership, permission, quantity, and history to composition: each class put a
harder property within reach of a check.

## The Handoff to Alignment

<!-- point: alignment-can-exist-without-explicit-models-its-limit-is-what-the-environment-can-state-and-observe | Alignment can exist without explicit models — a sandbox, a compiler, a test, a reviewer are real authority — but their limit is what the environment can state and observe without reconstructing semantics. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
Can Alignment exist without explicit models? **Yes.** A sandbox can forbid network access. A compiler can
reject an illegal construct. A test can block a regression against observed behavior. A reviewer can
adjudicate a system-level obligation no tool can currently express. These are real forms of authority,
and useful systems are built on them. Their limit is what the environment can state and observe without
reconstructing the missing semantics each time.

<!-- point: models-change-the-scale-of-the-questions-we-can-reason-about | Models enlarge the engineering questions we can reason about and the properties we can make explicit; as representation gets richer, more judgment becomes eligible for analysis and enforcement, and what stays outside stays probabilistic or human. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
That is the claim this Part has earned. Models enlarge the engineering questions we can reason about and
the properties we can make explicit. As representation grows richer, more judgment becomes eligible for
analysis and for deterministic enforcement; what stays outside the representation stays probabilistic,
human, or both.

<!-- point: part-2-ends-where-part-3-begins-the-models-already-pay-authority-is-a-separate-question | Part II ends where Part III begins: the models already pay in reasoning, prediction, composition, and traceability — they need not wait for enforcement — and Part III asks the separate question of authority. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
The models need not wait for enforcement to be worth their cost. They already pay in reasoning,
prediction, composition, and traceability. Part III opens on the separate question of authority — which
obligations the environment should hold, by which mechanisms, and with what evidence — and it begins
exactly at the boundary this chapter drew: the difference between a map that agrees with its territory
and a map that is right.
