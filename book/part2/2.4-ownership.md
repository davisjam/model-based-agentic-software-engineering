# Ownership Models: Who Controls What, and When?

With one worker, *who owns this job?* has an obvious answer. Add many workers, a crash, and a sweep
that reclaims abandoned work, and the same question acquires time and contention: ownership can be
claimed, can expire, and can be reclaimed, and a holder that went away can return to find its claim
gone. An ownership model keeps those relationships and discards the rest — who holds what, on what
terms, and for how long.

## The Canonical Move: Resource Ownership

<!-- point: ownership-has-a-long-vocabulary-of-claims-and-leases | Engineering has long represented control over a shared resource as locks, leases, and claims with a lifecycle; ownership combines a relation (holder to resource) with behavior over time. -->
Software engineering has a long vocabulary for control over a shared resource: locks and
leases[cite: graycheriton1989], resource-allocation relationships, capability and claim relationships,
ownership transfer, and the lifecycle of a claim from acquisition through expiry to reclamation.
Ownership is the family the earlier chapters most obviously touch. A claim relates a holder to a
resource, which is structural; the claim is acquired, held, and released over time, which is
behavioral. That overlap is why ownership is the natural place to show one representation carrying more
than one class of question. [ref:canonical-ownership] draws the move.

<!-- label: canonical-ownership -->
<!-- figure: assets/canonical-ownership.svg | *The ownership move: a claim over time.* A claim binds a holder to a resource and carries the generation and lifetime that tell a current claim from a superseded one; a second, stale claimant is exactly what the generation exists to reject. Its analyses explore concurrent interleavings for an invariant violation. -->

<!-- point: a-claim-carries-the-facts-that-make-contention-decidable | A claim binds a holder to a resource and carries which holder, which generation of claim, and when it expires — the generation being what tells a current claim from a superseded one. -->
A claim binds a holder to a resource and carries the facts that make contention decidable: which
holder, which generation of claim, and when it expires. The generation is the fact a bare "A owns X"
edge cannot express — it distinguishes a *current* claim from a *superseded* one. The properties a
designer wants to state follow from that: at most one valid holder at a time, a stale holder cannot
affect the current one, a release or transfer is valid only from the current claim, and abandoned work
is eventually reclaimed.

<!-- point: ownership-failures-live-in-interleavings-so-the-analysis-is-state-space-exploration | Because the hard ownership failures live in interleavings rather than any single transition, the characteristic analysis is state-space exploration over concurrent claim sequences with invariant checking. -->
The hard failures do not live in any single transition. They live in the interleavings — the orders in
which concurrent claims, expiries, and reclaims can occur. So the characteristic analysis is state-space
exploration over those sequences, with an invariant checked at each reachable configuration. This is the
behavioral toolkit of the previous chapter pointed at a concurrency question, which is again why
ownership reads as an overlap family.

## DocAble: In-Flight Ownership

DocAble leases each in-flight job to one worker. A lease carries an owner, a monotonically increasing
generation, and a lifetime; when a lease looks abandoned it can be reclaimed and the job re-leased to
another worker at the next generation.

<!-- box-family: model-card -->
> #### In-flight lease · Ownership
>
> - **Engineering question** — Who currently owns this in-flight work, and can a returning stale owner
>   disturb the current claim?
> - **Model** — a claim relating a worker to a job, stamped with a generation and a lifetime; reclaim
>   advances the generation.
> - **Property** — a stale claim cannot clear or supersede the current one.
> - **Quality attribute** — correctness under concurrency; crash-safety.

<!-- point: leased-iff-owner-is-the-models-definition | A job is leased exactly when it has an owner; the two are the same fact from two sides, so the model represents ownership as the presence of a current claim — this is what "leased" means, and it sets up the invariant that follows. -->
Start with what the model *means*. A job is leased exactly when it has an owner; the two are the same
fact seen from two sides, so the model represents ownership as the presence of a current claim rather
than as a separate flag that could disagree with it. That is a definition, not a property that could
fail. It sets up the invariant that can.

<!-- point: the-epoch-generation-makes-a-stale-release-inapplicable | Suppose A holds generation 7, stalls, and is reclaimed; the job is re-leased to B at generation 8; a late release from A carrying generation 7 must not clear B's generation-8 claim, and the generation makes that decidable. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
The consequential invariant is about time and contention. Suppose worker A holds the claim at
generation 7, appears to stall, and is reclaimed; the job is re-leased to worker B at generation 8. If A
now wakes and tries to release using its generation-7 claim, that release must not clear B's current
generation-8 claim. The generation makes the distinction decidable: a release carrying an old generation
names a claim that no longer owns, so it is inapplicable to the current one.

[ref:in-flight-ownership-states] draws the claim and its lifetime; the generation is the small addition
that separates a live claim from a stale one.

<!-- label: in-flight-ownership-states -->
<!-- figure: assets/in-flight-ownership-states.svg | *In-flight ownership model.* A current claim records owner, epoch, and lifetime. Merge releases the claim; expiry makes it reclaimable. The model distinguishes a live claim from a stale one without representing the storage machinery that implements the lease. -->

<!-- point: the-model-exposes-the-question-a-compare-and-set-holds-the-line | The generation makes "is this release applicable to the current claim?" decidable — the property the model exposes; whether a release path compares generations and rejects the stale one is the Part III authority, verified separately by exhaustive interleaving search. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
As with the recovery ordering, the two layers stay separate. The generation *exposes* the question — is
this release applicable to the current claim? — as one a checker or a release path can decide. Whether
the running system *holds* the line — whether the release path actually compares generations and rejects
the stale one — is a matter of authority for Part III. That guard is a **hard** one: the mechanism
refuses the stale release rather than merely reporting it, and an exhaustive search over the interleavings
serves as its verification. Here the model's job ends at making the invariant precise and checkable.

## One Representation, Several Questions

<!-- point: the-lease-answers-ownership-behavioral-and-measurement-questions-at-once | Read as ownership the lease says who controls a job; read as behavioral it traces a claimed-held-expired-reclaimed lifecycle; counted, it measures how much work is in flight — one representation, three questions. -->
The lease is the Part's clearest case of one representation answering several classes of question. Read
it as an ownership model and it says who controls a job. Read the same claims as a behavioral model and
they trace a lifecycle — claimed, held, expired, reclaimed. Count the active leases and the same
representation becomes a measurement: how much work is genuinely in flight right now.
[ref:in-flight-lease-overlap] sets the three readings side by side.

<!-- label: in-flight-lease-overlap -->
<!-- figure: assets/in-flight-lease-overlap.svg | *Model classes overlap.* The same lease state supports ownership, behavioral, and measurement questions. The classes distinguish purpose, not storage objects. -->

<!-- point: overlap-confirms-the-classes-sort-questions-not-data-structures | That a lease serves ownership, behavioral, and measurement questions at once confirms the §2.1 claim that the classes sort questions, not data structures — the observation §2.8 builds on to connect the models. -->
That a lease supports ownership, behavioral, and measurement questions at once is not a sign the model
is under-specified. It is the point the classes have been making since the opening chapter: they sort
*questions*, not data structures, and one purposeful representation can answer several when they share
the same underlying facts. This is the observation the closing chapter builds on when it connects the
models through shared identity.

<!-- point: ownership-boundary | The lease model makes ownership under contention precise and its invariant checkable; it does not establish the implementation obeys the invariant, nor decide what a stale release should trigger. -->
The lease model makes ownership under contention precise and its key invariant checkable. It does not
establish that the implementation obeys the invariant, nor decide what should happen when a stale
release is attempted — surface a conflict, drop the release, raise an alarm. Modeling names the claim;
Alignment decides its consequence.

Ownership introduced permission implicitly: a stale claimant *may not* clear a current lease. The next
family makes permission the whole question.
