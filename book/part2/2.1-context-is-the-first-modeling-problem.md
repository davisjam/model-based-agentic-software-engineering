# Context Is the First Modeling Problem

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, reasoning-horizon, context-window -->

<!-- point: a-reasoner-arrives-knowing-nothing-about-your-system | A foundation model knows the languages and patterns; it does not know your system — those facts live in the engineering environment. | terms: reasoning-horizon, context-window, thesis-modeling -->
A foundation model knows Python, React, queues, databases, and common architectural patterns. It does
not acquire your service boundaries, deployment topology, ownership rules, yesterday's migration, or
local engineering conventions from pretraining. Those facts live in the engineering environment — in
source, configuration, registries, documentation, history, and the people who maintain the system — and
each reasoning episode has to recover the subset the task needs.

<!-- point: rediscovery-is-the-binding-cost-at-system-scale | Reconstruction from raw artifacts is possible; cost and scale are the problem — past the point where the reasoner can keep the state active, the next agent pays it again. | terms: reasoning-horizon, context-window, long-horizon-task -->
An agent can reconstruct much of that knowledge from raw artifacts. Point it at the repository and it
can often discover what calls what, where a service runs, and who appears to own which piece. The
problem is cost and scale. Once the task requires more state than the reasoner can keep usefully active,
exploration becomes selective and repeated; the next agent may pay much of the same reconstruction cost
again.

<!-- point: the-first-purpose-of-a-model-is-better-context | The first modeling move is modest: externalize engineering knowledge that should not have to be rediscovered. | terms: thesis-modeling, reasoning-horizon, context-window -->
The first **modeling move** is therefore modest: externalize engineering knowledge that should not have
to be rediscovered. Name the services, modules, APIs, queues, owners, requirements, policies, and tests;
name the relations among them. The agent can then retrieve the connected slice its task touches instead
of rebuilding that slice from raw files every time. The represented domain need not be the product alone:
an engineering method can itself be modeled — its situations, moves, and selection criteria made explicit
for an agent to reason through — and [appendix: appendix-skill-recipe] returns to this
when it builds the self-governance skill from the same recipe.

<!-- point: the-modeling-thesis-in-its-weakest-form | The Modeling Thesis in its weakest form: externalize engineering knowledge into a compact world to reason through; this chapter earns only the first step. | terms: thesis-modeling, reasoning-horizon, context-window -->
<!-- index-def: thesis-modeling -->
**The Modeling Thesis.** Externalize engineering knowledge and intent into explicit, structured
representations that give engineers and agents a compact world to reason through. In other words, don't
make every agent rediscover the architecture from raw code; move durable engineering knowledge into
representations the next engineer and the next agent can start from.

This chapter begins with the weakest form: better externalized context. The chapters that follow make the
representation carry progressively more of the system's meaning.

<!-- point: engineering-is-comparison-through-representations | Engineering compares alternatives through representations that suppress irrelevant detail; commodity intelligence increases the return on making the representation explicit and machine-readable. | terms: engineering, software-engineering, thesis-modeling -->
<!-- index-def: engineering -->
<!-- index-def: software-engineering -->
**Engineering** compares alternatives through representations that suppress irrelevant detail — an
architecture, a queueing model, a state machine, a stress calculation — and reasons about the properties
each will produce before committing to one. **Software engineering** applies that move to code: choosing
among workable designs by the properties they will produce, before building rather than after. Commodity
intelligence increases the return on making those representations explicit, reusable, and machine-readable,
because the same engineering knowledge can guide many inexpensive acts of implementation.

## Knowledge graphs and engineering models

<!-- section-terms: rag, graphrag, externalized-knowledge, system-knowledge-graph -->

<!-- point: agent-architectures-augment-the-model-with-external-knowledge | Agent architectures already externalize knowledge; a knowledge graph serves as an external reasoning substrate. | terms: rag, graphrag, externalized-knowledge, context-window -->
<!-- index-def: rag -->
<!-- index-def: graphrag -->
Modern agent architectures already externalize knowledge. Two approaches are common: retrieval over
documents — *retrieval-augmented generation*, or RAG [cite: lewis2020rag] — and retrieval over structured
relationships — knowledge graphs and GraphRAG [cite: edge2024graphrag]. In these systems the knowledge
graph acts as an external reasoning substrate: it organizes entities and relationships so an agent can
retrieve context, traverse connections, and infer more than model weights or loose documents allow.

<!-- point: mage-begins-from-the-same-observation-asks-what-work-a-representation-can-do | MAGE begins from the same observation, then asks what additional work a representation can do; shape does not distinguish the artifacts, semantics do. | terms: thesis-modeling, system-knowledge-graph, externalized-knowledge -->
MAGE begins from the same observation — durable engineering knowledge belongs outside the foundation
model — and asks what additional work a representation can do. A knowledge graph, architectural
registry, state machine, and typed configuration model may all be graph-shaped internally; their shape
is not what distinguishes them. Their semantics do.

<!-- point: authority-is-a-separate-dimension | A knowledge graph exposes what exists; an engineering model carries the semantics an engineering question needs. Authority is a separate dimension. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
A knowledge graph may simply expose what exists and how facts relate. An engineering model goes further
when its structure is chosen to answer an engineering question and carries the semantics or intent
needed to reason about that question. **Authority is a separate dimension.** A sandbox can govern an
action without a model; a validator can use a rich model to check an obligation that neither the sandbox
nor the raw artifact expresses. The more explicit the representation, the
larger the set of properties available to reasoning, analysis, and — where useful — deterministic
governance.

## The system knowledge graph

<!-- section-terms: system-knowledge-graph, structured-relationships -->

<!-- point: the-simplest-useful-form-is-a-graph | The simplest useful form of that knowledge is a graph. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, structured-relationships -->
<!-- index-def: system-knowledge-graph -->
The simplest useful form for that knowledge is a graph — the *system knowledge graph*. Its nodes are
engineering entities: services, modules, endpoints, queues, databases, deployment targets, requirements,
tests, owners, policies. Its edges name the relations: calls, depends on, deployed to, owned by,
implements, verified by, governed by. Properties hang from both — a service has a tier, an endpoint an
authentication requirement, a deployment a region.

<!-- point: graph-is-structure-not-storage | Graph names the structure, not the storage technology. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, structured-relationships -->
The graph need not live in a graph database. Assemble it from YAML, repository metadata, infrastructure
declarations, schemas, and registries joined on stable identifiers, and you have the same graph. The word
*graph* names the structure, not the storage. What matters is that the knowledge has stable entities,
explicit relations, and machine-readable joins, so a tool or an agent can traverse it instead of
re-deriving it from raw files.

<!-- point: relevance-follows-relationships | Context engineering expressed structurally: relevance follows relationships. | terms: context-engineering, system-knowledge-graph, structured-relationships -->
<!-- index-def: context-engineering -->
This makes one important part of *context engineering* structural. Choosing what to put in the prompt
stops being a guess about which documents might help and becomes a question with a structural answer:
which engineering facts and relations does this task touch? Relevance follows relationships. Start at
the service the task names, and its neighbors come with it — the callers, the queue it consumes, the
policy that governs the queue, the tests that carry the policy. The agent reasons over a connected slice
instead of a document dump.

## Industry already lives here

<!-- section-terms: knowledge-representation, externalized-knowledge, thesis-modeling -->

<!-- point: knowledge-rep-is-the-first-rung | Externalized knowledge representation is the most basic move of the Modeling Thesis, and most of industry already stands on it. | terms: knowledge-representation, thesis-modeling, externalized-knowledge -->
<!-- index-def: knowledge-representation -->
<!-- index-def: externalized-knowledge -->
Externalizing knowledge this way is the most basic move of the Modeling Thesis, and much of current
practice already stands on it. Service catalogs expose components and ownership. Dependency graphs expose
what calls what. Infrastructure-as-code exposes placement. Policy corpora expose obligations. Developer
portals join these surfaces for the engineers who read them and, increasingly, for the agents that reason
over them. This is *knowledge representation*: engineering knowledge moved out of a person's head, or an
agent's transient context, into an artifact the next one retrieves rather than reconstructs.

<!-- point: knowledge-rep-universal-graph-common-executable-not | Knowledge representation is universal in the corpus; graph-shaped representation common; invariant-bearing models not. | terms: knowledge-representation, system-knowledge-graph, thesis-modeling -->
Knowledge representation is universal in the cases examined here; graph-shaped system representation is
common; invariant-bearing executable models are not. The organizations differ in how strongly they
structure that knowledge, yet all externalize facts an agent would otherwise reconstruct. Zenseact makes
the motive plain: its routing machinery selects the task-relevant expertise and loads only that, rather
than pouring the whole corpus into the window.

<!-- worked-examples: externalized-knowledge -->
### Example — Spotify
Spotify externalized its service estate before its agents arrived. The Backstage catalog names every
service, its owners, its dependencies, and its endpoints, each with a stable identity a tool can resolve —
the estate written down as a retrievable graph rather than carried in anyone's head. An agent that lands
on a migration reads the slice the task touches instead of crawling thousands of repositories.

### Example — Cloudflare
Cloudflare externalized its standards rather than its system. It turned a
sprawl of RFCs into a curated corpus where every requirement carries a stable slug that survives the
edits around it, so an agent retrieves the obligation governing a change without its author present. The
obligations are represented far more strongly than the system they govern, which stays mostly unmodeled.

### Example — Shopify
Shopify externalizes knowledge more softly. Every public agent session becomes a searchable artifact the
company mines into skills, conventions, and defaults, so one engineer's experience is retrieved by the
next agent rather than lost when the window closes. The representation is informal — prose skills loaded
on demand, not a structured model — externalization that is real, retrievable, and deliberately soft.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble is one software implementation of the pattern. It assembles a system knowledge graph from
registries, schemas, and YAML joined on stable identifiers, so an agent retrieves the connected slice a
task needs instead of re-reading the repository each time. It reaches further later; here it does only
what the others do — move the system's facts out of the window and onto disk where the next agent finds
them.

<!-- takeaway -->
Externalize engineering knowledge so the next reasoner retrieves it instead of reconstructing it.
Structure only as far as the engineering question requires.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

<!-- point: externalization-saves-reconstruction-structure-creates-leverage | Externalization first saves reconstruction; structure then creates additional leverage — typed relationships can be traversed and analyzed, explicit semantics support questions loose context cannot answer reliably. | terms: thesis-modeling, structured-relationships -->
Externalization first saves reconstruction. Structure then creates additional leverage: typed
relationships can be traversed and analyzed, while explicit semantics and intent can support questions
that loose context cannot answer reliably. The next chapter crosses that line.
