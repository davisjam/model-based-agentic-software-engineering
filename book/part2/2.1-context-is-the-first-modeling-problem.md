# Context Is the First Modeling Problem

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, reasoning-horizon, context-window -->

<!-- point: a-reasoner-arrives-knowing-nothing-about-your-system | A foundation model knows the languages and patterns; it does not know your system — those facts live in the engineering environment. | terms: reasoning-horizon, context-window, thesis-modeling -->
A foundation model knows Python, React, queues, databases, and common architectural patterns. It does
not acquire your service boundaries, deployment topology, ownership rules, yesterday's migration, or
local engineering conventions from pretraining. Those facts live in the engineering environment — in
source, configuration, registries, documentation, history, and the people who maintain the system — and
each reasoning episode has to recover the subset the task needs.

<!-- point: rediscovery-is-the-binding-cost-at-system-scale | Reconstruction from raw artifacts is possible; cost and scale are the problem — past the point where the reasoner can keep the state active, the next agent pays it again. | terms: reasoning-horizon, context-window, long-horizon-task -->
An agent can reconstruct much of that knowledge from raw artifacts. Point it at the repository and it
can often discover what calls what, where a service runs, and who appears to own which piece. The
problem is cost and scale. Once the task requires more state than the reasoner can keep usefully active,
exploration becomes selective and repeated; the next agent may pay much of the same reconstruction cost
again.

<!-- point: the-first-purpose-of-a-model-is-better-context | The first modeling move is modest: externalize engineering knowledge that should not have to be rediscovered. | terms: thesis-modeling, reasoning-horizon, context-window -->
The first **modeling move** is therefore modest: externalize engineering knowledge that should not have
to be rediscovered. Name the services, modules, APIs, queues, owners, requirements, policies, and tests;
name the relations among them. The agent then retrieves the connected slice its task touches instead of
rebuilding that slice from raw files every time.

<!-- point: the-modeling-thesis-in-its-weakest-form | The Modeling Thesis in its weakest form: externalize engineering knowledge into a compact world to reason through. | terms: thesis-modeling, reasoning-horizon, context-window -->
<!-- index-def: thesis-modeling -->
**The Modeling Thesis.** Externalize engineering knowledge and intent into explicit, structured
representations that give engineers and agents a compact world to reason through. In other words, don't
make every agent rediscover the architecture from raw code; move durable engineering knowledge into
representations the next engineer and the next agent can start from.

This Part begins with that weakest form — better externalized context — and, chapter by chapter, makes
the representation carry more of the system's meaning. This chapter sets up the coordinate system the
rest of the Part reads from: what a model is, and the small set of questions that decide which model to
build.

## A model is a purposeful reduction

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, model-as-map, scope-of-modeling -->

<!-- point: a-model-is-a-reduction-not-an-approximation | A model is a purposeful reduction; its value is what it leaves out. The engineering question decides what to keep. | terms: model-as-map, scope-of-modeling, thesis-modeling -->
<!-- index-def: model -->
A **model** is a purposeful reduction of a system. It preserves the information one engineering question
needs and suppresses the detail that question does not. Its value is not that it holds more — it is that
it holds less. A reduction an engineer or agent can reason through beats a faithful copy no one can hold
in view.

<!-- point: the-question-decides-what-to-keep | Hold the system constant, change the question, and the model changes; there is no single model of the system. | terms: model-as-map, scope-of-modeling, map-and-territory -->
The question is the design input. Ask whether two workers can process the same job at once, and the
color of the web interface falls away; you need who can own work, how ownership is acquired, when it
expires, and what happens after a crash. Ask instead whether one service may bypass another, and the
ownership details fall away; now you need components, communication edges, and the seams a call must pass
through. The system did not change. The question did.

> **Hold the system constant. Change the engineering question. Watch the model change.**

<!-- point: there-is-no-model-of-the-system | There is no model of the system, only purposeful reductions that each answer one question. | terms: model-as-map, scope-of-modeling -->
There is no single "model of the system," only reductions built to expose particular properties. A model
earns its place by the question it settles, not by how much of the system it draws. **What should I
model, and what will the model let me know?** — that is the question this Part asks of every system, and
asks again at every turn.

<!-- point: a-reduction-first-even-when-executable | A model is a reduction first, even when it runs; executability changes what you can DO with it, not whether it abstracts. | terms: model-as-map, executable-source-of-truth -->
The reduction comes first even when the representation is later made to run. Some models in this Part are
executable: a machine queries them, derives from them, or checks them against the code. Executability
changes what you can *do* with a model; it does not eliminate the abstraction that created it. That is
why the examples ahead are drawn as reductions before any implementation is discussed — so a reader sees
the model, not fancy code.

## Structure is what admits the machine

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, system-knowledge-graph, structured -->

<!-- point: structured-is-the-word-doing-the-work | Structure — an explicit declared shape a machine can read — is what lets a representation be traversed, checked, and joined. | terms: structured, system-knowledge-graph -->
<!-- index-def: structured -->
Externalizing knowledge saves reconstruction only if a machine can read it back. The word doing that
work is **structured**: an explicit, declared shape — a schema, not prose. Prose can be read; only a
structured representation can be traversed by a tool, checked against the code, and joined to the system
it represents.

<!-- point: the-simplest-useful-form-is-a-graph | The simplest useful form of externalized knowledge is a graph of engineering entities and their relations; graph names the structure, not the storage. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, structured -->
<!-- index-def: system-knowledge-graph -->
The simplest useful form is a graph — the *system knowledge graph*. Its nodes are engineering entities:
services, modules, endpoints, queues, databases, requirements, tests, owners. Its edges name the
relations: calls, depends on, deployed to, owned by, verified by. The graph need not live in a graph
database; assemble it from YAML, registries, and schemas joined on stable identifiers and you have the
same graph. The word *graph* names the structure, not the storage. Relevance then follows relationships:
start at the entity a task names, and its neighbors come with it, so the agent reasons over a connected
slice instead of a document dump.

<!-- point: shape-does-not-distinguish-a-model-semantics-do | A knowledge graph exposes what exists; an engineering model carries the semantics a question needs. Shape does not distinguish them. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
A knowledge graph, a registry, a state machine, and a typed configuration model may all be graph-shaped
inside. Their shape is not what distinguishes them; their semantics are. A knowledge graph exposes what
exists and how facts relate. A representation becomes an **engineering model** when its structure is
chosen to answer an engineering question and carries the semantics or intent needed to reason about it.
And **authority is a separate dimension**: a sandbox can govern an action with no model at all, while a
validator can use a rich model to check an obligation neither the sandbox nor the raw artifact expresses.

<!-- point: industry-already-lives-here | Externalized knowledge representation is the first move of the Modeling Thesis, and most of industry already stands on it. | terms: knowledge-representation, thesis-modeling, externalized-knowledge -->
Externalizing knowledge this way is the most basic move of the Modeling Thesis, and much of current
practice already stands on it. Spotify wrote its service estate down as a retrievable catalog before its
agents arrived. Cloudflare externalized its standards rather than its system, giving every requirement a
stable slug an agent can retrieve without its author present. Shopify externalizes more softly, mining
every agent session into skills the next agent loads on demand. DocAble — the production accessibility
service this book is built on — assembles its graph from registries, schemas, and YAML joined on stable
identifiers. Each moves facts out of the window and onto disk where the next reasoner retrieves them
rather than reconstructs them.

## Representations gain capability by degrees

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, capability-ladder -->

<!-- point: capability-comes-in-rungs | A representation gains capability one property at a time: externalize, structure, add semantics and obligation, derive or check. Each step answers more questions. | terms: capability-ladder, structured, thesis-modeling -->
<!-- index-def: capability-ladder -->
Externalization saves reconstruction; structure then creates leverage, and the leverage arrives in
steps. Suppose the graph records that a worker consumes a queue. As bare context, the edge helps an
agent locate code. Type the worker, queue, and relation, and a tool can traverse the edge reliably. Add
a semantic property and an authored obligation — only workers of class X may consume queues of class Y —
and the representation answers a question the bare edge could not. Derive it from the implementation, or
generate the implementation from it, and its correspondence to the system becomes mechanically testable.
Each step makes additional engineering questions answerable.

<!-- point: capability-is-not-maturity-authority-is-a-separate-axis | Read the steps as a capability ladder, not a maturity model; authority is a separate axis — strong local controls can exist with little modeling. | terms: capability-ladder, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
Read those steps as a capability ladder, not a maturity ranking. Authority is a separate axis. A team
can hold real authority — sandboxes, tests, compilers that reject a construct — while exposing little
system intent through invariant-bearing models. Docker works this way: heavy reliance on sandboxes and
tests over raw artifacts, comparatively little modeled intent. A stronger model earns its cost when a
recurring engineering question cannot be retired cheaply at the current rung.

<!-- point: the-consequence-is-selective-modeling | The consequence is not "model everything" but selective modeling: climb a rung where a fact is repeatedly reconstructed or a property repeatedly adjudicated. | terms: capability-ladder, thesis-modeling -->
The practical consequence is not *model everything*. It is selective modeling: notice where people
repeatedly reconstruct a fact, or repeatedly adjudicate a property the environment could settle if the
right abstraction existed. A catalog that ends the daily "who owns this?" rediscovery is a cheap rung
well spent. An invariant is worth stating the third time the same class of bug ships. Let recurring
judgment decide when to climb.

<!-- point: modeling-expands-the-surface-authority-can-operate-over | A stronger representation pays twice: in reasoning, and by making obligations explicit enough for an Alignment mechanism to consume. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
The climb pays twice. It pays first in reasoning: a status string becomes a transition, an ownership
convention becomes an explicit relation. It pays again by making obligations explicit enough for a
machine to evaluate — the service relation can be compared against the observed call graph, the legal
transition table against attempted changes. **Modeling does not create authority by itself, and
authority does not require modeling. Modeling expands the semantic surface over which authority can
operate.** Part III returns to that boundary; each example chapter ahead marks where it will.

## Six classes of engineering question

<!-- section-terms: model-classes, projection, thesis-modeling -->

<!-- point: models-sort-by-the-question-they-answer | Models sort by the engineering question they answer; the classes are the coordinate system for the rest of the Part. | terms: model-classes, projection -->
The models ahead sort by the kind of question each answers. This is the one coordinate system to carry
forward — a working ontology, organized by engineering purpose rather than by notation.

<!-- point: the-six-class-ontology-is-useful-not-exhaustive | The ontology caveat: six classes organized by the engineering question a model answers, useful rather than exhaustive, and able to overlap. | terms: model-classes -->
> MAGE uses six model classes as a working ontology. The classes are organized by the engineering
> question a model helps answer, not by notation or implementation form. They are useful rather than
> exhaustive, and they can overlap: one represented object may participate in several classes.

The six classes ([ref:model-ontology]) name the questions the rest of the Part is built around:

- **Structural** — what exists, and how the parts relate?
- **Behavioral** — what may happen, and in what order?
- **Ownership** — who controls what, and for how long?
- **Decision** — what is allowed, and what choice is appropriate?
- **Measurement** — how much, and against what envelope?
- **Provenance** — what happened, and what evidence records it?

<!-- point: the-classes-are-views-not-boxes | The classes classify questions, not data structures; one represented fact can answer several. The lease is Ownership, Behavioral, and Measurement at once. | terms: model-classes, projection -->
These are views, not boxes. The in-flight lease we reach in the ownership chapter is naturally an
Ownership model, part of a Behavioral model, and part of a Measurement model at once. The classification
tells us which question we are asking of it, not which drawer it lives in. A class suggests the questions
to ask and the relationships worth preserving; it does not prescribe one notation, and a useful model may
cross class boundaries.

<!-- label: model-ontology -->
<!-- figure: assets/model-ontology.svg | *MAGE's working model ontology.* The six classes organize representations by the engineering question each answers. They may overlap, and a class does not prescribe a particular representation form. -->

<!-- point: system-knowledge-is-substrate-not-a-seventh-class | System knowledge is the connected substrate where the six classes meet, not a seventh peer class. | terms: model-classes, system-knowledge-graph -->
System knowledge is not a seventh class. It is the connected substrate in which the six meet — where a
service named in a structural model is the same service a decision model references, and a work item in
an ownership model resolves to the measurements associated with it. The final chapter of the Part treats
that substrate directly.

## Purpose comes first; representation follows

<!-- section-terms: model-classes, representation-form, projection -->

<!-- point: purpose-and-shape-are-different-axes | A model's class (its purpose) is a different axis from its representation shape (graph, state machine, table); the same purpose admits several forms. | terms: representation-form, model-classes, projection -->
The class names a model's purpose. Its **representation form** — graph, registry, state machine,
contract, table, quantitative relation — is a separate axis. A state machine is not important because
"state machine" is a fundamental kind of knowledge. It is useful when state and legal transition are the
right reduction for the question at hand. Structured YAML does not become a model merely because it is
structured.

<!-- point: several-forms-can-serve-one-purpose | The engineering question fixes the purpose; several forms may serve it, and one form may serve several purposes. | terms: representation-form, model-classes -->
Purpose comes first; representation follows. The engineering question determines what the model is for;
several forms may be suitable for representing it, and one form — a registry, say — may serve structural,
ownership, and measurement questions in turn. This is the distinction that keeps a projection honest:
the question selects the view, and the view keeps only the entities, relations, and properties that
question needs. An agent planning a structural change need not carry every lock and user journey in its
window. Model once; project the view the question requires. The system-knowledge chapter draws this axis
in full.

## The road ahead

<!-- section-terms: model-classes, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: one-class-per-chapter-then-the-substrate | Each of the next six chapters takes one class through one DocAble model; the seventh treats the connected substrate. | terms: model-classes, thesis-modeling -->
Each of the next six chapters takes one class through one production model from DocAble:

- **Structural** — the document-processing architecture and its sanctioned mutation seam.
- **Behavioral** — a recovery ordering whose most important fact is an edge that does not exist.
- **Ownership** — an in-flight lease with an owner, an epoch, and a lifetime.
- **Decision** — a service-flow model that declares which services may communicate.
- **Measurement** — a generation-cost model relating usage, capacity, and budget.
- **Provenance** — an attribution model that records actions and derives the explanations.

<!-- point: the-substrate-and-the-forward-boundary | The final chapter treats the models as a connected substrate; Part III gives selected properties consequence. | terms: model-classes, thesis-alignment -->
The seventh chapter steps back to the connected substrate — how a family of structured models joins on shared
identity rather than collapsing into one universal graph. The order has a deliberate rhythm: architecture,
then time, then distributed ownership, then permission, then quantity, then history, then composition.
Every one of these is drawn as a model before its implementation is discussed. That is what finally makes
*these are models, not fancy code* visible rather than asserted.

<!-- point: modeling-creates-surfaces-for-alignment | A model does not govern by existing; modeling makes a property explicit, and Alignment gives selected properties consequence. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
A model does not govern the system merely by existing. The structural model can identify a forbidden
dependency without preventing it; the recovery model can name an illegal transition without rejecting it;
the measurement model can represent a budget without stopping work. That separation is deliberate.
Modeling makes an engineering property explicit. Alignment gives selected properties consequence. The
Part closes on that boundary, and Part III begins there.
