# Context Is the First Modeling Problem

<!-- section-terms: thesis-modeling, reasoning-horizon, context-window -->

<!-- point: a-reasoner-arrives-knowing-nothing-about-your-system | A foundation model knows the languages and patterns; it does not know your system. | terms: reasoning-horizon, context-window, thesis-modeling -->
A foundation model knows Python, React, queues, databases, and the common architectural patterns. It does
not know your services, your ownership boundaries, your deployment topology, or yesterday's migration.
Its training gave it general engineering sense. Your system it has never seen. Every fact it needs to
change that system safely lives in your repository, and the agent arrives at each task without them.

<!-- point: rediscovery-is-the-binding-cost-at-system-scale | Reconstruction from source is free until the system outgrows the reasoning horizon; then every task pays it again. | terms: reasoning-horizon, context-window, long-horizon-task -->
An agent can reconstruct those facts from source. Point it at the code and let it read its way to what
calls what and who owns which service. That works while the relevant system fits inside its reasoning
horizon. Past that point, discovery no longer fits in one window, and each task pays the reconstruction
cost again — a tax charged fresh every time another agent lands with an empty window.

<!-- point: the-first-purpose-of-a-model-is-better-context | The first job of a model is modest: preserve the knowledge the next reasoning episode should not have to rediscover. | terms: thesis-modeling, reasoning-horizon, context-window -->
<!-- index-def: thesis-modeling -->
The first job of a model is therefore simple. Before it verifies anything, before it governs anything, a
model preserves the engineering knowledge an agent should not have to rediscover. Name the services,
modules, APIs, queues, owners, requirements, policies, and tests. Name the relations among them. Now the
agent retrieves the connected slice its task needs instead of reconstructing the estate from raw files.

That is the **Modeling Thesis**, and it is worth stating whole. **The Modeling Thesis.** Externalize
engineering knowledge and intent into explicit, structured representations that give commodity
intelligence a compact world to reason through and give engineers a surface on which to specify, analyze,
and predict the system. As those representations grow richer — from selected context, to connected
knowledge, to invariant-bearing executable models — they make progressively more engineering questions
tractable without returning to raw implementation. *In other words:* don't make every agent rediscover
your architecture from raw code; move the engineering knowledge out of people's heads and into
representations the next engineer, and the next agent, can start from. This chapter earns only the most
modest form of that claim — better context for the intelligence — and the ones after it make the
representation carry intent.

<!-- point: engineering-is-prediction-and-selection-before-construction | Engineering compares alternatives and predicts their trade-offs before building; a model makes the prediction cheap. | terms: engineering, software-engineering, thesis-modeling -->
<!-- index-def: engineering -->
<!-- index-def: software-engineering -->
A word on why this is *engineering* and not merely construction, because the thesis leans on it. **Engineering**
is the discipline of developing operable artifacts to solve problems *and* of analytically weighing the
competing means to a solution, each with its own trade-offs — the responsibility to compare alternatives and
predict their outcomes *before* committing to one. **Software engineering** applies that move to software:
choosing among workable designs by reasoning about the properties each will produce, and choosing before
building rather than discovering them after. Agents sharpen the distinction. When implementation is cheap you
can afford to build more than one — in Fred Brooks's phrase, to build one to throw away — and select among
them; and selection needs a basis for prediction. A model is that basis. This is why the Modeling Thesis
opens the method: the representation is what makes prediction cheap, so it is where an engineering discipline
for commodity intelligence starts.

## Knowledge graphs and engineering models

<!-- section-terms: rag, graphrag, externalized-knowledge, system-knowledge-graph -->

<!-- point: agent-architectures-augment-the-model-with-external-knowledge | Agent architectures already externalize knowledge; a knowledge graph serves as an external reasoning substrate. | terms: rag, graphrag, externalized-knowledge, context-window -->
<!-- index-def: rag -->
<!-- index-def: graphrag -->
Modern agent architectures already externalize knowledge. Two approaches are common: retrieval over
documents — *retrieval-augmented generation*, or RAG [cite: lewis2020rag] — and retrieval over structured
relationships — knowledge graphs and GraphRAG [cite: edge2024graphrag]. In these systems the knowledge
graph acts as an external reasoning substrate: it organizes entities and relationships so an agent can
retrieve context, traverse connections, and infer more than model weights or loose documents allow.

<!-- point: mage-begins-from-the-same-observation-asks-a-different-question | MAGE begins from the same observation, then asks how knowledge should be represented so the environment, not only the agent, can reason over it. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, externalized-knowledge -->
MAGE begins from the same observation — durable engineering knowledge belongs outside the foundation
model — and asks one further question. Once that knowledge is externalized, how should it be represented
so the engineering *environment*, and not only the agent, can reason over it?

<!-- point: the-distinction-is-functional-not-structural | The distinction between a knowledge graph and an engineering model is functional, not structural: dependency, semantics, and authority over one substrate. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
That question sorts representations by function, not by shape. A knowledge graph, an architectural
registry, a state machine, and a typed configuration model may all be graphs internally, so structure
does not tell them apart. What they carry does. A plain knowledge graph helps an agent understand the
system. An engineering model carries more — it also states intent, obligations, and invariants. A
governed engineering model carries more still: the surrounding environment consumes those assertions
deterministically, to validate work, detect drift, and control admission. Dependency, semantics, and
authority over one substrate — three degrees along a single axis, not three kinds of thing. So the
Modeling Thesis does not replace knowledge graphs. It generalizes them, asking how the same externalized
knowledge becomes an artifact that supports validation, traceability, measurement, and, later,
deterministic governance.

## The system knowledge graph

<!-- section-terms: system-knowledge-graph, structured-relationships -->

<!-- point: the-simplest-useful-form-is-a-graph | The simplest useful form of that knowledge is a graph. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, structured-relationships -->
<!-- index-def: system-knowledge-graph -->
The simplest useful form for that knowledge is a graph — the *system knowledge graph*. Its nodes are
engineering entities: services, modules, endpoints, queues, databases, deployment targets, requirements,
tests, owners, policies. Its edges name the relations: calls, depends on, deployed to, owned by,
implements, verified by, governed by. Properties hang from both — a service has a tier, an endpoint an
authentication requirement, a deployment a region.

<!-- point: graph-is-structure-not-storage | Graph names the structure, not the storage technology. | terms: system-knowledge-graph, structured-relationships -->
The graph need not live in a graph database. Assemble it from YAML, repository metadata, infrastructure
declarations, schemas, and registries joined on stable identifiers, and you have the same graph. The word
*graph* names the structure, not the storage. What matters is that the knowledge has stable entities,
explicit relations, and machine-readable joins, so a tool or an agent can traverse it instead of
re-deriving it from raw files.

<!-- point: relevance-follows-relationships | Context engineering expressed structurally: relevance follows relationships. | terms: context-engineering, system-knowledge-graph, structured-relationships -->
<!-- index-def: context-engineering -->
This makes *context engineering* structural. Choosing what to put in the prompt stops being a guess about
which documents might help and becomes a question with a structural answer: which engineering facts and
relations does this task touch? Relevance follows relationships. Start at the service the task names, and
its neighbors come with it — the callers, the queue it consumes, the policy that governs the queue, the
tests that carry the policy. The agent reasons over a connected slice instead of a document dump.

## Industry already lives here

<!-- section-terms: knowledge-representation, externalized-knowledge, thesis-modeling -->

<!-- point: knowledge-rep-is-the-first-rung | Externalized knowledge representation is the most basic move of the Modeling Thesis, and most of industry already stands on it. | terms: knowledge-representation, thesis-modeling, externalized-knowledge -->
<!-- index-def: knowledge-representation -->
<!-- index-def: externalized-knowledge -->
Externalizing knowledge this way is the most basic move of the Modeling Thesis, and much of current
practice already stands on it. Service catalogs expose components and ownership. Dependency graphs expose
what calls what. Infrastructure-as-code exposes placement. Policy corpora expose obligations. Developer
portals join these surfaces for the engineers who read them and, increasingly, for the agents that reason
over them. This is *knowledge representation*: engineering knowledge moved out of a person's head, or an
agent's transient context, into an artifact the next one retrieves rather than reconstructs.

<!-- point: knowledge-rep-universal-graph-common-executable-not | Knowledge representation is universal in the corpus; graph-shaped representation common; invariant-bearing models not. | terms: knowledge-representation, system-knowledge-graph, thesis-modeling -->
State the reach of that claim carefully, because the evidence later in this book is precise about it.
Knowledge representation is universal in the corpus; graph-shaped system representation is common;
invariant-bearing executable models are not. The organizations studied differ in how strongly they
structure the knowledge, yet all of them externalize facts an agent would otherwise reconstruct. Zenseact
makes the motive plain: its routing machinery selects the task-relevant expertise and loads only that,
rather than pouring the whole corpus into the window.

<!-- worked-examples: externalized-knowledge -->
### Example — Spotify
Spotify externalized its service estate before its agents arrived. The Backstage catalog names every
service, its owners, its dependencies, and its endpoints, each with a stable identity a tool can resolve —
the estate written down as a retrievable graph rather than carried in anyone's head. An agent that lands
on a migration reads the slice the task touches instead of crawling thousands of repositories.

### Example — Cloudflare
Cloudflare externalized its standards, not its system, and that is the instructive limit. It turned a
sprawl of RFCs into a curated corpus where every requirement carries a stable slug that survives the
edits around it, so an agent retrieves the obligation governing a change without its author present. The
obligations are represented far more strongly than the system they govern, which stays mostly unmodeled.

### Example — Shopify
Shopify externalizes knowledge more softly. Every public agent session becomes a searchable artifact the
company mines into skills, conventions, and defaults, so one engineer's experience is retrieved by the
next agent rather than lost when the window closes. The representation is informal — prose skills loaded
on demand, not a structured model — externalization that is real, retrievable, and deliberately soft.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble is one software implementation of the pattern. It assembles a system knowledge graph from
registries, schemas, and YAML joined on stable identifiers, so an agent retrieves the connected slice a
task needs instead of re-reading the repository each time. It reaches further later; here it does only
what the others do — move the system's facts out of the window and onto disk where the next agent finds
them.

<!-- takeaway -->
Externalize engineering knowledge so the next reasoner retrieves it instead of reconstructing it.
Structure only as far as the engineering question requires.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

<!-- point: legibility-is-only-the-first-rung | A model first earns its keep by preventing repeated reconstruction; making it legible is only the beginning. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, structured-relationships -->
A model first earns its keep by preventing repeated reconstruction. But legibility is where it starts,
not where it ends. A graph that makes the system legible tells an agent what to think about; it does not
yet tell the environment what must be true. Once the knowledge has explicit structure, the same
representation can carry more than context — it can carry engineering intent a machine is able to check.
