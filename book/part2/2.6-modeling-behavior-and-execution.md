# Modeling Behavior and Measurement

<!-- point: behavior-and-execution-put-time-and-place-back | The structural models hold the system still; this chapter puts time and place back. -->
The structural models of the last chapter hold the system still. They name the parts, the
dependencies, the owners — and they leave time out. A design that is correct read part by part can
still fail once the parts run: two workers race on one record, an inverted lock ordering hangs the
build, a service correct in every test ships to the wrong tier and pages a user at 3 a.m. Those are
not structural bugs. They live in *behavior* — what runs when, in what order, on which box.

<!-- point: the-six-behavioral-questions | Six engineering questions this chapter answers. | terms: concurrency, placement -->
This chapter builds the models that make behavior analyzable, then adds the two models that keep the
running system honest about itself — what it measures, and what it records that it did. Each answers
a question raw code makes you reconstruct in your head:

- **What states can the system enter, and which transitions are legal?**
- **What can run concurrently — and what must stay true across every interleaving?**
- **What resources constrain execution?**
- **Where does each part run?**
- **Which deployment decisions are correctness decisions, not configuration trivia?**
- **What does the system promise about time and cost — and does its account of what it did stay true?**

A behavioral model turns each into a checked fact. The rest of the chapter takes them in that order,
one representative model at a time, and closes on the lesson the placement models share: correctness
dependencies belong in the graph; host-specific resource limits belong in a profile.

## Reading behavior formally

<!-- point: an-invariant-over-order-needs-a-state-machine-and-two-operators | Behavior invariants are predicates over an order of events; a state machine and two operators express them. | terms: state-machine, temporal-logic, safety-property, liveness-property, bounded-model-checking -->
The models below share a small formal vocabulary. You need it at the level required to *use* it, not
a survey of the field. One inset covers that level; the full primer, with the operators these models
do not use, is in the appendix. [appendix: appendix-g-ltl-primer]

> ### Inset — Reading behavior formally {#inset-behavior-formal}
>
> **A state machine** is a finite set of states and the legal transitions between them. A leased job is
> *queued*, *leased*, *running*, or *reclaimed*; a worker taking the lease moves *queued → leased*, a
> lease expiring moves *leased → queued*. Draw it and the illegal edges show themselves — a path back to
> *running* from *reclaimed* is the double-execution bug, visible before the code runs. The state machine
> is the spine a behavior invariant attaches to.
>
> **An invariant over behavior is a predicate over an order of events**, not over one state. Two shapes
> cover almost all of them, and each names one operator worth memorizing:
>
> - **Safety — □P ("always P").** A bad thing never happens. *"A leased job always has exactly one owner."*
>   You settle it by searching the reachable states for any that violate P.
> - **Liveness — P ↝ Q ("P leads-to Q").** A good thing eventually happens. *"A preempted job eventually
>   re-runs"; "a completed worktree eventually tombstones."* No single state settles it — it is a claim
>   about the *sequence*, so it needs a checker that reasons about cyclic runs.
>
> Declaring the shape is not decoration: it **routes the checker**. Route a liveness property to a safety
> runtime and the runtime structurally cannot see the violation — it reports green. So the shape is checked
> too (below).
>
> **Bounded model checking** settles a safety property the honest way a test cannot. A test *samples* the
> schedules you thought of; a distributed race hides in the one you did not. Model checking *exhaustively
> walks* every interleaving out to a fixed depth *k*, and either shows no reachable state violates the
> predicate or hands back the exact counterexample trace. The limit is the bound: a bug needing *k+1* steps,
> or one the model abstracts away, is out of scope — the price of a decidable check.
>
> *That is the whole vocabulary these models use: a state machine, an invariant as □P or P ↝ Q, and an
> exhaustive walk to depth k. The appendix primer adds the operators (◇, Until) and edge cases this Part
> does not.*

## A remediation job's lifecycle

<!-- section-terms: state-machine, safety-property -->

> #### Job lifecycle · Behavioral
>
> - **Engineering question** — Can a job move only through valid states, and are its terminal states truly terminal?
> - **Model** — a state machine: the states a job occupies and the table of legal transitions.
> - **Property** — illegal transitions are unreachable; a failure surfaces as a terminal state, and it never silently loops back into processing.
> - **Quality attribute** — correctness, reliability.

<!-- point: the-job-lifecycle-is-the-representative-behavioral-model | A remediation job's lifecycle — its states, legal transitions, and terminal states — is the chapter's representative behavioral model. | terms: state-machine, safety-property -->
The inset drew a leased job in miniature. DocAble's own job is the full version, and it answers the
first question the chapter promised: *can a job move only through valid states, and are its terminal
states truly terminal?*

A long-running job passes through many stages, and each stage sets a status field. When the status is
a bare string any code can overwrite, the lifecycle lives only in the programmers' heads. A job jumps
from *done* back to *processing*. A failed job gets picked up again. The bugs are not in any one stage;
they live in the transitions nobody wrote down. Read off the raw code, the answer is scattered across a
dispatcher, a worker, and a merge handler, each holding one arc of a job's life.

The state machine gathers it into one place. A job enters **queued**; a dispatcher **claims** it; the
claimed job is **planned and split** into chunks — the unit of work a single worker remediates. It then
**waits** while its chunks run, **merges** their results, **finalizes** the output, and reaches
**completed**. That is the whole happy path. The production machine distinguishes more intermediate
states; the teaching view collapses the three planning states into one node. One edge matters as much
as any of them: every active state can also move to **failed**, and a failed job does not loop back to
try again — it lands in a terminal state and surfaces to the user. [ref:fig-job-lifecycle-sm] draws the states
and that one-way edge into failure.

<!-- label: fig-job-lifecycle-sm -->
<!-- figure: assets/fig-job-lifecycle-sm.svg | The remediation job lifecycle; every active state also routes to a terminal FAILED with no edge back into processing — failure is a dead end, not a detour. -->

<!-- point: the-transition-table-makes-illegal-moves-unreachable | The transition table makes an illegal move unreachable in code, and terminal states have no edge back into processing. | terms: state-machine, safety-property -->
Two properties are worth stating precisely, because they are what the model buys:

- **Illegal moves cannot happen.** *Completed → processing* is not in the table, so the code cannot
  express it. One transition function is the only way to change state, and it refuses — loudly — any edge
  the table does not list.
- **Failure is a dead end, not a detour.** A failed job surfaces; it does not quietly re-enter the
  pipeline. That single decision — *failure never requeues* — is what breaks the retry storm Part V tells
  the story of.

Both facts rest on the same shape: a declared transition table plus one guarded operator that is the
only writer of state. The one illustrative invariant carries it:

<!-- label: job-lifecycle-invariants -->
<!-- table: One invariant of the job-lifecycle state machine — an illegal transition cannot be reached and a terminal state does not re-enter processing. [short: Job-lifecycle state machine — illustrative invariant] -->
| Invariant | Temporal shape | How it is checked |
|---|---|---|
| Illegal transitions are unreachable, and a terminal state never re-enters processing | *□P* (safety) | The machine exposes one operator to change state, and it raises on any edge absent from the declared transition table — so an illegal move cannot be reached in code. Terminal states carry no outgoing edge into processing. A build-time drift check reconciles the declared table against the running code, so the two cannot silently part. |

<!-- point: the-claim-transition-is-won-by-exactly-one-worker | The claim transition is won by exactly one worker, which sets up ownership as the next specialization. | terms: state-machine, concurrency -->
The model also exposes a boundary between workflow and ownership. Claiming work is not just another
status string. When the planner claims a job, a database compare-and-set moves it from queued to
remediating *only if it is still queued*; a second consumer racing for the same job matches zero rows
and does nothing. That compare-and-set is where the next model begins.

<!-- point: a-behavioral-model-makes-the-lifecycle-a-first-class-object | A behavioral model makes the lifecycle a first-class object: states as an enum, transitions as a table, one guarded function the single writer. | terms: state-machine -->
A behavioral model makes the lifecycle a first-class object instead of an emergent property of
scattered assignments. Write the states as an enum, the transitions as a table, and route every change
through one guarded function. Now *the system is in a legal state* is something you can assert, and
*this transition is impossible* is something the runtime holds for you. The move that pays: name the
states explicitly, and make the transition table the single writer.

<!-- point: the-discrete-job-lifecycle-machine-is-where-the-corpus-is-silent | The discrete job-lifecycle state machine is the behavioral model the surveyed industrial corpus rarely records. | terms: state-machine -->
State machines themselves are everywhere in computing — TCP guards its illegal transitions, an order
that cannot move from *shipped* back to *cart* enforces a terminal state, and workflow engines run on
explicit transition tables. But the discrete job-lifecycle machine, drawn and *governed* as a model,
is where the industrial cases we surveyed go quiet. The software teams document structure, policy, and
measurement; none records a discrete lifecycle of legal transitions like this one. The nearest
neighbor, Siemens, builds behavioral models heavily — but as continuous physics: finite-element models
and executable digital twins that *predict* how a physical system responds, sitting upstream of the
software rather than bounding a job's lifecycle. Stretching that into a homolog would misread it. The
silence scopes the claim rather than crowns it: the discrete-behavior model is one MAGE leans on and
the corpus rarely writes down.

> **Hands to Alignment** — a job moves only through legal transitions, and a terminal state never
> re-enters processing. *Part III turns that statement into an enforced boundary using the four
> mechanism roles.*

## Ownership over the lifecycle, a specialization

<!-- section-terms: concurrency, single-writer-registry -->

<!-- point: job-ownership-is-a-specialization-over-the-lifecycle | Job ownership specializes the behavioral and structural views — an owner and a lease over the job lifecycle and the queue structures — and is not a sixth class. | terms: concurrency, single-writer-registry -->
The state machine says what states a job may occupy. It does not say who holds a job while it sits in
one, or how many may hold it at once. Answer that and the concurrency questions this chapter opened
with come into focus — races, dropped work, torn writes — scoped tightly to one running job.

This is **a specialization of the behavioral and structural views**, not a sixth class of model. Add an
*owner* and a *lease* to the behavioral machine, add the queue the owner claims work from to the
structural picture, and you have it. The five classes stay five; the specialization sits beneath two of
them.

> #### Job ownership · Behavioral / Structural specialization
>
> - **Engineering question** — Can two workers ever own the same chunk of work at once, and what happens to a chunk when its owner dies?
> - **Model** — the behavioral machine with an *owner* and a *lease* added, over the queue the owner claims from.
> - **Property** — at most one active owner per chunk; a crash before acknowledgement re-delivers the work rather than dropping it; failure never requeues — the one sanctioned requeue is a planned preemption.
> - **Quality attribute** — safety, consistency, reliability.

<!-- point: two-workers-doing-it-twice-or-a-dead-owner-are-the-two-ugly-questions | Two workers doing the same chunk twice, and a dead owner losing work, are the two failures ownership must answer. | terms: concurrency -->
The moment work is claimed by one of many workers, two ugly questions appear. Can two workers grab the
same piece at once and do it twice? And if a worker dies mid-task, is the work lost, or does someone
notice and re-drive it? Do it twice and you double-charge the customer and corrupt the merge. Lose it
and the job hangs forever. Both stay invisible until they aren't.

<!-- point: ownership-rests-on-a-lease-and-a-compare-and-set | Ownership of a chunk rests on a lease plus a compare-and-set claim. | terms: concurrency, single-writer-registry -->
Ownership layers onto the job machine — which is why we present it as a specialization rather than a new
class. A chunk is claimed by exactly one worker. The claim is a compare-and-set against the database:
set this chunk to *being worked on*, but only if it is still *queued*. Exactly one claimant wins the
row; a duplicate delivery matches zero rows and quietly stops, having done nothing twice. On top of the
claim sits a lease: a claimed task is invisible to other workers until the owner acknowledges it or the
lease times out. If the owner crashes before acknowledging, the lease lapses and the task comes back. [ref:fig-lease-vs-cas] separates the two — the lease that
recovers from a crash and the claim that grants the one permission to act.

<!-- label: fig-lease-vs-cas -->
<!-- figure: assets/fig-lease-vs-cas.svg | Redelivery and permission-to-perform are different things; the lease says "I currently hold this delivery", the compare-and-set says "I am the one consumer allowed the side effect" — exactly-once rests on the claim, not the lease. -->

<!-- point: exactly-once-rests-on-the-claim-not-the-lease | The lease provides crash recovery; the compare-and-set provides the exactly-once correctness net. | terms: concurrency -->
Here is the subtle, load-bearing point. The lease alone does *not* give you exactly-once effects. A
worker can be slow rather than dead: it finishes the real work, then gets its task redelivered because
it took too long. What saves you is the compare-and-set — the redelivered claim loses the race and
acknowledges without repeating the side effect. **Exactly-once rests on the claim, not the lease.** The
lease provides crash recovery; the compare-and-set provides the correctness net.

<!-- point: crash-before-ack-redelivers-failure-never-requeues-except-preemption | Crash-before-ack redelivers; failure routes to terminal, never requeues — except the one sanctioned preemption path. | terms: concurrency -->
And there is exactly one sanctioned way for work to re-enter the queue: a planned preemption, when the
platform recycles a container that had *not* failed. The in-flight work re-enters ahead of fresh work
of its size, its place preserved. Timeout and crash do not requeue; they surface as failed. That
distinction is narrow and deliberate, and something watches to keep it narrow.

The production model runs two dispatch planes and eight queue structures. The teaching view keeps one
work queue, one in-flight lease, one claim, and the two edges that matter: *crash-before-ack
re-delivers*, and *failure goes to failed, never back to the queue — except preemption.*

<!-- point: put-a-correctness-net-under-the-lease | Represent single ownership explicitly and put an atomic claim under the lease; trust the claim for correctness, the lease for liveness. | terms: concurrency -->
Ownership is a lease plus a claim over a lifecycle. When many actors draw from a shared pool of work,
represent single ownership explicitly, and put a correctness net under the lease — an atomic claim a
duplicate loses. The move: do not trust the lease for correctness; trust the atomic state change the
lease is wrapped around.

<!-- point: the-distributed-race-is-common-knowledge-and-rarely-governed | Distributed locks and idempotency keys are the same shape; the surveyed corpus rarely governs it as a checked model. | terms: concurrency -->
You will recognize the shape elsewhere: distributed locks, leader election, exactly-once processing.
Message brokers offer at-least-once delivery and leave exactly-once *effects* to you; the idempotency
key on a payment API is the same compare-and-set trick under another name. Across the industrial cases
we surveyed, though, this distributed race is rarely written down as a *governed* model — the pattern is
common knowledge and seldom stated as a checked obligation.

Ownership bounds who holds a single job in flight. The models that follow widen the question — from one
owned job to what must stay true across every schedule of the machinery around it.

> **Hands to Alignment** — at most one active owner per chunk; a crash before acknowledgement
> redelivers the work; failure never requeues except a planned preemption. *Part III turns that
> statement into an enforced boundary using the four mechanism roles.*

## What must hold across every interleaving

<!-- point: concurrency-answers-are-a-declared-checked-contract | Concurrency is where correct-in-isolation designs break; the answer is a declared, checked contract, not a thought experiment about interleavings. | terms: synchronization-model, mediator-registry, single-writer-registry, concurrency -->
<!-- index-def: synchronization-model -->
*What runs at once, and what stays true across every schedule?* Concurrency is where a
correct-in-isolation design becomes a source of races, deadlocks, and torn writes, and the answer
cannot be a thought experiment about interleavings. It has to be a declared, checked contract. Three
models carry it; each compresses to its property here, with the full card in the appendix.

> **Synchronization model · Behavioral** *(compressed here; full card in the appendix)*
>
> A typed registry of every OS-level lock: which shared resource each guards, and the required
> acquisition ordering between them. Declaring the ordering turns deadlock-avoidance into a graph
> check — an ordering lint walks each code path's acquisition sequence against the declared graph, and
> a held lock reaching for one that must precede it is a finding, so the deadlock is caught before it
> can hang.
> *Property: no acquisition inverts a declared ordering.*
> [appendix: appendix-g-synchronization-reference] · full card: [synchronization model](appendix-b-synchronization-model.html)

<!-- index-def: mediator-registry -->
<!-- index-def: single-writer-registry -->
> **Two ways to make a concurrent write safe** *(compressed here; full cards in the appendix)*
>
> Above the raw locks sit two registries whose distinction is worth naming, because confusing them is
> how a corruption bug hides inside a load limit.
>
> - **The mediator registry answers *how many*.** It declares which heavy or shared-resource
>   invocations run through a serializer and at what cap; its failure class is **resource contention**.
> - **The single-writer registry answers *who*.** It declares that one function, and only one, may
>   write a given piece of shared state; its failure class is a **torn or interleaved write** — which a
>   concurrency cap of two would happily permit, because two mediated processes racing on one record is
>   still within cap.
>
> **Concurrency control bounds contention; the single-writer registry bounds ownership.** This one cost
> me a day of chasing a corrupted registry file before I saw that two "safely rationed" writers were the
> whole problem: the semaphore was doing its job and guarding nothing that mattered.
> [appendix: appendix-g-mediator-registry-reference] [appendix: appendix-g-single-writer-registry-reference] · full card: [concurrency contracts](appendix-b-concurrency-contracts.html)

<!-- index-def: formal-invariant-verification -->
> **Formal invariant verification · trunk machinery** *(compressed here; full card in the appendix)*
>
> All three concurrency models hang their invariant tables on one shared method — trunk machinery, not
> a model of the running system. An invariant's temporal shape routes its exhaustive checker: a safety
> form (□P) to a state-space search, a liveness form (P ↝ Q) to a temporal model checker. Declare the
> shape wrong and the safety runtime cannot see the violation and reports green, so a lint checks the
> declared shape against the checker that ran — the shape is *consumed*, and a mis-routed invariant is
> a build failure instead of a false all-clear.
> [appendix: appendix-g-invariant-dag-reference] · full card: [formal invariant verification](appendix-b-formal-invariant-verification.html)

## Where each part runs

<!-- point: the-deployment-topology-model-declares-where-each-service-runs | The deployment-topology model declares where each service runs, and parity holds it to reality. | terms: deployment-topology-model, placement, deployment-parity -->
<!-- index-def: deployment-topology-model -->
*Where does each part run, and which of those placements is a correctness decision?* A functionally
correct system can still be mis-deployed. Placement is not a detail an agent discovers after the fact
in scattered deployment configuration — it is a fact the fleet should read before it deploys.

The **deployment-topology model** makes it one: a typed statement of where each service runs — its
name, its owning layer, its tier class. From that, the deploy scripts reason about a declared topology
instead of scattered constants, and a parity check holds the model to reality in *both* directions.
Neither direction is optional. A parity set-diff run model ⊆ reality catches a tier that drifted in the
code; run reality ⊆ model it catches a service that shipped with no model row; either mismatch fails
the build. The illustrative invariant — *every declared service deploys at the tier the model names,
and every deployed service appears in the model* — is a □P safety property, and the record's schema and
derivation direction are in the appendix. [appendix: appendix-g-deployment-topology-reference] · full
card: [deployment and tier topology model](appendix-b-deployment-topology-model.html)

## Correctness in the graph, resource limits in a profile

<!-- point: correctness-deps-in-the-graph-host-limits-in-a-profile | Correctness dependencies stay in the graph; host-specific resource limits live in a scheduler profile. | terms: invariant-dag-execution-policy, edge-intent, per-host-load-profile -->
<!-- index-def: invariant-dag-execution-policy -->
<!-- slogan: profile-edit-not-graph-edit -->
The placement models raise one more question — *what resources constrain execution?* — and answering
it well turns on a single modeling principle, the most portable idea in this chapter: **correctness
dependencies stay in the graph; host-specific resource limits live in a scheduler profile.**

What must be true on *every* host — B is wrong without A — belongs in the deploy graph. How *hard a
particular box may be pushed* — how many steps run at once, whether a cost gate is worth honoring —
belongs in that host's profile, read by a scheduler. Move the stress between hosts and you edit the
profile, never the graph: **profile edit, not graph edit.**

The **invariant-DAG execution policy** embodies it. Each deploy-graph edge carries a typed intent:
`CORRECTNESS` (B is wrong without A; honored on every host) and `COST_GATE` (a cheap check gating an
expensive step; honored unless the budget is unbounded) stay in the graph, host-identical. The `LOAD`
intent — B contends with A for a scarce box — is *banned from the graph* and migrated to the scheduler,
which reads a per-host profile of a concurrency ceiling and a budget and emits the execution plan. The
mapping is a pure function of the profile, small enough to read as a table:

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — inline host-profile → plan mapping strip making the scheduler function scannable, a reference not a numbered cross-referenced float -->
| Host profile | Ceiling | Budget | Emitted plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic staging | none (unbounded) | ∞ (unbounded) | fan the whole ready wave out; relax cost gates |
| Metered prod | low finite | finite | small concurrency; honor cost gates |
| Scarce local box | 1 | finite | serialize; honor cost gates |

Moving stress between hosts edits one row of this table. The one illustrative invariant keeps the
graph portable:

<!-- label: host-stress-invariants -->
<!-- table: One invariant of the invariant-DAG execution policy — a load edge in the deploy graph is banned. [short: Invariant-DAG execution policy — illustrative invariant] -->
| Invariant | Temporal shape | How it is checked |
|---|---|---|
| No deploy edge carries the `LOAD` intent | *□P* (safety) | A load-edge lint reads the graph's resident intent set; a `LOAD` edge — the thing that would fork the graph per host — is a finding. Load rationing is a semaphore in the scheduler, never a graph edge. |

The edge-intent schema, the profile record, the scheduler code, and the full invariant set are in the
appendix; the model is also walked end to end in the next chapter's join example. [appendix: appendix-g-invariant-dag-reference]

## One honest gap

<!-- point: model-only-where-a-failure-lives-so-the-latency-slice-stays-empty | Model only where a failure lives; the pure-latency slice stayed empty until an incident filled it. | terms: model-only-where-a-failure-lives, pure-latency, honest-gap -->
<!-- index-def: model-only-where-a-failure-lives -->
That policy models the *load-and-cost* half of execution. The *pure-latency* half — request latency,
cache-hit ratios, data-movement cost — has no dedicated model, and the honest move is to say so rather
than invent one. The discipline behind the gap is **model only where a failure lives**: a model built
with no decision or named risk to exercise it rots before anyone checks it. The gap has drawn blood
once — scaling services to zero cut idle cost the policy welcomed but the latency half could not weigh,
and the cold-start incident in [Operating MAGE](4.5-operating-mage.html) is exactly this gap surfacing: a warm
per-request floor a fleet-average number hid, run down to a re-architecture that cut it 4,057 → 109 ms.
The gap stays honest; this is what it costs to leave open.

## Computing a migration's blast radius

<!-- index-def: control-substrate-dependency -->
> **Substrate-dependency model · Structural** *(compressed here; full card in the appendix)*
>
> One last behavioral question before an agent changes the ground everything runs on: *which mechanisms
> assume the substrate I am about to replace?* Each governance mechanism declares, as typed metadata,
> the substrate assumption it bakes in — bound to one substrate, substrate-aware, or substrate-agnostic.
> Joining those declarations against the topology model on a migration target turns a hopeful grep into
> a *computed* blast radius; a declaration lint requires the stance, so the one mechanism whose
> assumption is implicit cannot hide from the join. Worked once on the plane the fleet actually
> migrated: retiring a poll plane for a push plane, the join named every mechanism that baked in
> "workers pull from a shared sorted set" — the queue-depth metric, the stale-job sweep, the
> cancellation cleanup, the priority-preservation requeue — before the change landed.
> *Property: a migration's blast radius is computed from declared stances, not grepped for by hand.*

## What the system promises about time and cost

<!-- section-terms: operational-envelope-model, admission-gate, resource-ceiling -->

<!-- point: the-operational-envelope-model-states-what-the-running-system-must-keep-bounded | The operational-envelope model states the quantities the running system must keep within declared bounds. | terms: operational-envelope-model, resource-ceiling -->
<!-- index-def: operational-envelope-model -->
<!-- index-def: resource-ceiling -->
The structural models say what the system is made of. The behavioral models say what it does when it
runs. Neither says what it *costs* to run, how fast it answers, or whether the running system is still
inside those bounds today. That is a measured question, and it needs its own model.

Be clear about which measured question. The execution policy earlier in this chapter *rations* load —
it decides how hard a given box may be pushed, and moves that decision to a scheduler profile. This
model asks something the scheduler does not: **is the system staying inside the throughput, latency,
and cost bounds it promised?** One decides a plan; the other checks a fact.

A system that is never measured makes no promise it can keep. It is fast until it is slow, cheap until
a runaway job costs a fortune, healthy until the queue backs up. Worse, the numbers that would explain
a bad day were never recorded, so every incident is diagnosed from scratch. A configuration constant
does not help here: a wall-clock kill at ten minutes tells you *when* a process will be stopped. It
does not tell you whether the expected worst case is *approaching* ten minutes.

> #### Operational-envelope model · Measurement
>
> - **Engineering question** — What does the system promise about latency, cost, and throughput — and is it inside those promises now?
> - **Model** — a typed statement of the measured quantities that must stay bounded: end-to-end latency, per-job wall-clock, per-call output cost, and queue depth by work-size class, each paired with the ceiling it may not cross.
> - **Property** — every admitted unit of work runs within its declared resource ceilings; a breach stops the work rather than shipping a degraded result.
> - **Quality attribute** — performance, scalability, efficiency.

<!-- point: the-envelope-is-a-few-measured-quantities-each-paired-with-a-ceiling | The operational envelope is a small set of measured quantities, each paired with the ceiling it may not cross. | terms: operational-envelope-model, resource-ceiling -->
The measured envelope is itself modeled, not left to intuition. A small set of quantities carries the
story: the warm end-to-end latency, tracked as measured rows the deploy smoke appends — around
twenty-four seconds; a per-job wall-clock ceiling near ten minutes; a hard cap on how many tokens a
single generation call may spend; a size classification of each incoming document; and a credit balance
— not a per-file page cap — that throttles admission. Each is a promise; each has a bound. [ref:fig-measurement-envelope] places the observed warm path
against the predicted worst case and the budget the system promises to hold.

What the model lets you establish is that the running system does not silently leave the envelope. It
earns that through a sensor and a gate.

- **The sensor is instrumentation.** The pipeline emits phase timings, per-segment timers, server-clock
  start/finish pairs, and worker heartbeats; a deploy-time budget smoke drives a fixture through the
  whole pipeline and appends the measured end-to-end row. The promised numbers are compared against
  measured ones, not hoped for.
- **The gate is admission.** Before work begins, an **admission gate** sizes the job, classifies it, and
  debits the account's credit balance; a job whose account cannot cover it is refused at the door rather
  than started and abandoned mid-flight. A second gate refuses a different way: in the staging
  environment, a fidelity check fails an individual repair pass rather than shipping a regression — it
  emits a signal and exits with a distinct code the surrounding system reads as *this pass did harm; stop*.

One quantity, honestly, has *no* production gate. The per-chunk cost model exists. It is instrumented.
It can predict when a chunk's worst-case time would breach the job ceiling. But in production it only
*reports* — it does not split the work or refuse it. The single hard cost cap in the system lives in an
offline benchmark harness, not on the live path. We keep that gap in view rather than paper over it. It
is a real *modeled and sensed, but not yet governed* case, and it teaches something the tidy version
would hide: **a model does not automatically earn blocking authority. Earning it is a later, separate
decision.** Part III returns to exactly this.

<!-- point: model-the-envelope-where-a-breached-bound-must-stop-the-system-not-degrade-it | Model the operational envelope wherever a breached bound must stop the system rather than let it degrade. | terms: operational-envelope-model, resource-ceiling -->
A measurement model names the quantities you promise to hold and the bound on each. Its authority is a
gate that reads the sensed quantity and refuses to advance when the bound would break. The move: decide
what the measurement must *drive* — an admission decision, a stop decision — and sense one level below
the surface number so the data can act, not merely report.

<!-- point: operational-envelope-measurement-is-not-drift-measurement | Operational-envelope measurement asks if the running system is inside its bounds; drift measurement asks if a model still matches reality. | terms: operational-envelope-model, model-reality-drift -->
Two things in this Part are called *measurement*, and they answer different questions. This model
measures the **running system** against its resource bounds — is it fast enough, cheap enough, inside
the envelope now? The drift concept in [Making Models Executable](2.3-the-executable-zoo.html) measures
a **model against reality** — has the map drifted from the territory it claims to describe? One watches
the system; the other watches the model. Keep them apart.

This is the service-level-objective, capacity-model, and admission-control tradition. The mature ones
publish an error budget and act on it; the immature ones graph the number and never wire it to a
decision — exactly the rung DocAble's cost cap still sits on. Three teams show the same move realized on
materially different resources:

<!-- worked-examples: automatic-admission -->
### Example — Spotify
Spotify gates a merge on measured evidence. An automated merger admits a change only once its verifiers
and monitoring have generated the evidence that it is safe, so the measurement is the admission
criterion: the system reads a signal about the change and refuses to advance until the signal clears.
The measured quantity is verification evidence; the ceiling is "not yet proven safe"; the gate is the
merge itself. Same function as the envelope model — measure, then refuse to advance on a breach —
realized as an evidence-gated merge.

### Example — Zenseact
Zenseact treats context as a scarce resource and rations it against a measured budget. A deterministic
router measures how much context a task needs and holds it under a ceiling, cutting what each step
carries by a large, *measured* fraction rather than a guessed one. The measured quantity is context
size; the ceiling is the routing budget; the gate is the router that will not hand a step more than its
share. A different resource, a different mechanism — but the same move: measure the envelope, ration
against the bound.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble measures a latency-cost-throughput envelope and gates against it in two places. An admission
gate refuses a job whose account cannot cover its measured size before any work starts; an in-pass
fidelity gate fails a remediation that regresses quality rather than shipping it. The measured
quantities are wall-clock, cost, and queue depth by size class; the ceilings are the job budget and the
account balance; the gates are admission and per-pass fidelity. Same function again, realized as a
resource-and-quality envelope enforced at the door and at each pass.

Measuring a resource envelope and refusing to advance on a breach is one engineering move; an
evidence-gated merge, a rationed context budget, and a latency-cost admission gate are three
realizations of it — strong evidence the abstraction is real, not one team's habit.

## Keeping the explanation true to the change

Documentation is easy to dismiss as prose about the real system. That view becomes untenable when the
documentation is *evidence*. A system that changes a customer's artifact returns not only the
remediated document but a record of what it changed — and if implementation and explanation can drift
independently, the record cannot support an audit.

Measurement and Documentation answer kindred questions: both are the system keeping an account of
itself — one the quantities it must hold, the other the actions it took. A remediation inserts a
description, retags a heading, repairs a reading order — then hands back an account of the work. Those
are two claims, and they can disagree. When the explanation is written by hand, separately from the
code that does the work, the two drift: the account omits half of what happened, or names a change the
code never made. And when something goes wrong, "which step made this change?" becomes a multi-hop hunt
across files — each hop cheap, the whole chain expensive.

> #### Provenance-and-changelog model · Documentation
>
> - **Engineering question** — Does the human-facing explanation of what changed stay in sync with what the code actually did?
> - **Model** — a provenance model: every mutation stamped, every inserted artifact recorded, and the customer-facing changelog *derived* from those stamps rather than written by hand.
> - **Property** — every change is stamped, every inserted artifact is recorded and survives to the output, and the changelog is a loss-free projection of the stamps — so what the customer is told changed equals what the code did.
> - **Quality attribute** — understandability, maintainability, auditability.

The architecture reverses the usual relationship: rather than describe the work afterward, the code
records it as it works, and the explanation is a *view* of that record. Three moves, in order:

- **Mutate, then stamp.** Every verb that changes the document writes an entry — which verb, in which
  pass, visible or invisible — into a provenance registry embedded in the file. A change that ships
  without its stamp is the failure this move exists to prevent.
- **Insert, then record.** Every artifact the tool adds is recorded with its locator and its placement,
  and it carries a reserved name-prefix so a later reader — and the validator — can tell an inserted
  artifact from one the author wrote.
- **Derive, don't author.** The changelog the customer reads is projected from the registry, one row per
  stamped entry, loss-free. An empty registry yields an empty changelog — by construction, the document
  tells the truth about itself.

What the model buys is a single equality: what the customer is told changed equals what the code did.
It earns that because the registry the code writes as it works is the *only* source the changelog
reads. There is no second, hand-kept account to drift, so **provenance collapses a four-hop forensic
reconstruction into a registry lookup.** [ref:provenance-derivation] draws that derivation, from a
mutation's stamp to the changelog the customer reads.

<!-- label: provenance-derivation -->
<!-- figure: assets/provenance-derivation.svg | Provenance derivation — the explanation is derived, not authored: a mutation writes a stamp, every stamp lands in one provenance registry in the document, and the registry fans out to a validation pass and the customer-facing changelog (a loss-free projection, one row per entry). -->

**Generalize.** The class is **Documentation**, a model like the others in this Part, not a category
apart: the explanation is a *model of the action*, and like any model it can drift from what it
represents. The move is useful wherever a system acts on a user's artifact and must later explain or
reverse what it did — derive the explanation from a record the actor writes *as it acts*, force the
emission with a check, and generate the human-facing artifact from the record. Audit logs,
data-pipeline provenance, and regulatory audit trails are all this model; what separates the real ones
from theater is whether emitting the record is mandatory.
