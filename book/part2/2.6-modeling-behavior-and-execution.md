# Modeling Behavior and Execution

<!-- point: behavior-and-execution-put-time-and-place-back | The structural models hold the system still; this chapter puts time and place back. | terms: process-view, physical-view -->
<!-- index-def: process-view -->
The structural models of the last chapter hold the system still. They name the parts, the
dependencies, the owners — and they leave time out. A design that is correct read part by part can
still fail once the parts run: two workers race on one record, an inverted lock ordering hangs the
build, a service correct in every test ships to the wrong tier and pages a user at 3 a.m. Those are
not structural bugs. They live in *behavior* — what runs when, in what order, on which box.

<!-- point: the-six-behavioral-questions | Six engineering questions this chapter answers. | terms: concurrency, placement -->
This chapter builds the models that make behavior analyzable. Each answers a question raw code makes
you reconstruct in your head:

- **What states can the system enter, and which transitions are legal?**
- **What can run concurrently — and what must stay true across every interleaving?**
- **What resources constrain execution?**
- **Where does each part run?**
- **Which deployment decisions are correctness decisions, not configuration trivia?**

A behavioral model turns each into a checked fact. The rest of the chapter takes them in that order,
one representative model at a time, and closes on the lesson the placement models share: correctness
dependencies belong in the graph; host-specific resource limits belong in a profile.

## Reading behavior formally

<!-- point: an-invariant-over-order-needs-a-state-machine-and-two-operators | Behavior invariants are predicates over an order of events; a state machine and two operators express them. | terms: state-machine, temporal-logic, safety-property, liveness-property, bounded-model-checking -->
The models below share a small formal vocabulary. You need it at the level required to *use* it, not
a survey of the field. One inset covers that level; the full primer, with the operators these models
do not use, is in the appendix. [appendix: appendix-g-ltl-primer]

<!-- index-def: state-machine -->
<!-- index-def: temporal-logic -->
<!-- index-def: safety-property -->
<!-- index-def: liveness-property -->
<!-- index-def: bounded-model-checking -->
> ### Inset — Reading behavior formally {#inset-behavior-formal}
>
> **A state machine** is a finite set of states and the legal transitions between them. A leased job is
> *queued*, *leased*, *running*, or *reclaimed*; a worker taking the lease moves *queued → leased*, a
> lease expiring moves *leased → queued*. Draw it and the illegal edges show themselves — a path back to
> *running* from *reclaimed* is the double-execution bug, visible before the code runs. The state machine
> is the spine a behavior invariant attaches to.
>
> **An invariant over behavior is a predicate over an order of events**, not over one state. Two shapes
> cover almost all of them, and each names one operator worth memorizing:
>
> - **Safety — □P ("always P").** A bad thing never happens. *"A leased job always has exactly one owner."*
>   You settle it by searching the reachable states for any that violate P.
> - **Liveness — P ↝ Q ("P leads-to Q").** A good thing eventually happens. *"A preempted job eventually
>   re-runs"; "a completed worktree eventually tombstones."* No single state settles it — it is a claim
>   about the *sequence*, so it needs a checker that reasons about cyclic runs.
>
> Declaring the shape is not decoration: it **routes the checker**. Route a liveness property to a safety
> runtime and the runtime structurally cannot see the violation — it reports green. So the shape is checked
> too (below).
>
> **Bounded model checking** settles a safety property the honest way a test cannot. A test *samples* the
> schedules you thought of; a distributed race hides in the one you did not. Model checking *exhaustively
> walks* every interleaving out to a fixed depth *k*, and either shows no reachable state violates the
> predicate or hands back the exact counterexample trace. The limit is the bound: a bug needing *k+1* steps,
> or one the model abstracts away, is out of scope — the price of a decidable check.
>
> *That is the whole vocabulary these models use: a state machine, an invariant as □P or P ↝ Q, and an
> exhaustive walk to depth k. The appendix primer adds the operators (◇, Until) and edge cases this Part
> does not.*

## What must hold across every interleaving

<!-- point: the-synchronization-model-declares-every-lock-and-its-ordering | The synchronization model declares every lock and its required ordering. | terms: synchronization-model, lock-ordering, concurrency -->
<!-- index-def: synchronization-model -->
*What runs at once, and what stays true across every schedule?* Concurrency is where a
correct-in-isolation design becomes a source of races, deadlocks, and torn writes, and the answer
cannot be a thought experiment about interleavings. It has to be a declared, checked contract.

The **synchronization model** is the first. It is a typed registry of every OS-level lock: which
shared resource each guards, and the required acquisition ordering between them. Declaring the
ordering turns deadlock-avoidance into a graph check. Here is the one illustrative invariant.

<!-- label: process-invariants -->
<!-- table: One invariant of the synchronization model — an inverted lock acquisition is caught at author time. [short: Synchronization model — illustrative invariant] -->
| Invariant | Temporal shape | How it is checked |
|---|---|---|
| No acquisition inverts a declared ordering | *□P* (safety) | An ordering lint walks each code path's acquisition sequence against the declared ordering graph; a held lock acquiring one that must precede it is an inversion, and a finding — the deadlock is caught before it can hang. |

The natural diagram is an ER schema — one lock record, the sites that acquire it, the ordering edges
that constrain their sequence:

Read it as three related record kinds: a lock and its guarded resource, the sites that take it, and the ordering edges that constrain their sequence. The coverage and ordering lints walk this schema to catch an undeclared lock or a deadlock-inducing acquisition. -->

### Two ways to make a concurrent write safe

<!-- section-terms: mediator-registry, single-writer-registry, concurrency -->

<!-- point: capping-how-many-and-allowing-exactly-one-fail-differently | Capping how-many and allowing exactly-one fail differently. | terms: mediator-registry, single-writer-registry, concurrency -->
<!-- index-def: mediator-registry -->
<!-- index-def: single-writer-registry -->
Above the raw locks sit two registries that draw a distinction worth naming, because confusing them
is how a corruption bug hides inside a load limit. **There are two ways to make a concurrent write
safe, and they fail differently.**

- **The mediator registry answers *how many*.** It declares which heavy or shared-resource invocations
  must run through a serializer — the test runner, the build tool, a whole-repo lint mutex — and at what
  cap (1 for a mutex, M for a semaphore). Its failure class is **resource contention**: too many heavy
  processes on one box. A coverage lint fails any unmediated call to a mediated class at author time.
- **The single-writer registry answers *who*.** It declares that one function, and only one, may write a
  given piece of shared state. Its failure class is a **torn or interleaved write** — which a concurrency
  cap of two would happily permit, because two mediated processes racing on one record is still within cap.
  A second discovered writer of that state is a finding.

**Concurrency control bounds contention; the single-writer registry bounds ownership.** Only naming
both keeps a corruption bug from hiding behind a semaphore that was only ever rationing load. This one
cost me a day of chasing a corrupted registry file before I saw that two "safely rationed" writers
were the whole problem: the semaphore was doing its job and guarding nothing that mattered. Full
treatment of both registries is in the appendix. [appendix: appendix-g-mediator-registry-reference] [appendix: appendix-g-single-writer-registry-reference]

### The verification backbone

<!-- section-terms: formal-invariant-verification, temporal-logic -->

<!-- point: an-invariants-temporal-form-routes-the-exhaustive-checker | An invariant's temporal form routes its exhaustive checker, so a mis-declared shape is a build failure. | terms: formal-invariant-verification, safety-property, liveness-property -->
<!-- index-def: formal-invariant-verification -->
All three concurrency models hang their invariant tables on one shared method, and it is trunk
machinery rather than a model of the running system. **Formal invariant verification** takes an
invariant's temporal shape and routes the exhaustive checker for it: a safety form (□P) to a
state-space search, a liveness form (P ↝ Q) to a temporal model checker. Get the shape wrong — declare
a liveness property as safety — and the safety runtime cannot see the violation and reports green. So a
lint checks the declared shape against the checker that ran: the shape is *consumed*, and a mis-routed
invariant is a build failure instead of a false all-clear. Its full construct-and-invariant treatment
is in the appendix. [appendix: appendix-g-invariant-dag-reference]

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [formal invariant verification](appendix-b-formal-invariant-verification.html).

## Where each part runs

<!-- point: the-deployment-topology-model-declares-where-each-service-runs | The deployment-topology model declares where each service runs, and parity holds it to reality. | terms: deployment-topology-model, placement, deployment-parity -->
<!-- index-def: physical-view -->
<!-- index-def: deployment-topology-model -->
*Where does each part run, and which of those placements is a correctness decision?* A functionally
correct system can still be mis-deployed. Placement is not a detail an agent discovers after the fact
in scattered deployment configuration — it is a fact the fleet should read before it deploys.

The **deployment-topology model** makes it one. It is a typed statement of where each service runs:
its name, its owning layer, its tier class. From that, the deploy scripts reason about a declared
topology instead of scattered constants, and a parity check reconciles it against the real deploy
table. The one illustrative invariant runs in *both* directions.

<!-- label: physical-invariants -->
<!-- table: One invariant of the deployment-topology model — bidirectional parity holds the model to the real deploy table. [short: Deployment-topology model — illustrative invariant] -->
| Invariant | Temporal shape | How it is checked |
|---|---|---|
| Every declared service deploys at the tier the model names, and every deployed service appears in the model | *□P* (safety) | A parity set-diff, run both ways: model ⊆ reality catches a tier that drifted in the code; reality ⊆ model catches a service that shipped with no model row. Either mismatch fails the build. |

Neither direction is optional. Check only model ⊆ reality and you miss a service that shipped without
a model row; check only reality ⊆ model and you miss a tier that drifted in the code. The record's
schema, its layer-boundary graph (checked under structure), and the derivation direction are in the
appendix. [appendix: appendix-g-deployment-topology-reference]

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [deployment and tier topology model](appendix-b-deployment-topology-model.html).

## Correctness in the graph, resource limits in a profile

<!-- point: correctness-deps-in-the-graph-host-limits-in-a-profile | Correctness dependencies stay in the graph; host-specific resource limits live in a scheduler profile. | terms: invariant-dag-execution-policy, edge-intent, per-host-load-profile -->
<!-- index-def: invariant-dag-execution-policy -->
<!-- slogan: profile-edit-not-graph-edit -->
The placement models raise one more question — *what resources constrain execution?* — and answering
it well turns on a single modeling principle, the most portable idea in this chapter: **correctness
dependencies stay in the graph; host-specific resource limits live in a scheduler profile.**

What must be true on *every* host — B is wrong without A — belongs in the deploy graph. How *hard a
particular box may be pushed* — how many steps run at once, whether a cost gate is worth honoring —
belongs in that host's profile, read by a scheduler. Move the stress between hosts and you edit the
profile, never the graph: **profile edit, not graph edit.**

The **invariant-DAG execution policy** embodies it. Each deploy-graph edge carries a typed intent:
`CORRECTNESS` (B is wrong without A; honored on every host) and `COST_GATE` (a cheap check gating an
expensive step; honored unless the budget is unbounded) stay in the graph, host-identical. The `LOAD`
intent — B contends with A for a scarce box — is *banned from the graph* and migrated to the scheduler,
which reads a per-host profile of a concurrency ceiling and a budget and emits the execution plan. The
mapping is a pure function of the profile, small enough to read as a table:

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — inline host-profile → plan mapping strip making the scheduler function scannable, a reference not a numbered cross-referenced float -->
| Host profile | Ceiling | Budget | Emitted plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic staging | none (unbounded) | ∞ (unbounded) | fan the whole ready wave out; relax cost gates |
| Metered prod | low finite | finite | small concurrency; honor cost gates |
| Scarce local box | 1 | finite | serialize; honor cost gates |

Moving stress between hosts edits one row of this table. The one illustrative invariant keeps the
graph portable:

<!-- label: host-stress-invariants -->
<!-- table: One invariant of the invariant-DAG execution policy — a load edge in the deploy graph is banned. [short: Invariant-DAG execution policy — illustrative invariant] -->
| Invariant | Temporal shape | How it is checked |
|---|---|---|
| No deploy edge carries the `LOAD` intent | *□P* (safety) | A load-edge lint reads the graph's resident intent set; a `LOAD` edge — the thing that would fork the graph per host — is a finding. Load rationing is a semaphore in the scheduler, never a graph edge. |

The edge-intent schema, the profile record, the scheduler code, and the full invariant set are in the
appendix; the model is also walked end to end in the next chapter's join example. [appendix: appendix-g-invariant-dag-reference]

## One honest gap

<!-- point: model-only-where-a-failure-lives-so-the-latency-slice-stays-empty | Model only where a failure lives; the pure-latency slice stayed empty until an incident filled it. | terms: model-only-where-a-failure-lives, pure-latency, honest-gap -->
<!-- index-def: model-only-where-a-failure-lives -->
That policy models the *load-and-cost* half of execution. The *pure-latency* half — request latency,
cache-hit ratios, data-movement cost — has no dedicated model, and the honest move is to say so rather
than invent one to fill the slot. The discipline behind the gap is **model only where a failure
lives**: a model built with no decision, invariant, or named risk to exercise it rots before anyone
checks it, because nothing reads it.

The gap has drawn blood once. To cut idle cost, the fleet let its services scale to zero — a change
the cost half of the policy welcomed and the latency half could not weigh. The cold-start incident in
[Metrics](3.5-metrics.html) is exactly this gap surfacing: a warm per-request floor a fleet-average
number hid, run down to a resident-runtime re-architecture that cut it 4,057 → 109 ms. A latency slice
here — request latency typed per service, the cold-start penalty an invariant over it — would have
weighed that trade before a user waited on it. The gap stays honest; this is what it costs to leave open.

## Computing a migration's blast radius

<!-- point: declared-substrate-stances-turn-a-migration-grep-into-a-blast-radius | Declared substrate stances make a migration blast radius computable. | terms: control-substrate-dependency, migration-safety, blast-radius -->
<!-- index-def: control-substrate-dependency -->
One last behavioral question an agent asks before it changes the ground everything runs on: *which
mechanisms assume the substrate I am about to replace?* The **substrate-dependency model** makes each
governance mechanism declare, as typed metadata, the substrate assumption it bakes in — bound to one
substrate, substrate-aware, or substrate-agnostic. Joining those declarations against the topology
model on the migration target turns a hopeful grep into a *computed* blast radius: a declaration lint
requires the stance, so the one mechanism whose assumption is implicit cannot hide from the join.

Worked once, on the plane the fleet actually migrated. When the system retired a Redis poll plane for
a push plane, the join answered *which mechanisms bake in "workers pull from a Redis sorted set"?*
before the change landed — the queue-depth metric, the stale-job sweep, the cancellation cleanup, the
priority-preservation requeue, each declaring "bound to the poll plane" and each surfacing in the
table. A substrate-agnostic mechanism that reads only durable Postgres truth declares its stance and
correctly does not appear. The typed schema and the join code are in the appendix. [appendix: appendix-g-control-substrate-dependency-reference]

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [control↔substrate dependency model](appendix-b-control-substrate-dependency.html).

These models make behavior analyzable: time and interleaving through the state machines and
concurrency contracts, placement and resource cost through the topology and scheduling models. Each
answered one question in isolation. A real engineering task rarely stays inside one — a user's request
starts as a scenario, resolves its endpoints in the structural model, reaches concurrent workers,
deployment tiers, and the scheduler's plan. The next chapter is where the models meet.
