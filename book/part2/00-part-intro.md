Every mature engineering discipline reasons through models. Aerospace, mechanical, and civil
engineering each externalize the system into a representation they can analyze before they build,
and they treat that representation as the work surface. Software mostly did not. It reached for
models, found them too expensive to keep matched to fast-moving code, and learned to work without
them.

Model-based engineering never failed on its ideas. It failed on upkeep: the standing cost of
keeping a hand-drawn model equal to a codebase that moved underneath it. Coding agents change that
term. The dull, unending reconciliation that once buried the practice is now the cheap repeated
work a fleet supplies. The novelty is not that models exist. The novelty is that keeping them
synchronized has finally become affordable, so software can join the rest of engineering in
reasoning through models rather than around them.

This Part shows what that buys, and it walks a single ascent to show it. A commodity reasoner
arrives knowing nothing about your system. You give it better context. You externalize that
context into connected knowledge. You structure the knowledge into models. At the top, you let the
environment hold those models to the code. Each step earns a rung of capability, and the climb is
the Part's spine. The goal is not to teach one modeling framework. It is to trace a progression
from *helping the intelligence reason* to *governing what it builds* — the same progression the
capability ladder later in this Part draws rung by rung.

The reader who wants the older motive keeps it: the time for models has come. But the claim
underneath is an engineering claim about cost, not a claim about what AI can suddenly do. The
expense that kept software out of model-based practice has moved off the human payroll, and that is
what makes the rest of this Part practical.
