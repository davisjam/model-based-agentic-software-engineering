# The Governed Environment: Ex-Ante and Ex-Post

<!-- point: a-governed-environment-has-an-ex-ante-and-an-ex-post-half | A governed environment has an ex-ante and ex-post half. | terms: ex-ante-governance, ex-post-governance -->
One rule runs this whole chapter: **an ex-post failure becomes an ex-ante control when
judgment classifies the recurrence and encodes it in the environment.** That is the named
mechanism — the move that turns a mistake you caught once into a check the environment
enforces on every agent after. You cannot write a trustworthy environment down in advance;
the question becomes how trust grows from the failures no specification predicted.

It has two halves, because governance does. Some things you know in advance about what
should be true of your software. When an obligation is known in advance, the path is
comparatively direct: write it into a specification up front and hold the agent to it. But
real software engineering rarely works that way — you discover what you are building by
building part of it. So a governed environment carries what you can decide *before* you
start and what you can only learn *along the way*, and the promotion rule is the hinge
between them. This chapter is about both halves, and about the two forms every governance
mechanism finally takes.

That an agentic team needs governance of its own is beginning to draw formal treatment.
Recent academic work proposes a domain-specific language for defining and enforcing
governance policies across mixed human-agent software projects [cite: ait2025governance].
MAGE converges with that direction but works at a different grain: not a single policy
language, but a repertoire of governance mechanisms drawn from a production case — the
machinery this chapter and the companion catalogue lay out.

## Ex-ante: everything you can decide up front

<!-- section-terms: ex-ante-governance, specification-driven-development -->

<!-- point: ex-ante-write-down-what-you-know-and-determinize-it-with-cheap-checks | Write down what you know; determinize it with cheap checks. | terms: ex-ante-governance, specification-driven-development -->
<!-- index-def: ex-ante-governance -->
<!-- index-def: specification-driven-development -->
Some things you genuinely know from the beginning, and you should write every one of
them down. This is specification-driven development, and where you can do it, you should.
A coding style guide is the everyday example — Google has one, Airbnb has one — full of
rules like "every function carries a comment" or "we never call `exec` outside this one
approved place." Give the agent those rules, and then write *deterministic checks* for
them. If a program's output has a required format, write the checker that confirms it —
or, better, have the agent write the checker. Determinizing what you know up front gives
you a much stronger starting point. Implementation of many checks is cheap, and the agent
writes them — but deciding the right policy and maintaining its fit are not, a cost this
Part later proves.

## Ex-post: everything you learn by building

<!-- section-terms: ex-post-governance, governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: ex-post-you-build-governance-as-failures-reveal-themselves | You build governance ex-post, as failures reveal themselves. | terms: ex-post-governance -->
<!-- index-def: ex-post-governance -->
But you will not know everything, and pretending otherwise is the mistake. You will
discover that performance is a problem and you have no performance model. You will
discover that a particular model version has a tic (every LLM's fondness for the
em-dash is the running joke, and nobody quite knows why — the training data is not full of
them, yet every model reaches for one) and that the tics move when you upgrade. So you will build
governance *ex-post*: after the fact, iteratively, strapping on new mechanisms as the
failures reveal themselves and as the software's real properties emerge. You define what
you can up front, and you stay willing to discover the rest. The rest is the mistakes you are
making, the mistakes the model is making, the gap between what you thought the customer
wanted and what they did, and the gap between how you thought the code would perform and how it did.

<!-- point: ex-post-is-where-most-real-mechanisms-come-from-not-a-fallback | Ex-post is where most real mechanisms come from. | terms: ex-post-governance, governance-conversion -->
The honest version of the loop is less tidy than the ex-ante story makes it sound. You
set up the governance you can imagine in advance, you launch the work, and then you
**watch it burn and then try to fix it.** The failures you did not foresee are the ones
that teach you which mechanism to build next. Ex-post governance is not a fallback for a
weak plan; it is where most of the real mechanisms come from, because most of the real
failures are the ones no specification predicted.

<!-- point: the-engine-is-to-hold-a-quality-goal-with-a-mechanism-placed-up-front-or-after-a-failure-seen-twice | Hold a quality goal with a mechanism that keeps it. | terms: governance-conversion, constraints-and-sensors -->
<!-- index-def: governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: constraints-and-sensors -->
The engine of the whole discipline is what you do to hold a quality goal: **you set up a
mechanism that keeps it.** This is the organizing move of the companion catalogue. You
place some mechanisms up front, from what you know about the domain, and you add more in
response to a recurring or consequential failure — a mistake caught in yesterday's manual
audit becomes today's automatic check, enforced on every agent thereafter instead of
re-caught by inspection. That conversion runs the printer loop's own shape (introduced in
[Loops and Models](1.3-loops-and-models.html)), escalated: when the
loop keeps hitting one wall, judgment turns the failure class into a durable
mechanism ([ref:governance-conversion-loop]). The payoff is not one failure retired; it is
an environment left more capable of absorbing the next change. Conversions accrue — the
positive dual of churn, a dynamic Part 6 names and bounds.

<!-- label: governance-conversion-loop -->
<!-- figure: assets/governance-conversion-loop.svg | *The Governance-Conversion Loop.* The loop that hardens the environment, drawn in the same shape as the printer loop, escalated one level. The printer loop produces; when it keeps hitting one wall — the same failure class, again and again — judgment reads the recurrence as evidence of a missing mechanism and converts the class into a durable control the environment enforces on every later agent. A mistake caught in yesterday's manual audit becomes today's automatic check. The payoff is not one failure retired; it is an environment left more capable of absorbing the next change, so conversions accrue — the positive dual of churn the closing theory names and bounds. -->

None of this is new as an instinct. Manufacturing stops the line at a defect and reshapes
the work so the defect cannot recur; resilience engineering treats an incident as a signal
about the system, not a verdict on the operator [cite: hollnagel2006resilience]; site
reliability practice turns each outage into a blameless postmortem whose action items become
durable reliability work [cite: beyer2016sre]; Shingo built mistake-proofing into the fixture
itself [cite: shingo1986zqc]. MAGE inherits that family; the promotion rule this chapter
opened with is its statement for a fleet. The changed ingredient is velocity: the fleet
surfaces structural gaps fast enough that the environment accumulates reliability one
failure class at a time.

## The controls accumulate {#control-growth}

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: the-control-substrate-grew-from-zero | The control substrate grew from zero. | terms: governance-conversion -->
Run the promotion rule for a season and the environment fills with controls — nearly all of
them discovered ex-post, the obligations no up-front specification would have named. The case
project shows the shape. [ref:control-growth-curve] counts its two hardest control surfaces —
project-specific lint files and gate scripts — at the same four windows the build passed
through, with the exact counts on the curve.

<!-- label: control-growth-curve -->
<!-- figure: assets/control-growth.svg | The controls accumulate across four windows — lint files (0 to 336 to 595 to 747) and gate scripts (0 to 20 to 76 to 102). Both start at literal zero: the substrate is post-prototype. The climb is steepest through mechanization and rises after, one class at a time. -->

The curve is the accumulation, not its cause. Those lint files carry 993 registered lint
specs at the final window, and each spec is a policy the environment now enforces on every
agent. What the count cannot separate is which controls a failure drove and which the domain
suggested up front — the two modes interleave by design, and no commit trail cleanly splits
them. But the discipline that converts a failure is itself documented and named. A paired
fix-and-lint tag marks the commits that ship a code fix beside the check that forbids its
recurrence, and it appears in 208 commits; 27 lints name a specific dated incident in their
own text, spot-checked as genuine after-the-failure conversions. Read the curve as a
documented, growing discipline, then — not a measured causal rate. [data: control-growth]

How large should the governed environment grow relative to the product? Conventional wisdom
offers a rough rule — about one line of tests for every line of production. [The timeline and
the work](5.2-the-timeline-and-the-work.html) measures the support ratio MAGE actually
reaches against that yardstick.

Either way you arrive there, a mechanism takes one of two forms, and the
difference between them is the difference between a firewall and a smoke detector.

## Constraints and sensors

<!-- section-terms: constraints-and-sensors, constraint, sensor -->

<!-- point: a-mechanism-is-one-of-two-moves-prevent-or-catch | A mechanism is one of two moves: prevent, or catch. | terms: constraint, sensor -->
A mechanism is one of two moves — prevent the mistake, or catch it — and [ref:constraint-vs-sensor] draws the split.

<!-- label: constraint-vs-sensor -->
<!-- figure: assets/constraint-vs-sensor.svg | *Constraint or Sensor.* The two moves and their asymmetric cost. A constraint prevents the mistake inside the iteration, so it costs no failed loop; a sensor catches it after the fact, so it costs at least one. Prefer the constraint where you can build one; add a sensor for the drift you cannot scope away. -->

<!-- point: words-alone-are-a-probability-flip-so-determinize-them-into-a-gate | Words are a probability flip; determinize them into a gate. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, constraint -->
<!-- index-def: gate -->
Before the two forms, the argument for reaching for a mechanism at all. Words alone
do not hold. Tell the agent "make sure the briefs always follow this template" and it
will say, *"Sure, boss"* — and then it will not reliably do it, not all the time,
because it is a probability flip. A prompt raises the odds; it does not settle them. But
you can *determinize that*. Instead of hoping, you check: before the agent launches,
confirm every required part of the template is present, and refuse the launch when it is
not. The soft instruction becomes a hard gate. That move — from a probability you nudge to a
condition you enforce — splits into the two forms below.

<!-- point: a-sensor-detects-drift-after-the-fact-and-costs-iterations | A sensor detects drift after the fact and costs iterations. | terms: sensor -->
<!-- index-def: sensor -->
<!-- index-example: observability -->
**A sensor detects drift.** It lets the mistake happen and catches it after the fact —
and because it fires after, it fails the loop iteration: the agent has to run again to fix
what the sensor caught. Your test suite in CI is a sensor, and so are your lints. It would
be better if the model never wrote the bug; but if it does, the sensor catches it before
the code ships. The cost of a sensor is iterations — detect, fail, re-iterate — and that
cost is the reason you do not reach for one first.

<!-- point: a-sensor-is-only-as-good-as-its-observability | A sensor is only as good as its observability. | terms: sensor, observability -->
<!-- index-def: observability -->
A sensor is only as good as the signal it can read. **Observability** is the property
that a running system exposes enough signal to explain what it did and to catch what went
wrong. It is the wiring under every sensor: the event a gate fires on, the log a checker
reads, the trace that turns "the build failed" into "the build failed *here, for this
reason.*" Build the observability first and the sensor has something to watch; skip it
and it stares at a blank wall.

<!-- point: a-constraint-prevents-drift-within-the-iteration-and-costs-no-iteration | A constraint prevents drift within the iteration, costing none. | terms: constraint, typed-enum -->
<!-- index-def: constraint -->
**A constraint prevents drift.** Where a sensor detects an instance of the mistake after
the fact, a constraint makes the whole class impossible: it scopes the agent's action space
*within* the iteration, so the wrong move is never available to pick. The cleanest example is types. Agents are careless with unconstrained symbolic
vocabularies, and a compiler exists to confirm that functions are called with the
right arguments. Suppose the agent has represented a state with bare strings. Because it is
a language model underneath, it will cheerfully reach for a synonym next time, and the
synonym silently fails to match — you only learn that an iteration later, when a sensor,
the test suite, crashes. Tell it to use an enumeration instead, and the whole failure mode
evaporates: it can no longer invent an unlisted value, and if it tries, the compiler
rejects it on the spot. Choosing the enum over the string forbids the bug rather than
watching for it, and because the wrong value can never be picked, no iteration is spent
catching it. This is why the catalogue leans so hard on structured models and closed
enumerations over free-form strings: a constraint that removes a mistake costs no
iteration, while a sensor that catches it costs at least one.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [closed enumerations over free-form strings](appendix-c-typed-categories.html).

<!-- point: constraint-and-sensor-are-control-engineerings-working-terms | Constraint and sensor come from control engineering. | terms: constraint, sensor, constraints-and-sensors -->

> *A footnote on control engineering.* Constraint and sensor are the working terms of
> control engineering. A governed plant — a reactor, an autopilot — carries constraints
> on the inputs and states it may reach, and sensors on the outputs it produces, so the
> controller can read the error and correct the next cycle. I kept the terms because the
> parallel is useful and structurally close. A fleet of probabilistic machines is a plant
> you cannot run open-loop; the governed environment is the loop you close around it. Constraints scope
> the action space, sensors read the output and fail the iteration, and the next
> iteration corrects. Engineers have run unruly physical processes this way for a
> century. Governing a fleet inherits the discipline along with the words.
>
> Learn more: Nancy Leveson's *Engineering a Safer World* [cite: leveson2011] applies the same systems view to safety engineering.

> *A footnote on closing the loop in the wild.* Ericsson's EIAP closed loop pairs
> intent-based operation over curated, topology-aware real-time network state with a
> controlled actuation surface (Network Manager and Orchestrator send coordinated actions
> back into the network), and decouples the model lifecycle — declarative deployment,
> monitoring, automated retraining under drift detection — from the application lifecycle:
> closed-loop autonomy needs both a state representation and a bounded actuation surface, or
> it is not operational autonomy [cite:ericsson2026closedloop].

> **Sidebar — Field note: the pipeline that ran open-loop.**
> One of our document-remediation pipelines had every mark of a clean design: a single driver, one
> phase taxonomy, a typed contract on every pass, and a topological sort that checked the order at
> startup. It had drifted anyway. The order it declared and the order it actually ran had come apart at
> seven places, and the seven were not scattered. They sat exactly where the accessibility-AI loop
> churned hardest, each new generation trick wired in "just for now," straight into the run path,
> skipping the registry. The formats with little AI surface never drifted at all.
>
> The registry was a constraint, and it held for the passes that went through it. The inline additions
> did not, and the one guard I had — the startup sort — only ever looked at registered passes. So the
> new work slipped outside the constraint, and nothing watched the gap. That is an open loop: a part of
> the system adding capability every iteration, with no sensor reading its output and failing the loop
> when the output drifts. A constraint you can route around is not holding the class; it is holding the
> cases that happened to stay inside it. The cost did not show up as a failed iteration. It showed up
> months later, as seven sites to reconcile.
>
> The repair was the missing sensor: a parity check that compares the passes that actually run against
> the declared graph and fails when an unregistered one appears. It earns its keep precisely where the
> iteration is hottest, which is where I could least afford to notice by hand. One honest caveat travels
> with this. Not every divergence was decay; a few passes are forced inline by the format libraries and
> are correctly waved through with a stated reason. Telling corrosion from a sanctioned exception is a
> judgment the sensor frames but does not make, and this is one pipeline in one project. The transferable
> part is narrower and, I think, sturdier: a clean architecture under active iteration is the *most*
> likely place to decay, not the least, so that is exactly where a sensor pays for itself.

<!-- point: form-and-move-are-two-independent-axes | Form and move are two independent axes. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, constraints-and-sensors -->
<!-- index-def: governance-package -->
<!-- gloss-only: Constraint | A governance mechanism that prevents drift by scoping the agent's action space so the wrong move is unavailable within a loop iteration (a typed enum instead of a string); the prevented mistake costs no failed iteration. -->
<!-- gloss-only: Governance mechanism | A repeatable environmental structure that encodes an engineering policy and either constrains an action, detects a violation, requires evidence, or controls admission — four classes: prevention, detection, evidence, admission. The umbrella for a constraint or a sensor, replacing the ambiguous word "control"; a model joins governance only when something reads or enforces it. -->
<!-- gloss-only: Governance package | A mechanism that bundles several: a soft constraint (a structured model that aims the agent) shipped with hard sensors (the lints and drift-parity gates that catch what the constraint only aims at). -->
<!-- gloss-only: Sensor | A governance mechanism that detects drift after the fact and fails the loop iteration, so the agent must run again to fix what it caught (a lint, a test, a gate); it costs iterations. -->
Two axes cross here, and it pays to hold them apart. A mechanism has a *form* — **soft**, it
aims the agent, or **hard**, it holds regardless — and a *move* — a **constraint**, it prevents,
or a **sensor**, it detects. The two are independent: a constraint can be soft (a structured model
that aims the agent's reasoning) or hard (an enum the compiler enforces), and a sensor can be
soft (a convention that reminds) or hard (a lint that blocks). Constraint-versus-sensor is *what*
the mechanism does; soft-versus-hard is *how firmly* it does it. [ref:form-and-move] lays the
two axes out as a grid.

<!-- label: form-and-move -->
<!-- figure: assets/form-move-2x2.svg | *Form × Move.* The two axes are independent: form (soft aims a reasoner, hard holds a line) crosses move (a constraint prevents, a sensor catches). Any move can be built either way — the four cells give one worked example each. -->

<!-- point: most-real-mechanisms-are-a-package-so-tag-the-primary-move | Most mechanisms are a package; tag the primary move. | terms: governance-package, constraints-and-sensors -->
Most real mechanisms are a *package* across both axes. A structured model is a soft constraint on the
agent's reasoning — it aims the agent at the right shape without forcing it — and it ships
with hard sensors, the lints and drift-parity gates that catch what the soft constraint
only aims at. The definition-of-done review is the same shape: a runbook that constrains
the steps, wrapped in sensors that flag a drifted one. So name the package, and tag its
primary move rather than forcing it into one bin: prefer a constraint where you can build
one, and add a sensor for the drift you cannot scope away. ("Architecture" is the design
activity that builds constraints — not a third kind of thing.)

<!-- point: a-purely-organizational-answer-is-all-sensors-and-never-removes-a-failure-class | A purely organizational answer is all sensors, removing no class. | terms: sensor, constraint -->
Seen from the outside, this is what a purely organizational answer leaves on the table.
Faced with an unreliable worker, the instinct is to wrap it in process: a review, an approval, an
evidence trail, a sign-off before the work counts.
Every one of those is a sensor.
Each lets the mistake happen and catches it after the fact: ex-post, an iteration late, an inspection of
output the agent has already produced.
A discipline built only of sensors can raise the odds and shrink the blast radius, and yet it never removes
a failure class, because it watches the action space instead of scoping it.
The constraint is the move that answer does not have: it works ex-ante, inside the iteration, so the
wrong move is never available to pick.
That is the difference between supplying reliability by construction and inspecting for it after the fact.

<!-- point: the-constraint-is-poka-yoke-the-sensors-stop-is-jidoka | The constraint is poka-yoke; the sensor's stop is jidoka. | terms: constraint, sensor -->

> *A footnote on the factory floor.* [The Engineer's Seat](1.5-the-engineers-seat.html)
> introduced Toyota's names for these two moves: the constraint is *poka-yoke*, and the
> sensor's fail-the-iteration reflex is *jidoka*. The lesson Toyota drew is the one this
> section just argued — inspection at the end of the line cannot build quality in. A fleet
> of agents is a production line for code, and it relearns the same economics.

### Two axes, one bounded term {#four-classes-one-bounded-term}

<!-- section-terms: governance-mechanism, constraints-and-sensors -->

<!-- point: governance-mechanism-needs-a-boundary | Without a boundary, the word mechanism swells. | terms: governance-mechanism -->
One boundary before moving on, because without it the word swells. Prompts, documents,
lints, tests, metrics, hooks, models — everything in the environment starts to look like
"a governance mechanism," and a term that covers everything governs nothing. Bound it:

<!-- index-def: governance-mechanism -->
> **Governance mechanism.** A repeatable environmental structure that encodes an
> engineering policy and either constrains an action, detects a violation, requires
> evidence, or controls admission.

<!-- point: four-classes-prevention-detection-evidence-admission | Two questions cross: how it treats failure, where it acts. | terms: governance-mechanism, constraints-and-sensors, governance-package -->
<!-- index-def: validator -->
The clause list is not one flat set of four peers. It is two questions crossing.
**Constraint and sensor classify _how_ a mechanism treats a failure — prevent it, or catch
it — and that is the catalogue's Move axis, three-valued: constraint, sensor, or _package_
for a bundle carrying both. Evidence and admission classify _where_ a mechanism acts — at
the loop's closing door, not at the action.** An evidence mechanism demands the change
carry its proof: a provenance stamp, a re-derived test result, a completion re-checked
rather than self-reported. An admission mechanism decides whether the result may advance at
all — the admission rule of the engineered loop (1.3), holding the last door. Both are
still built out of constraints and sensors; they earn their own names from their position
in the loop, not from a third way of treating failure. So the earlier split was never the
whole taxonomy — it is the whole Move axis, and evidence and admission are a second,
positional cut across it.

Evidence and admission compose into how a loop earns the right to advance. Cheap checks
run early and bind their result to an exact tree, so a later, more expensive stage can
*check that binding* rather than *trust* a report; and the definition of done recomputes
the full evidence at close instead of reading a marker. Cheap evidence early, full
re-derivation late, never a trusted self-report — the catalogue lists it as a composition
of staged admission and re-derived completion.

<!-- point: a-model-is-not-a-mechanism-until-something-enforces-it | A model joins governance only when something enforces it. | terms: governance-mechanism, model-as-map -->
The definition also settles a boundary this book leans on. **A model file is policy. It
becomes a mechanism only when some agent, tool, or gate consumes it** — injected into an
agent's context, it is the soft constraint met earlier; read by a lint, a gate, or a
generator, it is a hard sensor. That is the reconciliation: a structured model can be a
soft constraint *and* still be inert on disk, because "soft constraint" already names the
consuming step — the injection into a reasoner that the model alone does not perform. The
model supplies the policy; the consumption supplies the holding.

### What it adds up to

<!-- point: the-governed-environment-is-determinize-what-you-can-then-convert-every-surprise-into-a-constraint-or-sensor | Determinize what you can; convert every surprise into a mechanism. | terms: governed-environment, constraints-and-sensors -->
<!-- index-def: governed-environment -->
Step back and this is what you are really doing: describing policies
for how the engineering work should proceed, and letting those policies produce a
**governed engineering environment** — the rules of the road the agents operate within.
That combination — determinize what you can, then convert every recurring surprise into
a constraint or a sensor — *is* the governed engineering environment.

<!-- point: governance-extends-the-reasoning-horizon-by-holding-the-obligation-in-the-environment | Governance extends the reasoning horizon: the environment holds the obligation. | terms: governed-environment, reasoning-horizon, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
<!-- index-example: reasoning-horizon -->
One frame ties this chapter to the last Part. A model extends the reasoning horizon by
externalizing *knowledge*: the fleet reasons through a compact picture instead of
rebuilding the whole system in working state. A governed environment extends the same
horizon by externalizing *judgment*: the engineer decides once that some invariant must
hold, encodes it as a constraint or a sensor, and the environment carries that obligation
afterward. Neither agent nor engineer has to keep "don't break invariant X" in working
memory, because the ground they stand on keeps it for them. Modeling externalizes
knowledge and alignment externalizes judgment, and both buy back the same scarce thing:
how much of a system a bounded reasoner can hold coherent at once. Sometimes the obstacle
is not a missing checker but a missing representation for the checker to run on; Part 3
develops that idea through explicit models.

## The residual: goals no mechanism reaches

<!-- section-terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->

<!-- point: some-goals-split-into-neither-because-the-failure-is-an-absence-nobody-specified | Some goals split into neither, being an absence nobody specified. | terms: residual -->
<!-- index-def: residual -->
That closing line reads as if it settles everything. It does not: some goals split into
**neither**. No cheap sensor can see the failure and no constraint can scope it away,
because the failure is an **absence nobody specified**, and you cannot watch for the
violation of a rule no one wrote or forbid an action the design never mentioned.

<!-- point: the-residual-is-a-human-job-that-sets-the-throughput-ceiling | The residual is a human job setting the throughput ceiling. | terms: residual, judgment-is-the-scarce-resource -->
Security remediation is the clean case. A missing authorization check has **no failing
test — by definition.** If a test caught it, the check was specified; if it was specified,
it would not be missing. The gap is invisible because nothing ever named it. So the goal stays a **human** job:
someone has to look at the system and *know* the check should be there. Call this the
**residual** — the third outcome beside prevent and detect, the goals no mechanism reaches.
Which of your goals land in it sets your real throughput ceiling: the mechanized goals run
at fleet speed, and these run at yours. It is the residual, not the size of the catalogue,
that bounds how fast the whole system can go.

<!-- point: the-residual-is-the-complement-of-the-two-moves-not-a-third-value-on-the-soft-hard-axis | The residual is the complement of both moves, not soft. | terms: residual, soft-vs-hard-governance -->
This is **not** the soft end of the soft-versus-hard axis you just met. A soft mechanism —
a structured model that aims the agent, a convention that reminds — is still a *mechanism*: a
named artifact that raises the odds and that you can point to. The residual has none.
There is nothing to name, nothing to tag as a constraint or a sensor, nothing to add to
the census. It is the *complement* of the two moves, not a third value on the axis.

<!-- point: naming-the-residual-is-the-honest-edge-that-makes-the-mechanized-core-credible | Naming the residual is the honest edge that earns trust. | terms: residual -->
Naming the residual costs the method nothing; a method that claims to mechanize
everything is the one to distrust. The residual is the honest edge that makes the
mechanized core credible — it says plainly what the catalogue does *not* cover, and that
admission is what lets you trust the coverage it does claim.

<!-- point: the-residual-is-not-a-fixed-wall-authoring-the-missing-spec-pulls-a-goal-out | The residual is not fixed; authoring the spec shrinks it. | terms: residual, mirror-vs-spec -->
Nor is the residual a fixed wall. You can sometimes pull a goal out of it by
**authoring the missing spec.** The same absence that defeats a sensor defeats a *derived*
model: a model read off the code records the missing check as correctly absent, and the
drift gate agrees with the bug — a mirror cannot see what was never specified (the same
absence, restated for models, 2.2). And a journey test that asserts only "the flow ran"
greens while the user's task is broken, until a human authors the terminal task-closure
assertion that says what *done* means — turning an un-sensable goal into a checkable
post-condition (3.6). Both are the same absence in two guises: a model that cannot reflect
an unwritten intent, and a spec that authorship supplies. What that human residual
*becomes* when the model, not the code, is the thing you author — the enduring job that
moves one level up — is the subject of the closing chapter (6.6). Everything in this
chapter governed the code the fleet writes: invariants, enums, lints, gates. But the fleet
does not only write code — it operates a system, and the operating judgment an agent lacks
is its own residual. The next chapter captures as much of that judgment as a runbook can
hold, and pushes the human boundary the same way authoring a spec does here.
