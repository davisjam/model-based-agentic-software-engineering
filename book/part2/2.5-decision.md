# Decision Models: What Is Allowed?

The models so far have been descriptive: they say what the system *is* or *does*. A decision model says
what the system *may* do. It represents alternatives and the rules that permit, require, or forbid them.
That single change in mood — from *does* to *may* — is the whole subject of the section.

## The Canonical Move: Permitted Alternatives

<!-- point: permission-has-many-canonical-forms-unified-by-mood-not-shape | Decision tables, rule systems, access-control matrices, policy graphs, feature models, and constraint systems all represent permission; what unifies them is not their shape but their normative mood. -->
Engineering represents permission in several familiar forms: decision tables that map conditions to
allowed actions, rule systems, access-control matrices that record which subject may act on which
object,[cite: lampson1974] policy graphs, feature models that constrain which combinations of options
are valid, and constraint systems that declare which assignments are admissible. What unifies them is
not their shape but their mood.

<!-- point: same-arrow-different-mood-is-the-sharpest-case-of-topology-not-fixing-meaning | A structural edge and a decision edge can be drawn identically and mean opposite kinds of thing — a fact about what happens versus a rule about what is permitted; an absent decision edge means "not allowed," not "does not happen." -->
This is the sharpest instance of the earlier lesson that topology does not fix meaning. A structural
edge and a decision edge can be drawn identically and mean opposite kinds of thing: `A → B` as a
structural edge says "A calls B," a fact about what happens; the same arrow as a decision edge says "A
*may* call B," a rule about what is permitted. The consequence lives in the absent edge. A missing
structural edge means "no such call happens"; a missing decision edge means "no such call is allowed."
The gap between those two readings is the reason decision models exist ([ref:canonical-decision]).

<!-- label: canonical-decision -->
<!-- figure: assets/canonical-decision.svg | *The decision move: the same arrow, a different mood.* A structural edge states that a call happens; a decision edge states that it is permitted. The absent edge carries the weight — "not allowed," not "does not happen" — and the analyses check a declared policy for consistency and least privilege. -->

<!-- point: a-declared-policy-turns-is-this-allowed-into-an-evaluation | Once permission is declared, the claims a policy designer wants become expressible — permitted/forbidden, mutual exclusion, prerequisites, consistency, completeness, least privilege — and "is this allowed?" becomes an evaluation rather than an argument. -->
Once permission is declared, the claims a policy designer wants become expressible: which alternatives
are permitted or forbidden, which are mutually exclusive, which imply prerequisites, whether the policy
is internally consistent, whether it is complete over the cases that can arise, and whether it achieves
least privilege. The analyses follow — evaluating a rule against a case, checking or solving
constraints, detecting conflicts between rules, testing completeness and consistency. A declared policy
turns "is this allowed?" into an evaluation rather than an argument.

## DocAble: Service-Flow Policy

DocAble declares which of its services may communicate with which. The model is a graph whose edges
carry the normative reading: an edge means the call is part of the sanctioned policy, and an absent edge
means the policy does not permit it.

<!-- box-family: model-card -->
> #### Service-flow model · Decision
>
> - **Engineering question** — Which service may call which, and what may be reached from outside?
> - **Model** — a declared graph of services and the permitted edges between them; the deployment is
>   wired from the graph rather than the graph inferred from the deployment.
> - **Property** — internal communication is permitted only along a declared edge; one declared node is
>   the public entry.
> - **Quality attribute** — least privilege; architectural integrity; security.

<!-- point: the-service-flow-model-is-a-declared-source-of-truth-not-a-firewall | The declared graph is the single source of truth for permitted inter-service edges, with one node declared publicly reachable and every other edge authenticated; a completeness check requires each gated edge to be fully specified, and the deployment config is generated from the graph. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
[ref:service-flow-model] draws the permitted calls. One node is declared publicly reachable; every other
edge is an authenticated internal call. The model is the single source of truth for those edges, and the
deployment is generated from it rather than inferred from it — so a completeness check can require every
gated edge to be fully specified before the deployment is wired. That check is real and enforced, but it
governs the *declaration's* internal consistency, not the running traffic. The model declares the
topology; it is not a runtime firewall.

<!-- label: service-flow-model -->
<!-- figure: assets/service-flow-model.svg | *Service-flow model.* A declared graph of which service may reach which. The public web tier is the only node facing the outside; an absent edge means the relationship is not permitted by the model, and enforcement is separate. -->

<!-- point: observed-versus-declared-is-correspondence-not-correctness | The observed call graph reveals that A calls C; the decision model states whether A may call C; agreement is correspondence, not correctness — a faithfully-obeyed rule can still be the wrong rule. -->
Declaring the policy exposes a question the running system cannot answer for itself: do the services
call only along declared edges? That is a correspondence between an observed structural fact and a
declared decision, and the two are different claims. The observed call graph reveals that A calls C; the
decision model states whether A *may* call C. [ref:observed-vs-declared] sets them side by side.

<!-- label: observed-vs-declared -->
<!-- figure: assets/observed-vs-declared.svg | *Observation and intent answer different questions.* The observed system can reveal that A calls C; the decision model can state whether that relationship is permitted. Agreement establishes correspondence, not correctness. -->

<!-- point: docable-does-not-reconcile-observed-traffic-so-correspondence-is-the-open-question | DocAble generates config from the declared graph but does not reconcile observed traffic against it at runtime; that open correspondence is the cleanest illustration in the chapter of a model exposing a checkable property without, by itself, establishing correspondence. -->
Agreement between observation and policy would establish *correspondence* — the implementation matching
the declared intent — not *correctness*: a faithfully-obeyed rule can still be the wrong rule. DocAble
generates its deployment from the declared graph, but nothing at runtime reconciles observed traffic
back against the declaration. That open correspondence is not a gap to hide. It is the clearest
illustration in this chapter of a model exposing a checkable property without, by itself, establishing that
the implementation satisfies it. Modeling declares what is permitted; whether the code stays on the
declared edges, and what a departure should cost, is Alignment's question in {{chapter:alignment}}.

## DocAble: When the System Changed but the Model Did Not

<!-- point: the-entitlement-model-was-born-from-a-production-failure | The service-flow policy was declared in advance; DocAble's upload-admission decision acquired its model in the opposite order, after a production failure exposed a representation that could no longer express the decision. -->
The service-flow policy was declared as intent, ahead of any violation. DocAble's upload-admission
decision acquired its model in the opposite order: the system changed first, one representation of the
decision did not, and the gap surfaced in production. The history is short enough to tell in order.

<!-- point: upload-entitlement-flows-through-groups | A user receives upload entitlement through groups — finite-credit or unlimited — so the admission decision depends on two facts: how many credits remain and whether any entitlement is unlimited. -->
DocAble admits an upload only when the user is entitled to it. A user receives that entitlement through
groups. A group may carry a finite credit balance, drawn down as pages are processed, or it may provide
unlimited use. The admission decision therefore depends on two different facts: how many finite credits
are available, and whether any applicable entitlement is unlimited ([ref:entitlement-domain]).

<!-- label: entitlement-domain -->
<!-- figure: assets/entitlement-domain.svg | *The upload-entitlement domain.* A user belongs to one or more groups; a group carries either a finite credit balance or unlimited use. The admission decision needs both facts, so a representation that can express only the finite balance cannot answer the question the decision asks. -->

<!-- point: the-scalar-was-adequate-until-the-domain-changed | DocAble originally had only finite-credit groups, so a scalar sum was an adequate admission representation; when unlimited groups arrived, no explicit model of the admission decision existed to carry the change. -->
DocAble originally represented upload entitlement in finite credits alone. In that system, reducing a
user's groups to a single number — the sum of their credit balances — was adequate for deciding whether
a document could be processed. The product later gained unlimited groups, and the domain change altered
the admission question: entitlement was no longer a single finite quantity. No explicit model of the
upload-admission decision existed at the time. The decision lived in several implementation sites — a
server-side reduction, a client-side preflight check, the server's own admission gate — each answering
it through its own hand-rolled representation of the user's groups.

<!-- point: the-old-reduction-observed-the-distinction-and-discarded-it | The legacy reduction read each group's unlimited flag and used it to exclude unlimited groups from the credit sum; its scalar output had no place for "unlimited," so an unlimited user became zero credits — a representation failure, not missing information. -->
One of those representations did not evolve. In August 2026, a user whose one group carried unlimited
entitlement was blocked from uploading a thirty-eight-page document by a paywall: the upload needs 38
credits, and you have 0. The root cause was a legacy server function that reduced a user's groups to one
scalar, the sum of their finite credit balances. The function was not ignorant of unlimited entitlement.
It read each group's unlimited flag twice, using it once to *exclude* unlimited groups from the sum and
once to compute a display label. Its output type simply had no place to put the fact, so the distinction
died at the return statement. A purely-unlimited user became `0` credits; the client compared 0 against
38 and rejected the upload; the server's authoritative admission logic, which handled unlimited
correctly, was never reached. This was a representation failure, not an information-availability
failure. The implementation held the fact and threw it away.

<!-- point: the-production-failure-produced-the-model | The same-day root-cause analysis made the admission question explicit and produced an explicit entitlement decision model — with property tests, a cross-runtime parity corpus, and a shape lint — in roughly five hours from RCA to deletion of the lossy reduction. -->
The production failure produced the model. The same-day root-cause analysis made the engineering
question explicit — given the user's group entitlements and the requested work, should this upload be
admitted, and under which entitlement? — and by that evening an explicit entitlement decision model had
landed. It did not land alone. The founding change carried property tests, a cross-runtime parity corpus
pinning the server and the client to the same decision table, and a check on the model's shape. About an
hour later the legacy scalar reduction was deleted and the remaining display projections were re-sourced
from the model, with no compatibility shim; later the same evening the admission gates themselves were
delegated to it. From root-cause analysis to model to deletion of the wrong representation took roughly
five hours.

<!-- box-family: model-card -->
> #### Entitlement decision model · Decision
>
> - **Engineering question** — May this user upload this document, and under which entitlement?
> - **Model** — per-group entitlements that keep finite and unlimited as distinct cases; the output is a
>   structured admission decision: admitted or not, the group that authorizes it, and the ordered plan by
>   which the verdict was reached.
> - **Property** — an unlimited entitlement can never be reduced to zero credits; the displayed balance is
>   a projection of the model, not an authority for admission.
> - **Quality attribute** — decision correctness; fidelity between the domain and its representations.

<!-- point: the-repair-was-model-to-model | The repair changed the representation through which the decision was made, not merely the failing conditional: the revised model preserves the finite/unlimited distinction and states the admission decision explicitly, and the implementation was then changed to consume it. -->
The repair did more than patch the conditional that failed. It changed the representation through which
the decision was made. Upload entitlement could no longer be represented as a scalar credit balance: the
revised model preserves finite and unlimited entitlement as distinct cases and represents the admission
decision explicitly — whether the upload is admitted, which group supplies the entitlement, and the
ordered plan used to reach the verdict. The implementation was then changed to consume that model. The
same triggering state that once read as `0` credits now reads as *admit by unlimited entitlement*. The
finite-credit scalar still exists where that quantity is the actual question, displaying remaining
credits, but it is no longer used as a model of upload permission ([ref:entitlement-model-evolution]).

<!-- label: entitlement-model-evolution -->
<!-- figure: assets/entitlement-model-evolution.svg | *The repair, told model to model.* The original scalar model was adequate while every entitlement was finite. The system evolved — unlimited groups arrived — but the admission representation did not, and the new case had nowhere to survive the reduction. The revised model preserves the distinction, and the implementation followed it. Read the figure for the loss and recovery of one distinction, not for repository architecture. -->

<!-- point: the-model-was-part-of-the-repair | The failure revealed a decision made through a representation that could no longer express the system's domain; making the decision explicit forced the representation to preserve the distinction the system had acquired. -->
The model did not document the repair. The model was part of the repair. The failure revealed that an
engineering decision was being made through a representation that could no longer express the system's
domain, and making the decision explicit forced the representation to preserve the distinction the
system had acquired. The opening section defined a model as a purposeful reduction that preserves the
distinctions its question requires. The old scalar was a reasonable model of *how many finite credits
remain*. It was never a sufficient model of *may this user upload*, and when the domain changed, the gap
between those two questions became a production failure.

<!-- point: an-explicit-model-would-have-made-the-obligation-visible | The author changed the system concept in one place and did not carry it into another representation of the same decision; an explicit model would not have prevented the bug, but it would have made the obligation to update the corresponding representation visible. -->
A note in the first person: at the time, I was not maintaining the entitlement decision as an explicit
model. I changed the system concept in one place — groups could now be unlimited — and did not carry
that change into another representation of the same decision. An explicit model would not have made the
change automatically correct. It would have made the inconsistency visible as an engineering obligation:
adding *unlimited* changes the state space of entitlement, and therefore the properties and checks
defined over it. {{chapter:alignment}} gives the recurring pattern here — a failure converted into
explicit structure that later work inherits — a name.

Decision models delimit what is allowed. The next family turns to quantity: how much, and against what
bound?
