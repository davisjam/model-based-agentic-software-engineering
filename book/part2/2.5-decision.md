# Decision Models: Who May Decide or Act?

A decision model represents permitted choices and relationships. Where a structural model says what is
connected, a decision model says what is *allowed* to connect. In DocAble, one such model describes which
services may communicate, and under what conditions.

The implementation contains HTTP clients, endpoints, credentials, deployment configuration, and network
calls. The model reduces that machinery to a declared flow.

> #### Service-flow model · Decision
>
> - **Engineering question** — Which service may call which, and reach which resource?
> - **Model** — a declared graph of services and the permitted edges between them: the public web tier
>   faces the outside; every other node is internal and reached only along a drawn edge.
> - **Property** — an edge means the relationship is part of the declared architecture; an absent edge
>   means the model declares no such relationship.
> - **Quality attribute** — least privilege, architectural integrity, security.

<!-- point: a-decision-model-declares-permitted-relationships-not-observed-ones | The service-flow model declares which service-to-service relationships are intended, turning an architectural permission into a represented fact. -->
[ref:service-flow-model] draws the permitted calls as a declared graph — a short list of arrows. The
public internet reaches the web tier; the web tier reaches the worker, the document editor, and a few
others; the worker reaches generation, render, and OCR. An edge means the relationship is part of the
declared architecture. An absent edge means the model does not declare such a relationship.

<!-- label: service-flow-model -->
<!-- figure: assets/service-flow-model.svg | *DocAble's service-flow model.* A declared graph of which service may reach which. The public web tier is the only node facing the outside; every other relationship is an internal edge the model either draws or withholds. -->

That wording is deliberate. The model tells us what relationships are *intended*. It does not tell us, by
itself, whether an undeclared connection is physically impossible. This gives engineers and agents a much
better question than *does the repository happen to contain a call from A to B?* They can ask instead:
*is A supposed to call B?*

<!-- point: observed-structure-and-declared-structure-answer-different-questions | Observed structure reveals what a repository does; a decision model represents what belongs in the intended system; agreement between them is correspondence, not correctness. -->
The first question is descriptive; the second requires represented intent. [ref:observed-vs-declared]
sets the two side by side: implementation reveals that A calls C; the decision model says whether A *may*
call C. Part III returns to this distinction when it separates correspondence from correctness and
decides which declared relationships should receive enforcement.
