# Provenance Models: What Happened, and What Evidence Records It?

The other families look at the system as it is or as it may be. A provenance model looks backward: it
records what was done, by what, to what, and with what evidence, so that a later question — why is this
artifact the way it is? — can be answered from a representation rather than reconstructed from logs.

## The Canonical Move: Provenance, Attribution, and Derivation

<!-- point: provenance-is-built-on-relations-over-stable-identities | Provenance is built on the most general representational primitive — a relation over stable identities; tuples, entity-relation records, and triples all express that one entity stands in a relation to another, and RDF generalizes the form. -->
Provenance is built on the most general representational primitive there is: a relation over stable
identities. Relational tuples, entity-relation records, and subject-predicate-object triples all express
"this entity stands in this relation to that entity," and RDF-style graphs generalize that form to the
web[cite: w3crdf2014]. The distinction is easy to blur: RDF and triples are *representation mechanisms*,
not provenance models in themselves. They become provenance when the
relations they carry are attribution and derivation relations over stable identities.

<!-- point: the-established-provenance-vocabulary-distinguishes-entities-activities-agents | The established vocabulary distinguishes entities, the activities that act on them, and the agents responsible, connected by typed produced-by, derived-from, and attributed-to edges carrying evidence — a purposeful reduction of history. -->
The established vocabulary for those relations distinguishes entities, the activities that act on them,
and the agents responsible[cite: w3cprovdm2013]. A provenance graph is then a purposeful reduction of
history: nodes are entities, activities, and agents; edges are typed derivation and attribution
relations — produced-by, derived-from, attributed-to — each carrying the evidence associated with its
step. It keeps the derivation and its evidence, not every runtime event.

<!-- point: provenance-makes-lineage-attribution-and-audit-into-traversals | Once history is a typed graph, an attributable producer for every artifact, traceable derivation, and evidence bound to each operation become expressible, and lineage, root-cause, and audit become traversals rather than log-grepping. -->
Once history is a typed graph, the claims an auditor wants become expressible: every consequential
artifact has an attributable producer, derivation chains are traceable, evidence is associated with the
operation it supports, and an explanation corresponds to recorded history rather than being
reconstructed after the fact. The analyses are traversals — backward lineage, forward impact,
root-cause tracing, audit reconstruction, and, where the record carries enough, replay. A question that
would otherwise mean grepping logs becomes a walk over a recorded graph.

## DocAble: Attribution

When an autonomous system changes a user's artifact, completion is not the only fact worth preserving.
Engineers and users may need to know what changed, what changed it, and why. DocAble stamps every
consequential change with structured attribution, so the history is recorded evidence rather than prose
reconstructed after the fact.

<!-- box-family: model-card -->
> #### Attribution model · Provenance
>
> - **Engineering question** — What happened to this artifact, which operation produced the change, and
>   what evidence records it?
> - **Model** — a run made of operations, each naming its target, its mutation, the pass that made it,
>   and the evidence it left.
> - **Property** — consequential operations have structured attribution linking the action, target,
>   mutation, responsible pass, and retained evidence.
> - **Quality attribute** — auditability, debuggability, and consistency between what changed and what is
>   reported.

<!-- point: a-provenance-model-selects-events-that-matter-to-explanation-not-a-full-trace | The attribution model records the events that matter to engineering explanation and audit — target, mutation, responsible pass, evidence — not a trace of every function call. -->
[ref:attribution-model] draws a reduced provenance model: a run contains operations, and each operation
names a target, a mutation, the pass responsible, and the evidence it left. This is not a trace of every
function call. That would reproduce execution rather than model it. The model keeps only the events
needed for explanation and audit.

<!-- label: attribution-model -->
<!-- figure: assets/attribution-model.svg | *Attribution provenance model.* A run contains consequential operations; each operation records its target, mutation, responsible pass, and retained evidence. Audit and explanation can be derived from this shared history. -->

<!-- point: record-once-derive-the-explanations | A human-facing explanation is derived from the structured action record rather than maintained as a second, independently-written account. -->
The system derives its other views from that record. Human-facing explanations are generated from the
structured action history rather than maintained separately. Record once, and derive the explanations.
This keeps what the system did and what its documentation says from drifting apart.

<!-- point: event-grain-is-part-of-the-provenance-model | Choosing the event grain is part of the model — too coarse collapses distinct changes into "document modified," too fine reproduces an execution trace; the useful grain answers what changed, why, and whether it can be reproduced. -->
Recording history is not the same as modeling it. An execution offers far more than an engineer wants to
keep — function calls, allocations, retries, intermediate values, model requests. Capturing all of it
produces an execution trace, not a useful provenance model. The choice of grain is therefore part of the
model. Too coarse, and distinct consequential changes collapse into an opaque "document modified" event;
too fine, and the record becomes another execution trace that must be reconstructed into meaning. For
DocAble the useful grain is the consequential mutation, and the record answers the questions the model
exists for: what changed, why, and can this be reproduced?

## Record, Replay, and Explanation

<!-- point: a-typed-edit-record-replays-deterministically | DocAble's edit record runs — the PDF path records mutations as typed edit values in a per-session log, and a replay engine applies the same sequence deterministically, so the record can both explain and reproduce the remediation. -->
DocAble's edit record has an unusual property for a provenance model: it runs. During a remediation
session the PDF path records document mutations as typed edit values in a per-session log — roughly
thirty variants covering the changes the editor can make. A replay engine applies the same sequence
deterministically, so the record can both explain and reproduce the remediation. Replay is a capability
this particular representation happens to support, not part of what makes a model provenance.

<!-- point: an-executable-record-is-not-the-implementation | Executability does not make the record a complete behavioral model; it still omits the reasoning, analyses, control decisions, and retries that produced the edits, so it says which mutations happened, not why the system chose them. -->
Executability does not make the record a complete behavioral model of the remediation system. It still
omits the reasoning, analyses, control decisions, and retries that produced those edits. The record says
which consequential mutations happened, not why the system chose them.

<!-- point: record-and-replay-separate-three-questions | The computation graph asks what may compose, the provenance record says which mutations did occur, and replay asks whether that history can be reproduced — three separate questions, no one representation subsuming the others. -->
That separates three questions easy to conflate. The computation graph asks what computations may
compose. The provenance record says which consequential mutations did occur. Replay asks a third: can
that realized history be reproduced? No one of these representations subsumes the others.
[ref:two-reductions-static-realized] sets the static structure and the realized history side by side —
the reader's first sight of two heterogeneous models describing the same operation from different sides,
which is the work the next chapter takes up.

<!-- label: two-reductions-static-realized -->
<!-- figure: assets/two-reductions-static-realized.svg | *Computation structure versus realized history.* The computation graph models declared computations and their typed composition; the per-session edit record logs the consequential mutations one run actually produced. They remain separate reductions today. A shared computation identity could join runtime edits to the static model when an engineering question requires that relation, without collapsing the two representations into one. -->

<!-- point: provenance-boundary-model-records-a-lint-guarantees-every-mutator-stamps | The model records attribution per mutation; whether every consequential operation actually stamps is a presence check the environment enforces, and even then the stamp's semantic accuracy is the author's — Part II models the evidence, Part III gives the obligation authority. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
One limit closes the chapter. A provenance model does not guarantee that every consequential operation
actually records its attribution. DocAble addresses that separately, by requiring typed mutation verbs
to emit attribution and checking that obligation structurally. That check is a **hard** presence
guarantee over a **soft** content claim: it can ensure every mutator stamps, but not that the stamp's
semantic account of what it did is accurate — that remains the author's. Part II models the evidence the
operation should leave; Part III asks what mechanism gives that obligation authority.

The next and final chapter of this Part steps back from any single model to ask how the six fit together
as a system.
