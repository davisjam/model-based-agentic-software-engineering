# Lessons Learned

<!-- point: the-takeaway-was-a-methodology | The takeaway was a methodology, not a model. | terms: judgment-into-infrastructure -->
I spent {{study_window}} building a real piece of software with a coding agent, and the thing I
came away with was a methodology, not a model release or a benchmark. I thought the
project would take a weekend; it spiralled to {{total_loc}} lines, written at a peak of
{{peak_week_commits}} commits a week. Somewhere in that spiral the important
questions stopped being *what can the model do* and became *how do I keep it doing the right
thing at speed*. This chapter is what I learned. [ref:lessons-quotable-strip]
gathers the lessons; the sections that follow earn each line.

## Three ways to run an agent, and why I chose one

<!-- section-terms: three-ways-to-run-an-agent, governance-centric -->

<!-- point: three-ways-pick-by-risk-not-taste | Three ways to run an agent; pick by risk. | terms: three-ways-to-run-an-agent -->
<!-- index-def: three-ways-to-run-an-agent -->
There are three ways to run a coding agent, and you choose among them by where you sit on the risk map,
not by taste. The frontmatter distinguished the three stances at length; the practical choice comes down
to consequence and reversibility. **Velocity-centric** — maximum speed, minimal oversight — is right for
a disposable prototype whose only stakeholder is the engineer building it. **Oversight-centric** — a
human reads every step — is right where the work is consequential and human review is still affordable,
though it throws away the speed you brought the agent in for. **Governance-centric** — bound the agent's
operations up front, then let it move fast inside the bounds — is right where volume and consequence
together make per-change attention untenable. That last voice is the quiet one, and it comes from
traditions like safety engineering.

<!-- point: chose-governance-monitor-failures-not-steps | I chose governance: monitor failures, not every step. | terms: governance-centric, governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: governance-centric -->
I chose the third, and the rest of this book is the argument for it. Governance is not a midpoint on
that dial; it changes the reliability mechanism. A well-drawn constraint does not feel like a
compromise because it is the very frame that lets you take your hands off the wheel. If you demand that
the system get built but refuse to read the code, then either the agents work on the first try (they
don't), or you must monitor *something*. I monitored failures and converted what they revealed, and I
encoded the known high-cost obligations before they could fail. When I saw a failure, instead of asking
Claude to fix it, I asked Claude how we could keep it from ever happening again.

## Claude is the fastest road to hell

<!-- section-terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->

<!-- point: agents-accelerate-within-the-frame-even-wrong | Agents accelerate within your frame, even a wrong one. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
Coding agents accelerate *within the
frame you give them* — even when the frame is wrong. For weeks I set the agents optimizing a
service *within* its architecture when the architecture itself was the constraint — the
Kubernetes scale-to-zero dead-end that the {{part:5}} failures retell in full — and the effort
never paid off, because I was hand-building against the platform's own ceiling instead of
changing platforms. The model is both an implementation aid and a way to search a solution
space, and if you do not keep an open mind it will only speed your descent into the circle of
hell you already chose. The tell, in hindsight, was that the optimization kept hitting the
*same* wall: when an in-paradigm fix keeps colliding with the paradigm's own limit, that
recurring wall is the signal to price out a paradigm change.

<!-- point: speed-exposes-the-danger | The speed exposes the danger, it doesn't create it. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
<!-- index-def: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
The speed is not the danger. The speed *exposes* the danger. A bad decision that would have
cost you a slow week of hand-coding now costs you an afternoon and ten thousand lines, all of
them committed, all of them plausible. Velocity is a floodlight on the quality of your
thinking. This is why the frame comes first and why so much of the work is front-loaded. Your
work now happens before the agent types: deciding what "correct" means, and how the code will
be held to it.

## The new economics

<!-- index-def: refactoring-is-free -->
### Refactoring used to be too costly; now it feels free

<!-- section-terms: refactoring-is-free -->

<!-- point: refactoring-was-rationed-now-its-cheap | Refactoring was rationed; now it's an afternoon's work. | terms: refactoring-is-free -->
For my whole career, refactoring was something you rationed. It was expensive, so you lived
with a compromised design longer than you should have, and the compromise compounded. That
economics has shifted — but read the shift precisely. **Refactoring feels free; assurance does not.**
Agent labor made large mechanical transformations cheap enough to attempt in an afternoon. The cost
moved to deciding the target shape and proving the behavior survived. The refactors I had always
deferred as too expensive became an afternoon's work to *attempt*. [data: refactor-cost] In one leap I unified the
remediation interfaces across the document formats, which let me unify the remediation passes, which let
me track what each change does — for corruption checks — and what it costs, for pricing. None of that
was justifiable when the transformation itself meant days of careful hand-surgery.

<!-- point: no-reason-to-enshrine-a-bad-shape | There is almost no reason to enshrine a bad shape. | terms: refactoring-is-free -->
So the threshold for tolerating accidental complexity should rise sharply. If the design is wrong and
the tests can hold the change, change it. The
cost that used to make you tolerate accidental complexity is gone, and the discipline that
replaces it is the willingness to keep paying the now-cheap price. Audit, find the compromise,
fix it, and move on. The prototype is disposable; the learning it produced is what you keep.

<!-- index-def: broken-cost-estimator -->
### The cost estimator is broken, and scope creep is guaranteed

<!-- section-terms: broken-cost-estimator -->

Two consequences follow from cheap implementation, and both caught me off guard.

<!-- point: cheap-implementation-breaks-the-cost-estimator | Cheap implementation breaks your sense of what things cost. | terms: broken-cost-estimator -->
The first is that my sense of what things cost stopped working. Deferred refactors became
cheap; migrations that sounded like weeks took hours. Agentic engineering makes an engineer's
output a function of toolchain, environment, taste, context, and orchestration skill, so the
range widens sharply. "What can one engineer ship this quarter?" gets a far wider spread of honest
answers, and historical estimates built on hand-implementation rates become unreliable.

<!-- point: free-implementation-guarantees-scope-creep | When implementation feels free, scope creep is guaranteed. | terms: broken-cost-estimator, judgment-is-the-scarce-resource -->
The second is scope creep. When implementation feels free, the pull is constant — more features, more
integrations, more scope, and the feeling changes your behavior before you notice. State it as the rule
rather than the slogan: **cheap implementation creates strong scope-expansion pressure unless product
judgment supplies a hard boundary.** Cheap implementation does not just raise output. Every new thread
still has to be decomposed, reviewed, and kept coherent, so the scarce resource stops being code and
becomes judgment. Choosing what *not* to build is now as much of the work as building. Call it taste.

## The failure modes

### Optionality is poison

<!-- section-terms: optionality-is-poison -->

<!-- point: manufactured-optionality-is-poison-at-midnight | AI manufactures optionality, and at midnight it is poison. | terms: optionality-is-poison -->
<!-- index-def: optionality-is-poison -->
AI reduces friction, of course; but what it chiefly manufactures is optionality — an endless
stream of choices, some architecturally critical, some product-relevant, and some stupid
things the agent should have sorted for itself. Optionality is valuable up in the architecture, before
you commit; it turns to poison at the point of execution, where an open choice becomes a choice someone
has to make now. At midnight, that stream is poison. Some
decisions take you to hell, others to a parking lot where you spin your wheels, and others
merely distract you from sleep, and in the moment they are hard to tell apart.

<!-- point: an-unmade-decision-is-where-the-agent-guesses | Every unmade decision is where the agent guesses. | terms: optionality-is-poison -->
The deeper trap is the decision you leave open. When you do, the agent makes it for you —
and it will pick whichever option unblocks it now rather than the one you would have chosen
with the consequences in view. I once signed off on a one-flag fix for a stalling merge-train
cron without pinning down what the flag would do on a conflict; for days it silently deleted
real work from the main branch every time two changes overlapped — the merge-train silent-delete
that the {{part:5}} failures retell in full. The right answer was a merge resolver that understood the files
it was merging, and I could have decided that up front. Instead I left the choice open and the
agent filled it with the cheapest thing that made the error go away. It is the same reflex as
the strings-and-ints story from Part 3 and Part 5: every unmade decision is a place the agent
guesses, and its guesses optimize for the next green checkmark, with your system nowhere in
the objective. So decide — but decide the right things. Not every detail should be predetermined; decide
the dimensions whose downstream consequences matter, and deliberately delegate the rest. Name the
architecture, name the types, name what happens on a conflict. The narrower you make the agent's search,
the more of its speed lands on the target instead of scattering. Explicitness is the mechanism by which
you draw consistency out of a system that will otherwise improvise.

<!-- index-def: done-is-a-claim -->
### "Done" is a claim, not a fact

<!-- section-terms: done-is-a-claim -->

<!-- point: done-reported-but-work-is-half-done | Agents report done while the work stays half-done. | terms: done-is-a-claim -->
[The Skills](4.2-the-skills.html) gave the rule: a report of "done" describes the agent's intent, not the state of your
repository. Two incidents made it non-negotiable — a lint gate that swallowed its own error in a
catch-all and passed a run of unlinted commits while the log said fine, and a chain of pin tests that
ran green against a tree missing the very field they were meant to check. Both said "done"; neither
was. The {{part:5}} failures retell each in full.

<!-- point: trust-nothing-rerun-tests-and-lints | Trust nothing; re-run the tests and lints yourself. | terms: done-is-a-claim -->
So trust nothing at the boundary between the agent's report and reality. Re-run the tests
yourself. Re-run the lints the work was supposed to satisfy. Compare the counts against the
claims. When the agent says "done," your verification has only begun — and the discipline
that catches the gap is mechanical: a gate the agent cannot talk its way past.
My job shifted from writing implementation to running the control system around it —
decomposing work, cross-checking invariants, hardening tests, and deciding whether the
architecture was genuinely cleaner or merely different.

### Your tests must survive agent-shaped failure modes

<!-- section-terms: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->

<!-- point: an-agent-fails-differently-than-a-person | An agent fails differently than a person does. | terms: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
<!-- index-def: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
Agent-produced failures have a different frequency profile than human ones. An agent rarely forgets a
semicolon; it confidently produces a whole subsystem that looks right and is subtly wrong across every
file at once — coherent, cross-file, locally optimized mistakes become the common case. And faced with a
failing check, an agent may select the smallest local edit that turns it green, even when that edit
weakens the intended behavior: delete the tricky behavior, special-case the path that makes the input
work, weaken a validation, bypass a pipeline stage — satisfy the visible symptom while corrupting the
invariant.

One field note from that stretch runs in full among the {{part:5}} failures: a content-free image
detector — clean, typed, careful — that would have dropped a Rothko as decorative because it measured
the right quantity on the wrong world. Its own comment promised a false positive was "not possible," and
that confident "not possible" was the tell — it marks the spot where the model's picture of its own
inputs was too small.

<!-- point: build-tests-for-agent-failure-modes-pin-the-class | Build tests for the agent's failure modes; pin the class. | terms: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
A test suite tuned to human mistakes may systematically miss all of that. So the tests have to be
built for the failure modes the agent actually has. Mine stopped being conventional regression
checks and became sensor barriers: asserting invariants, negative cases, architectural
boundaries, end-to-end semantics. Pin the invariant, not the example. Reach for the strategy
that catches the *class* — a property test where the shape is what matters, a state-machine
check where a lifecycle can tear, a dynamics-aimed test where repetition or concurrency or
stale state is the real risk. The goal is to block the agent from reaching the degenerate
paths through an ambiguous specification.

<!-- point: property-testing-heads-the-generative-family | Property testing heads a family of generative validation. | terms: generative-validation, property-test-models-the-output -->
Of those strategies, the property-based one is the same move
the whole book turns on, worn as a test — a compact, checkable model of what a correct output
looks like, which a generator re-hunts on every commit. It turns out not to stand alone but to
head a whole family of generative validation — property testing, fuzzing, and their synthesis
with the structured model — pulled into its own chapter, [Validation with Agents](4.6-generative-validation.html),
where the three sit together as one move against three specifications.

<!-- index-def: vibe-coding-vs-engineering -->
### Vibe coding treats the program as an I/O device; engineering treats it as a system

<!-- section-terms: vibe-coding-vs-engineering -->

<!-- point: vibe-coding-trusts-io-engineering-checks-structure | Vibe-coding trusts external evidence; engineering checks structure. | terms: vibe-coding-vs-engineering -->
When you vibe-code, you ask the agent to
produce something that appears to satisfy the requested behavior, and the evidence you use is
all external: does the demo run, does the output look right? That works until it doesn't —
until the thing you cannot see is the thing that matters. Engineering asks the agent to
preserve and improve a system's essential properties while changing behavior, and the bar for
evidence includes the internal structure: does it respect the architecture, hold the
invariants, keep state in the right place, stay testable, avoid making the next change harder?

<!-- point: the-difference-is-what-counts-as-proof | The difference is what counts as proof. | terms: vibe-coding-vs-engineering -->
The difference lies in what counts as proof that a change is good. I did not vibe-code a
million-line repo; I could not have. Agents make both modes cheap, which is exactly why you
must choose deliberately — the fast, seductive default is the I/O device, and the I/O device
is what turns a hundred thousand lines into a mess nobody can reason over.

Vibe coding often produces what in Hindi is called *jugaad*: an improvised, resourceful
solution assembled from whatever components are immediately available. Large language models
naturally reach for jugaad-type strategies, opportunistically combining APIs, libraries,
design patterns, and generated code into systems that work surprisingly well. The ingenuity is
real, and it is valuable for exploration. But it rarely yields the architectural coherence, the
explicit invariants, or the long-term maintainability that production software demands. Jugaad is
excellent for discovery. Production engineering begins when the improvisation's successful idea is given
an explicit owner, an invariant, and a maintenance path — which is where MAGE picks it up.

> *Jugaad* (जुगाड़) is a Hindi term for an ingenious improvised solution made from whatever
> materials, tools, or opportunities are available — widely used in India for frugal innovation
> and problem-solving under constraints. English has no exact equivalent; the closest popular
> analogy is "MacGyvering" a solution, though jugaad is broader and often carries a positive
> connotation of frugal ingenuity.

### Explicitness is essential

<!-- section-terms: explicitness-is-essential -->

<!-- point: agents-cant-hold-tacit-structure | Agents can't hold the tacit structure humans keep. | terms: explicitness-is-essential -->
<!-- index-def: explicitness-is-essential -->
A normal codebase keeps most of its conceptual structure in human memory. Humans remember what
the components mean, which files are canonical, which patterns are deprecated, and they make
excellent progress on that tacit knowledge. Agents do not retain the team's tacit architecture across
fresh sessions unless the environment reconstructs it for them. They start cold, carry finite context,
can't sketch on a whiteboard with the senior engineer, and drift toward plausible-but-wrong edit sites.

<!-- point: explicit-artifacts-make-the-system-agent-legible | Explicit, queryable artifacts make the system agent-legible. | terms: explicitness-is-essential, mbse -->
The more the system is represented in explicit, queryable artifacts — component indexes, pass
registries, standards manifests, lints, epics, handoff notes — the more agent-legible it
becomes and the better the agents perform. I began with a soupçon of model-based software engineering —
I made the system architecture explicit and executable, so it was visible to the agents working on it —
and it grew into the system described in Part 2. What lives only in your head is invisible to the
machine, and the machine is now most of your workforce.

## The MAGE discipline

### Governance is a spectrum from soft to hard

<!-- section-terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, audits-into-lints -->

<!-- point: soft-aims-hard-holds | Soft governance aims the agent; hard governance holds. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
<!-- index-def: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
The mechanisms I ended up with sorted along the axis Part 3 named: soft governance *aims* the agent,
hard governance *holds* regardless of cooperation. The operational question here is not what soft and
hard mean; it is when a failure earns promotion from guidance to machinery. A mature environment runs
both, and the skill is reading which a given failure warrants. Not everything earns a wall. But when you
find yourself saying "this must *never* happen," you have just specified a hard mechanism, and you should
build it — a convention that says never is a convention that eventually gets violated at velocity.

<!-- point: second-occurrence-turns-an-audit-into-a-lint | The second occurrence of a failure earns a lint. | terms: audits-into-lints, governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: audits-into-lints -->
The move that makes this compound is turning audits into lints. The first time a failure class
shows up, you inspect for it by hand. The signal that you have a mechanism owed is the *second*
time: a failure that recurs has earned its mechanism, and once you build it, the same failure
is caught on every future agent, automatically, until the mechanism is revised or retired.

<!-- index-def: autonomy-amplifier -->
### AI is an autonomy amplifier

<!-- section-terms: autonomy-amplifier, judgment-into-infrastructure -->

<!-- point: agents-amplify-autonomy-not-typing | Agents amplify autonomy, not typing speed. | terms: autonomy-amplifier -->
The thing agents amplify is your autonomy — your capacity to turn intent into a working
system without waiting on anyone. I have spent years in industry and
never felt more like a real engineer than on this solo project, and I think that feeling came
from autonomy. I had end-to-end ownership: the problem, the regulatory environment, the user
value, the architecture, the correctness conditions, the deployment, the optimizations and
their direct relationship to pricing, and the consequences. Agents lowered the implementation
burden enough that I could inhabit roles usually spread across a team — developer, architect,
DevOps, UX, pricing strategist, support, security analyst.

<!-- point: amplification-is-neutral-judgment-is-scarce | Amplification is neutral; judgment is now the scarce resource. | terms: judgment-is-the-scarce-resource, judgment-into-infrastructure -->
<!-- index-def: judgment-into-infrastructure -->
<!-- index-example: judgment-is-the-scarce-resource -->
That amplification is neutral. It magnifies good judgment and bad judgment with equal
enthusiasm, which is why the scarce resource has moved. Implementation used to be the
bottleneck; now implementation is cheap and *judgment* is the thing in short supply. What is
worth building, what "correct" means here, which failures deserve a wall and which deserve a
note — those are the calls for which the engineer stays accountable, even when an agent supplies the
options and the analysis. So the discipline that pays is the one that converts your judgment into
infrastructure: every decision you make well, you encode once, so you never have to make it again and
neither does any agent after you.

### What this means for the job

<!-- section-terms: judgment-into-infrastructure -->

<!-- point: experience-compresses-the-role-expands | The experience compresses; the role expands. | terms: judgment-into-infrastructure -->
Watching my own experience of software engineering get compressed, I expected to feel the role
shrinking. The opposite happened. The *experience* is being compressed — the hours at the
keyboard, the mechanical translation of a design into syntax. The *role* is expanding. When one
person can stand up hundreds of thousands of lines of governed production code, the
constraint has moved from how fast you can write to how well you can decide, model,
constrain, and verify.

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, judgment-into-infrastructure -->

<!-- point: converting-failures-needs-org-authority | Converting failures into practice requires organizational authority. | terms: governance-conversion, judgment-into-infrastructure -->
This is where I think companies may have trouble. I had authority to change everything — the
architecture, the CI, the documentation, the task format, the release process, the validation
hierarchy. In a real organization, the person who sees the recurring failure may only be
allowed to patch their local problem. That is a recipe for churn: the same class of problem
appears again and again, and no one has the authority to convert it into shared engineering
practice. So to software organizations I offer one piece of advice, and it says nothing about
whether to use agents: look at what your failures are teaching you, and ask who in your
engineering organization is empowered to turn those lessons into architecture, process, and
mechanisms. Put those people in charge of your agentic adoption.

<!-- point: future-is-converting-judgment-into-infrastructure | Engineering's future is converting judgment into infrastructure. | terms: judgment-into-infrastructure -->
The first engineers were locomotive engineers. They stood, literally, astride the harnessed
steam engine — monitored it, tuned it, designed it. The analogy is authority over a powerful system, not
equivalence of disciplines. Software engineering has its DevOps and its SREs in the same lineage, and I
suspect much of what I observed connects to their wisdom. The future of software engineering is the
ability to convert judgment into infrastructure.

<!-- point: solo-field-report-shape-feels-durable | A solo field report; the shape still feels durable. | terms: field-report, judgment-into-infrastructure -->
I will not pretend this is settled. My evidence is a single-person project without real
customers, run against one vendor's models — Claude, Opus and Sonnet, over the
{{study_window}} window — and the specifics will not all generalize. A legacy codebase full of
ancient spaghetti, or a system with many stakeholders, may behave differently; if you are
starting from spaghetti, the honest first move is to fix the spaghetti, because agents are
excellent at refactoring once you have the test sensors to keep them from regressing. But the
shape of it feels durable: convert judgment into infrastructure, let the machinery hold what
your attention cannot, and spend your scarce judgment on the decisions the machinery cannot
make. What this does to the shape of the profession — the roles, the org chart, who is trusted to
build the walls — is the subject of Part 6. Here I will only say that it was the most fun I have had
building software in years: if we want to learn anything new, we have to try crazy things and ponder them.
