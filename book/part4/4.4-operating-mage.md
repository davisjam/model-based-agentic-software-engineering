# Operating MAGE

<!-- point: a-method-must-survive-ordinary-days | A method has to survive ordinary days, not only successful changes. | terms: lifecycle, runbook -->
A method has to survive ordinary days, not only successful changes. Repositories fill disks, branches
conflict, agents disappear, queues stall, tests flake, deployments degrade, and assumptions age.
Engineers already know how to recover from much of this — but often only as tacit operating
knowledge. Autonomous work exposes the cost of leaving that knowledge in someone's head: the disk
fills, and the instruction for what to do about it will never be in the agent's context unless you
put it there.

<!-- point: operating-mage-has-four-connected-forms | Operating MAGE makes recurring work explicit in four connected forms: lifecycles, events, runbooks, and metrics. | terms: lifecycle, runbook, measure-one-level-deeper -->
Operating MAGE makes recurring work explicit in four connected forms. **Lifecycles** model healthy
progress and important failure states. **Events** trigger intervention when relevant transitions
occur. **Runbooks** externalize sanctioned responses and separate execution from judgment.
**Metrics** expose the state needed to judge whether the loop is healthy. Events are the bridge
between modeling the lifecycle and making the environment react.

<!-- point: not-all-operating-structure-is-authoritative | Not all operating structure is authoritative; a lifecycle is a model, an event fires deterministically though its payload may carry judgment, a metric is evidence, and authority appears only where a constraint, validator, or gate acts. | terms: lifecycle, soft-vs-hard-governance -->
Not all of that structure is authoritative. A lifecycle is a model. An event fires deterministically,
though its payload may still carry judgment. A runbook may contain both deterministic tools and
judgment guidance. A metric produces evidence. Authority appears only where constraints, validators,
or gates act on those structures. Keeping those roles separate is what lets the operating environment
remain understandable as it grows.

## Model the Lifecycle

<!-- section-terms: lifecycle, failure-mode-effects-analysis -->

<!-- point: a-lifecycle-is-the-healthy-path-then-a-failure-map | A lifecycle: write the healthy path, then name the failure states that matter and the sanctioned recovery from each. | terms: lifecycle -->
<!-- index-def: lifecycle -->
Start with the healthy path of a recurring activity: how a bug moves from reported to fixed, how a
feature moves from designed to shipped, how an agent moves from dispatched to landed. Then name the
failure states that matter and the sanctioned recovery from each. The result is a lifecycle model:
the states and transitions an operator needs to recognize healthy progress and recover from important
breaks.

<!-- point: fmea-supplies-the-enumerate-failures-instinct | FMEA supplies a neighboring instinct — enumerate failure modes rather than inventing recovery under pressure. | terms: failure-mode-effects-analysis -->
<!-- index-def: failure-mode-effects-analysis -->
**FMEA** — failure mode and effects analysis — supplies a useful neighboring instinct: enumerate
important failure modes before recovery is needed. MAGE represents the states, legal transitions, and
sanctioned recoveries needed to operate the lifecycle.

<!-- point: pair-every-prohibition-with-a-recovery-path | Pair each prohibition with its sanctioned way out; a bare "don't" leaves a stuck agent with no exit. | terms: pink-elephant -->
Pair prohibitions with recovery paths. "Never use this destructive merge command" leaves a stuck
agent with a prohibition and no exit. "When this state occurs, do not use X; use Y because it
preserves the already-applied work" supplies the missing path. Where the prohibition can be made
structural, encode it as authority. Where it cannot, preserve the guidance explicitly.

A prohibition without a recovery path can strand the actor. DocAble encountered this when agents used
a destructive Git escape during stuck merges. The durable rule named the forbidden action and the
sanctioned recovery that preserved already-applied work.

<!-- point: the-fleet-itself-runs-on-a-lifecycle | The fleet's own lifecycle deserves the same treatment as the product: make its legal transitions explicit and validate the ones the environment can decide. | terms: agent-orchestration-model, lifecycle-soundness -->
<!-- index-def: agent-orchestration-model -->
The fleet's own lifecycle deserves the same treatment as the product. Point the discipline at the
substrate that *produces* the software and you get the **agent-orchestration model** — the agent's
life written as a typed state machine (`Dispatched → Working → Landed → Tombstoned`, with `Abandoned`
and recovery), and the orchestrator's refill-and-bank loop as the journey that drives it. The model
makes lifecycle properties explicit, and associated checks assess **lifecycle soundness** by detecting
decidable illegal transitions, such as tombstoning before a required commit. If Dispatched, Working,
Landed, and Tombstoned are meaningful states, make their legal transitions explicit and validate the
ones the environment can decide. The one liveness property — *a landed agent eventually tombstones* —
cannot be falsified by a single bad state, so it instead routes to a temporal checker.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [agent-orchestration model](appendix-b-agent-orchestration-model.html).

<!-- point: dont-trust-a-done-claim-re-run-the-gates | A report of "done" does not establish that the repository entered the required state; re-evaluate the entry condition at the work-unit boundary. | terms: done-is-a-claim, drift-gate -->
That state machine is also why an operator never takes a report at face value. **"Done" is a claim,
not a fact.** An agent reporting a task complete is describing its *intent*, not the state of your
repository, and the two drift apart constantly — a sibling change broke a test, a marker rotted, the
thing it "verified" quietly routed through a fallback. The report therefore should not be confused with
repository state. Where completion is consequential, the environment may re-evaluate the relevant entry
conditions against current state rather than relying on the report alone. In DocAble, the lifecycle
names `Landed` as a state with an entry condition, and the orchestrator re-runs the relevant gates at
HEAD before accepting that transition.

## Attach Reactions to Lifecycle Events

<!-- section-terms: lifecycle, lifecycle-event-binding, deterministic-firing -->

<!-- point: a-lifecycle-says-what-states-matter-events-make-the-right-step-arrive | A lifecycle names the states that matter; binding a reaction to a transition makes the right procedure arrive at the right time. | terms: lifecycle, lifecycle-event-binding -->
<!-- index-def: lifecycle-event-binding -->
A lifecycle model says which states matter. Operation improves when an important transition can also
make the right procedure available at the right moment. Do not rely on an actor to remember to
inspect the lifecycle, notice that a transition happened, retrieve the right procedure, and invoke
it. Where a recurring event is mechanically observable and a standard response is useful, bind the
reaction to the event.

<!-- point: the-firing-can-be-deterministic-while-the-payload-holds-judgment | Binding does not require a mechanical response: the firing can be deterministic while the payload still contains judgment. | terms: deterministic-firing, deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->
<!-- index-def: deterministic-firing -->
A mechanical trigger does not require a mechanical response. **The firing can be
deterministic while the payload still contains judgment.** An agent reaches a context threshold: a
hook deterministically requests a handoff, but the agent must still summarize what matters. A
deployment fails a health check: the event deterministically opens the recovery path, but an engineer
may still decide whether rollback is warranted. A repeated failure is observed: the reflection
procedure runs on its own, but whether that failure deserves a new mechanism stays an engineering
decision.

<!-- point: wire-known-failures-to-sanctioned-reactions-automate-the-arrival-not-the-decision | Wire known failure events to sanctioned reactions rather than waiting for an operator to notice; the aim is to automate the arrival of the decision procedure, not every decision. | terms: lifecycle-event-binding -->
Wiring known failure events to their sanctioned reactions completes the idea: the reaction fires
rather than waiting for an operator to notice the failure. A structured signal surface carries the
events; something reacts to each one, reducing dependence on an operator noticing and polling the
signal manually. **Automate the arrival of the decision procedure.** [ref:event-reaction-fire]
draws the full path from a lifecycle transition to the next state.

<!-- label: event-reaction-fire -->
<!-- figure: assets/event-reaction-fire.svg | *Binding a Reaction to a Lifecycle Event.* A lifecycle transition emits an observable event that fires the reaction deterministically, though its payload still splits three ways — execute a known action, delegate a bounded judgment, or escalate to a human. The firing is mechanical; the procedure it delivers may still hold judgment. [short: Lifecycle event → deterministic fire → execute / delegate / escalate → next state] -->

## Externalize Operational Judgment

<!-- section-terms: runbook, operational-playbook, deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->

<!-- point: a-good-runbook-preserves-how-an-engineer-reasons-not-only-the-commands | A good runbook preserves how an experienced engineer reasons through a recurring situation, without pretending every step has one mechanical answer. | terms: runbook, operational-playbook -->
<!-- index-def: runbook -->
A runbook preserves how an experienced engineer reasons through a recurring situation, including which
steps are mechanical and which require judgment.

> ### Inset — Runbooks
>
> A runbook is a prepared procedure for a recurring operational task or failure. Site reliability
> engineering uses runbooks and incident playbooks to preserve diagnostic and recovery knowledge before
> an outage requires it [cite: beyer2016sre]. An empirical study of real incidents shows how variable
> that recovery work is [cite: sillito2020incident]. Executable runbooks can combine commands, scripts, API
> calls, and manual steps into a repeatable workflow [cite: pagerduty2021runbook].
>
> For agentic operation, one useful addition is to record who or what can decide each step.
> Deterministic steps can execute directly; bounded judgments can be delegated with prepared
> context; irreducible judgments stay with the operator.
>
> Runbooks can drift. Their procedures therefore need the same maintenance discipline as other
> engineering models.

<!-- point: type-each-step-by-who-or-what-can-honestly-decide-it | MAGE types each runbook step by who or what can honestly decide it. | terms: deterministic-vs-judgment-split, runbook -->
MAGE types each step by who or what can honestly decide it.
[ref:externalize-judgment] shows the three kinds and the form each takes.

<!-- point: runbook-steps-split-three-ways-runnable-delegable-human | Runbook steps split three ways: runnable, delegable judgment, human judgment. | terms: deterministic-vs-judgment-split, runbook -->
**Runnable.** One mechanically determined outcome; preserve the executable operation.
**Delegable judgment.** Bounded reasoning supplied with state, choices, criteria, and a required
output.
**Human judgment.** A decision surfaced to the operator.

<!-- label: externalize-judgment -->
<!-- figure: assets/externalizing-judgment.svg | *Externalizing operational judgment.* A deterministic step becomes an executable tool; a bounded-judgment step receives prepared context and a decision procedure; an irreducible judgment is surfaced explicitly to a human. [short: Externalizing judgment — recurring procedure → three kinds (deterministic/bounded/irreducible) → three forms (tool/prepared brief/human)] -->

<!-- point: playbooks-externalize-which-kind-of-reasoning-each-step-requires | A structured operational playbook can serve as a model of engineering practice: it externalizes which kind of reasoning each step requires. | terms: operational-playbook, mastery-skill -->
<!-- index-def: operational-playbook -->
A structured operational playbook can serve as a model of engineering practice. It externalizes not
only what to do, but which kind of reasoning each step requires. It connects directly to the next chapter: a
mastery skill is one way to package exactly this kind of recurring judgment. [ref:runbook-split]
fences the one judgment step between two deterministic ends.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [runbook](appendix-a-operational-playbooks.html).

<!-- label: runbook-split -->
<!-- figure: assets/config-optimization-runbook.svg | *The Fenced Judgment Step.* A run-measure-assess loop wraps a three-step spine: determinize the data space, apply judgment, then determinize the experiment space. Fencing the one judgment step between deterministic ends bounds the messy part and leaves a logged trace a later checker can evaluate. -->

<!-- point: determinizing-buys-a-trace-not-an-explanation | Determinizing a step buys a structured execution trace — evidence of what ran, not an explanation of why the decision was correct. | terms: reasoning-trace, deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->
<!-- index-def: deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->
A structured execution trace records what ran. Preserve rationale separately when judgment selected
the action. The trace can become input to the next run and evidence for an independent checker.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [brief-linting](appendix-a-brief-linting.html).

<!-- point: the-fence-is-no-docable-idiosyncrasy | Systems that share no code reach for the same fence between the step a machine can decide and the step that needs a mind. | terms: deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->
The split is no DocAble idiosyncrasy. Systems that share no code and answer to different regulators
reach for the same fence between the step a machine can decide and the step that needs a mind.

<!-- worked-examples: determinization -->
**Siemens** draws the fence as a clean division of labor across a safety-critical toolchain:
probabilistic semantic reasoning upstream, deterministic physics and verification downstream. The
decidable obligation lands on a deterministic mechanism; the open-ended reading of intent stays with
the model. **Zenseact** hardened three judgments other sites leave probabilistic — execution through
deterministic tools, authority settled by an explicit permission table, context selection decided by
a router rather than a guess. **Docker** determinizes what carries hard evidence — the tests run as
deterministic gates — but keeps admission deliberately probabilistic, an independent reviewer plus a
human, because collapsing that call to a threshold would forfeit the judgment it exists to weigh.
**DocAble** routes structural work (reading a tag tree, checking reading order) to deterministic tools
that leave a replayable trace, and judgment-laden work (describing an image) to a model against a
rubric, because no script can write the alt text.

<!-- takeaway -->
The shared move is *determinization*: separate the steps with a single correct outcome — which earn
an executable tool and a replayable trace — from the steps that need judgment, which should receive
only as much procedure and context as the decision warrants, and govern each by its kind.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

## Measure the State That Matters

<!-- section-terms: measure-one-level-deeper, sensor, resource-mediation -->

<!-- point: operational-control-fails-when-it-acts-on-a-proxy | Operational control fails easily when it acts on a proxy that only sometimes tracks the property of interest. | terms: sensor, measure-one-level-deeper -->
Operational control fails easily when it acts on a proxy that only sometimes tracks the property that
matters. A worker count is not resource pressure. Separate processes are not independent when they
contend for the same machine-wide resource. A marker left on disk is not proof that an actor is
alive.

<!-- point: govern-the-state-that-constrains-the-system-not-the-one-convenient-to-count | Observe and govern the state that actually constrains the system, not merely the state that is convenient to count. | terms: sensor, measure-one-level-deeper -->
<!-- index-def: resource-mediation -->
The general rule: observe and govern the state that actually constrains the system, not merely the
state that is convenient to count. Sometimes the answer is a better sensor. Sometimes it is an
explicit registry. Sometimes it is mediation — when many actors share a resource whose capacity
matters, route access through a control that knows the capacity, rather than asking each actor to
infer whether proceeding is safe.

<!-- point: use-the-simplest-direct-signal-available | Use the simplest direct signal available: govern memory pressure rather than job count, mediate a shared test resource rather than trust process isolation, record lifecycle state rather than reconstruct liveness from side effects. | terms: sensor, resource-mediation, agent-orchestration-model -->
Use the simplest direct signal available.

- **Govern the pressure, not the proxy.** Read memory pressure rather than assuming job count predicts it.
- **Mediate the shared resource.** Route a contended test resource through a mediator rather than assuming
  process isolation provides resource isolation.
- **Record the state, don't reconstruct it.** Write lifecycle state to a registry rather than inferring
  liveness from incidental side effects.

<!-- point: this-is-not-a-demand-for-perfect-observability | This is not a demand for perfect observability; the point is to notice when a proxy drives a consequential decision and the approximation has stopped being good enough. | terms: measure-one-level-deeper -->
Improve the signal when a proxy drives a consequential decision and no longer tracks the relevant
state. From here, measure one level deeper.

<!-- point: an-operating-loop-needs-evidence-about-its-own-state | An operating loop needs evidence about its own state; the useful metric is the one that changes the next engineering decision. | terms: loop-engineering, measure-one-level-deeper -->
An operating loop needs evidence about its own state. A useful metric changes the next engineering
decision. A whole-project cloud bill tells you
what you spent; cost by service may tell you what to change. Aggregate line coverage tells you how
much source ran; claim-level coverage may tell you which invariant your suite never exercised.
Counting AI-assisted pull requests or generated lines tells you activity changed; pairing throughput
with yield and rework [cite: linearb2026] gives a more informative view of engineering outcomes than
activity counts alone.

<!-- point: ousterhouts-rule-measure-one-level-deeper | Ousterhout's rule: measure one level deeper — one level, not arbitrarily deep — where the deeper cut exposes the decision the surface number hides. | terms: measure-one-level-deeper -->
<!-- index-def: measure-one-level-deeper -->
Ousterhout's rule is apt [cite: ousterhout2018]: **measure one level deeper.** One level, not
arbitrarily deep. A metric earns its maintenance cost when the deeper cut exposes an optimization
target, failure class, or decision the surface number hides. An aggregate such as "87% line coverage"
reports activity at a level that may hide the engineering claim that matters — a line in a throwaway
string helper counts the same as a line in the job state machine.

<!-- point: keep-measurement-separate-from-authority | A metric is evidence; a validator may interpret it against a bound and a gate may then act, but the number itself carries no authority. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, sensor -->
Keep measurement separate from authority. A metric is evidence. A validator may interpret it against
a bound; a gate may then act on that verdict. Some evidence is mechanically decidable and appropriate
for automated admission. Other evidence is intentionally judgment-laden and should point a reader
toward a decision rather than masquerade as a threshold.

### One Hard Example: Coverage Measured One Level Deeper

<!-- section-terms: coverage-model-mapping, traceability -->

<!-- point: requirements-based-coverage-measures-at-the-semantic-unit | Requirements-based coverage measures at the semantic unit that matters: whether each modeled claim has exercising evidence, not whether a line ran. | terms: coverage-model-mapping, requirements-based-coverage -->
Requirements-based coverage is one example of measuring at the semantic unit that matters. Join
model-to-code traceability with ordinary coverage and report whether each modeled claim has exercising
evidence. The result names the gap — *this invariant is untested* — instead of hiding it inside an
aggregate line percentage.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [requirements-based coverage](appendix-b-coverage-model-mapping.html).

### One Judgment-Laden Example: Doc-Derived Tests

<!-- section-terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->

<!-- point: a-doc-derived-tests-metric-is-genuinely-soft | A doc-derived test's metric is genuinely soft: whether every consequential prose claim has an adequate test is a judgment, so it should direct review, not drive a threshold. | terms: doc-derived-test -->
Doc-derived tests mark the other end of the spectrum. Whether every consequential prose claim has an
adequate test requires judgment, so the measurement should direct review rather than drive an
admission threshold.

### Let Useful Models Drive Machinery

<!-- section-terms: executable-source-of-truth -->

<!-- point: an-executable-model-becomes-a-query-surface-for-consumers | An executable model earns more than documentation value when tools consume it directly — it becomes a reusable query surface for many consumers. | terms: executable-source-of-truth -->
An executable model earns more than documentation value when tools consume it directly. A
deployment-topology model can tell a latency analysis which services and paths matter. A traceability
graph can tell a coverage tool which code realizes a particular claim. A component graph can help a
validator decide whether a finding is local to the current change or belongs to an unrelated part of
the estate. This is one of Modeling's practical payoffs: the representation becomes a reusable query
surface. It does not itself supply authority — the consuming validator or gate determines what
consequence follows from the model-derived result.

<!-- point: a-component-distance-grade-supplies-evidence-to-a-validator | A model can scope a mechanism: DocAble grades findings by component-model distance from the change, so a local finding blocks while unrelated debt stays a broader gate's responsibility. | terms: component-zone-model, sensor, alignment-principle -->
A model can also scope a mechanism. DocAble grades findings by their distance from the current change
in the component model, letting a local finding block while unrelated debt stays the responsibility of
a broader gate.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [model-graded finding severity](appendix-b-model-graded-finding-severity.html).

<!-- point: the-grade-is-a-model-read-metric-not-an-authority | The grade is evidence read from the model; the gate carries the authority — read the number one level below the surface, where it names a target instead of a total. | terms: component-zone-model, sensor -->
The grade is evidence read from the model at check time; the gate carries the authority. Read the
number one level below the surface, where it names a target instead of a total.

## The Operating Rhythm

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, lifecycle -->

<!-- point: operating-is-one-recurring-rhythm-across-four-forms | Operating MAGE is one recurring rhythm: the lifecycle names healthy progress and failure, events bind the reaction to the transition, the runbook gives each state a sanctioned next move, and metrics tell whether the loop is improving or merely busy. | terms: lifecycle, runbook, measure-one-level-deeper -->
Put the pieces together and operation becomes a recurring rhythm. The lifecycle tells you what healthy
progress and important failure look like. Events bind the reaction to the transition, so the right
procedure arrives when it matters. The runbook gives each state a sanctioned next move and separates
deterministic execution from judgment. Metrics expose enough state to tell whether the loop is
improving or merely busy. When a break recurs, governance conversion asks what later work should
inherit from the lesson.

> ### Inset — When work stalls, diagnose before adding machinery {#inset-when-work-stalls}
>
> Repeated failure does not always call for another control. Before you build one, ask which part of
> the loop is limiting the work.
>
> | What may be limiting | The question | If that is the gap |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Searcher** | Can the available intelligence find a good candidate? | Change the model, tools, decomposition, or search strategy. |
> | **Model** | Is consequential structure missing from the representation? | Make it explicit. |
> | **Oracle** | Can the environment tell good candidates from bad? | Strengthen the evidence, validation, or simulation. |
> | **Target** | Do we know what "good" even means? | Stop building and learn. |
>
> A stronger reasoner does not settle an unknown requirement; another validator does not supply a
> missing abstraction; more modeling cannot answer a product question whose answer must come from users
> or deployment. Diagnose what is limiting the work before adding machinery — the same distinction
> governance conversion already draws between failures of knowledge, obligation, evidence, evaluation,
> and consequence.

<!-- point: the-rhythm-is-still-model-align-convert | The rhythm is still Model, Align, Convert, run day after day; maintain the resulting structure while it pays and retire it when it does not. | terms: lifecycle, governance-conversion -->
This is still Model, Align, Convert. Model the recurring work and its failure states. Give stable
operational obligations appropriate authority. Convert repeated recovery and repeated judgment into
reusable structure. Maintain that structure while it pays; reconcile or retire it when it does not.

<!-- point: the-rhythm-names-three-competencies-part-six-develops | Run the rhythm long enough and the engineer's work concentrates on representation, authority, evidence, and judgment. | terms: modeling-principle, alignment-principle, governance-conversion -->
Run the rhythm long enough and the engineer's work increasingly concentrates on representation,
authority, evidence, and the judgments that determine what the environment should preserve. Part VI
returns to that professional shift.
