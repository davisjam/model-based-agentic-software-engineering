# Migrating to MAGE

<!-- point: most-readers-inherit-an-estate-of-liabilities-and-assets | Most readers inherit an estate that is both liabilities and assets. | terms: brownfield-migration, engineering-capital -->
<!-- index-def: greenfield -->
<!-- index-def: brownfield-migration -->
Most readers do not begin on a blank page. You inherit some combination of code, tests,
schemas, architectural documents, CI rules, operational lore, permissions, dashboards, and
conventions. That estate carries both liabilities and assets: technical debt, certainly, but
also accumulated engineering capital in forms nobody has connected strongly enough to reuse.
Migration is partly **asset recovery** — find the useful representations and controls already
present, reconnect them to their territory, retire what has rotted, and invest where recurring
costs justify stronger structure. That is a richer starting story than "legacy code is spaghetti."

<!-- point: two-independent-axes-fix-the-starting-move | Two independent axes fix the starting move: existing system, settled intent. | terms: brownfield-migration, greenfield -->
Two axes fix the starting move. The first is **how much system already exists**. A mature
estate gives you implementation, history, and latent representation to recover; a new system
does not. The second is **how settled the relevant intent is**. A detailed contract can justify
substantial modeling and authority before implementation; an exploratory product question
cannot. These axes are independent. Greenfield does not imply uncertainty, and brownfield does
not imply settled intent: a blank-page regulated system can carry highly settled obligations,
while a mature product may still be exploring what its users need.

<!-- point: the-cells-prescribe-per-obligation-not-project-wide-governance | The cells prescribe how a body of intent is represented and given authority, not project-wide governance. | terms: brownfield-migration, thesis-alignment -->
[ref:migration-2x2] crosses the two axes. The cells do not prescribe how much "governance" an
entire project deserves. They prescribe how aggressively a *particular body of intent* should be
represented and given authority at the start. The more settled a particular obligation, the more
authority it can safely receive — which is why a security, legal, or safety obligation may earn
enforcement even inside an otherwise exploratory product.

<!-- label: migration-2x2 -->
<!-- figure: assets/migration-2x2.svg | *Two axes fix where a migration starts.* How much system already exists sets the representation and controls you inherit; how settled the intent is sets how much of a given obligation can safely be made explicit and authoritative. The four cells name the proportionate first move — explore, model early, recover then explore, recover reconcile and govern. Governance is not a single project-wide dial: the more settled a *particular obligation*, the more authority it can safely receive, so a security, legal, or safety constraint may be settled even when the product behavior around it is still exploratory. -->

<!-- point: brownfield-enters-through-two-doors-that-converge | Brownfield enters through two doors that converge fast. | terms: brownfield-migration, demoable-vs-productizable -->
Brownfield work enters through many doors, but two are useful extremes. At one end is the
fast-built system whose implementation outran its explicit engineering account: code exists,
perhaps even a working product, but the architecture, obligations, and operating rules were
never made durable. DocAble began near this end — it vibe-coded a plausible product, then
migrated it into a governed one; {{part:5}} diagnoses the gap it was crossing, from
[demoable to productizable](5.1-the-problem-and-the-bar.html). At the other end is the
established organization with years of accumulated structure: ADRs, schemas, ownership files,
developer portals, CI policy, tests, and operational playbooks. Its problem is not absence but
fragmentation.

<!-- point: induce-and-author-versus-recover-and-reconcile | The first estate induces and authors; the second recovers and reconciles. | terms: brownfield-migration, model-as-map -->
The first estate must **induce and author** more of its initial models. The second can
**recover and reconcile** more of what it already knows. They converge quickly because both
eventually face the same four questions: which representations answer recurring engineering
questions, which existing controls already carry useful authority, where do humans still bridge
missing semantics, and what is the cheapest next improvement that pays for itself? Industry is
reaching the same conclusion about the surfaces an organization already owns: a developer
portal's value was never its UI but the curated context underneath — service and API catalogs,
golden-path templates, ownership, CI/CD signals — and agentic engineering turns that existing
human-facing surface into an execution substrate "the portal isn't dying; it's becoming
infrastructure" [cite:redhat2026developerportals].

<!-- point: neither-door-scaffolds-a-complete-governed-environment-first | Neither door scaffolds a complete governed environment before useful work. | terms: governed-environment, brownfield-migration -->
Neither door should scaffold a complete governed environment before useful work begins.
Preserve the obligations already worth preserving, surface the representations already worth
trusting, and let implementation and operation expose what is still missing. The method is
constant; the justified starting stock is not. A safety-critical project may begin with extensive
explicit requirements and heavy validation; a product search may begin with a few non-negotiable
constraints and measurements; a brownfield estate begins with the accumulated evidence of its own
history, however poorly represented.

## Start with what you already have

<!-- section-terms: brownfield-migration, governed-environment, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: survey-the-estate-before-designing-the-replacement | Survey the estate before designing the replacement. | terms: brownfield-migration, model-as-map -->
Survey the estate before designing the replacement. Architecture documents, API specifications,
deployment manifests, ADRs, schemas, ownership files, tests, CI configuration, permissions, and
runbooks are all partial engineering structures. Some are **models**: reduced representations of
the system used for a purpose. Some are already **governance mechanisms**: CI rejects a merge, a
schema excludes an invalid state, a permission denies an action. Many are both weakly connected
and unevenly trusted.

<!-- point: run-two-inventories-in-parallel-representations-and-authority | Run two inventories in parallel: representations and authority. | terms: thesis-alignment, governed-environment -->
So the first brownfield move is two inventories in parallel, not one:

- **What representations do we already reason through?** Which document, schema, graph, test
  structure, or configuration repeatedly saves someone from reading raw implementation?
- **What authority already acts?** Which permissions, types, validators, CI checks, reviews,
  and operational gates currently constrain or admit work — and what semantics can each actually
  see?

<!-- point: mature-orgs-often-have-much-alignment-and-weak-system-modeling | Mature orgs often have much Alignment and weak system-level Modeling. | terms: thesis-alignment, thesis-modeling -->
That second inventory matters because the old claim that inherited artifacts are all
non-authoritative is too broad. A CI configuration may already gate merges; a schema may already
reject data; a type system may already constrain states; tests may already carry admission
authority. Mature organizations often have a great deal of Alignment and weak system-level
Modeling — their human reviewers occupy the semantic gap. The migration target is not "turn every
artifact into a model." It is to connect useful representations to the territory they claim to
describe, make important obligations explicit enough to evaluate, and place appropriate authority
where the necessary semantics are available. The migration strengthens **both inventories**,
rather than mechanically repairing the map first and then installing authority.

<!-- point: treat-the-wiki-you-have-as-an-informal-model | Treat the wiki you have as an informal model, not a knowledge graph. | terms: model-as-map, brownfield-migration -->
A wiki is a useful first path because many organizations already have one and the migration
distance is short. Treat it as an **informal model**: a reduced representation people already use
to reason about the system, even though its semantics are weak and nothing mechanical guarantees
correspondence with the code. Pages supply nodes of a kind, links supply relationships of a kind,
and search supplies navigation. That does not make the wiki equivalent to the structured graph
Part 2 built. It makes it raw material.

<!-- point: the-first-improvement-is-connection-not-replacement | The first improvement is connection, not replacement. | terms: traceability, map-and-territory -->
The first improvement is connection, not replacement. Important claims should point to the code,
tests, configuration, or runtime evidence that realizes them; important implementation regions
should point back to the pages that explain why they exist.[note: The earlier Parts develop the stronger form of this join: every model node points to the code it governs, and that code points back to the model node that explains it.] The resulting trace is not
yet a proof that the prose is true. It is a disciplined join that lets audits and later mechanisms
discover when the map and the territory disagree. [ref:representation-authority] draws the two
independent ways that join can be strengthened.

> ### Inset — Informal, structured, or executable: which kind of model? {#inset-model-stages}
>
> "Model" spans a range of strengths, and this chapter walks up it.
>
> - **Informal model.** A reduced representation you reason over — a diagram, a prose spec, a
>   wiki. Its claimed properties are legible to a human; nothing mechanical maintains them. *The
>   wiki starts here.*
> - **Structured model.** A machine-readable declared shape — typed fields, invariants,
>   dependencies — that a tool can parse. Legible now to an agent and a check, not only a reader.
> - **Executable model.** A structured model a tool actually *consumes* — to derive context,
>   generate checks, drive retrieval, validate, or measure. Traceability and drift controls may
>   keep the model trustworthy, but they are not what makes the representation executable; being
>   *consumed* is.
>
> A wiki is a real model at the first strength — do not wait for the third to start.

<!-- figure: assets/representation-authority.svg | *Strengthening representation and authority are two dimensions, not one.* A brownfield representation can become more structured and more tightly connected to the system — informal to structured to executable — without every claim becoming a gate. Authority attaches to *selected* obligations along a separate track — optional context, to audited correspondence, to obligations placed under enforcement — once the representation is trustworthy enough and the obligation warrants it. The old picture that ended a single ladder at "gate-enforced" collapsed the two; keep them apart. -->

### Audit, synchronize, govern, extend

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: the-migration-runs-in-four-stages-each-a-stopping-point | Four stages, each a useful stopping point. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The migration runs in four stages, each a useful stopping point, so an organization does not have
to reach executable models before the work starts paying for itself.

- **Audit.** Build the join and classify gaps without blocking work. The representation is known
  to drift; making every claim mandatory before repairing it would only canonize old errors.
- **Synchronize.** Use ordinary feature and maintenance work to reconcile nearby claims with the
  current repository. Require disagreements to be surfaced rather than silently choosing prose or
  code as truth.
- **Govern.** Once particular obligations have earned trust, make the relevant checks part of
  acceptance. This may include required analysis of linked material — but that is guidance and
  workflow policy; authority comes from the mechanism that *evaluates or gates* the obligation, not
  from placing the wiki in context.
- **Extend.** Promote high-value claims into more structured representations where a real consumer
  exists: retrieval, analysis, generation, validation, measurement, or gating. **Never add
  metadata without a reader** — a tag, a backlink, or an ownership field earns its place only when
  an audit, a brief, a retrieval step, an analysis, or a gate reads it. Spotify built exactly this
  ground before pointing any agent at its estate: a machine-readable catalog of services and
  owners, each fact wired to a check that reads it so it cannot drift unread.

<!-- point: the-four-stages-compress-to-one-operating-line | The four stages compress to one operating line. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The four stages compress to one line you can keep at the bench: **link the surface → drain the
drift → make synchronization mandatory → extend selected claims into executable models.** The
companion [From-Drifted-Wiki-to-Trusted-Model](appendix-d-from-drifted-wiki-to-trusted-model.html)
operator card holds the drill whole.

## Know the destination, but migrate by useful slices

<!-- section-terms: brownfield-migration, quality-case-traceability -->

<!-- point: the-near-destination-is-a-trusted-map | The near destination is a trusted map over the parts that matter now. | terms: traceability, map-and-territory -->
The near destination is a **trusted map** over the parts of the system that matter now: important
claims have known anchors, important implementation regions have explanatory homes, and agents and
engineers can navigate the join without rediscovering it from scratch. In a bounded subsystem this
can begin paying off within weeks — long before the far destination arrives.

<!-- point: the-far-destination-is-adequate-representation-and-evidence | The far destination is adequate representation and evidence for the quality case. | terms: quality, quality-case-traceability -->
<!-- index-def: quality -->
<!-- index-def: quality-case-traceability -->
The farther destination is not a completely modeled codebase. It is **adequate representation and
evidence for the system's quality case**: enough architecture, behavior, ownership, measurement,
and traceability to answer the properties you actually mean to defend. For any property you claim,
you can point to the code that implements it, the analysis that enforces it, the tests that confirm
it, and the record of every change and why it was made. Some organizations stop at a trusted,
human-facing map. Others promote selected claims into typed or executable models. The right
endpoint is the one **whose future value justifies its upkeep**.

## Migrate one bounded surface at a time

<!-- section-terms: brownfield-migration, top-down-refinement, bottom-up-induction, lint-cover-induce -->

<!-- point: choose-a-bounded-surface-and-keep-old-and-new-coexisting | Choose a bounded surface; let old and new coexist while the replacement earns trust. | terms: brownfield-migration, strangler-fig -->
A live estate rarely tolerates a wholesale cutover. Choose a bounded surface — a subsystem,
service, policy family, or recurring workflow — and let old and new coexist while the replacement
earns trust. A **strangler** migration and a **pilot cell** are two versions of the same
operational principle: *bound the experiment, preserve reversibility, and generalize only what the
slice proves.* DocAble's serverless re-platforming ran this shape — a stateful job pipeline moved
off long-lived polling workers onto instances that scale to zero behind a managed push queue; the
push plane grew beside the poll plane behind a default-off toggle, proved green on a staging
namespace running identical code, took the traffic, then the old cluster was deleted and preserved
on an archive branch.

<!-- point: recover-the-representation-in-whichever-direction-the-evidence-supports | Recover the representation top-down, bottom-up, or clean-and-induce. | terms: top-down-refinement, bottom-up-induction, lint-cover-induce -->
<!-- index-def: top-down-refinement -->
<!-- index-def: bottom-up-induction -->
<!-- index-def: lint-cover-induce -->
Within that slice, recover the representation using whichever direction the evidence supports.
**Top-down** work starts from intended architecture or requirements and refines toward
implementation. **Bottom-up** work induces stable abstractions from the code and rises toward a
model. **Clean-and-induce** first removes accidental complexity — under tests — so the latent
abstractions become legible enough to recover; agents induce models from code far more reliably
when the code presents named abstractions and shallow control structure. Real migrations use all
three, and the directions should meet at the grain where the relevant property becomes answerable.

<!-- point: a-disagreement-has-the-familiar-three-way-diagnosis | A model-code disagreement has a three-way diagnosis: model, grain, or code. | terms: model-drift, generative-validation -->
A disagreement then has the familiar three-way diagnosis — the representation is wrong, the grain
is wrong, or the implementation is wrong. Do not decide which in advance; shifting a legacy
codebase from "the tests pass" to "these properties hold across all behaviors" surfaces
disagreements by design.

> ### Inset — When the model and the code disagree {#inset-three-outcomes}
>
> Tighten a model against real code and it will, sooner or later, contradict the code. A contradiction is not
> a failure of the method; it is the method working. It means exactly one of three things:
>
> - **The model is wrong.** It claims something the code never promised. Refine the model.
> - **The model is at the wrong grain.** It is asking a question at a level of abstraction that cannot see the
>   answer. Build a different model at the grain the property lives at.
> - **The code is wrong.** The model is right, the code disagrees, and the modeling just found a bug.
>
> The discipline is to decide *which* before you act. Fixing code when the model was wrong bakes in a false
> claim; refining the model when the code had a bug hides the bug behind an adjusted spec.

<!-- index-def: control-substrate-dependency -->

> ### Inset — Compute the blast radius before you replace the ground {#inset-blast-radius}
>
> **Substrate-dependency model · Structural** *(compressed here; full card in the appendix)*
>
> One question comes before you re-pave a bounded surface everything else runs on: *which
> mechanisms assume the substrate I am about to replace?* Have each governance mechanism declare,
> as typed metadata, the substrate assumption it bakes in — bound to one substrate,
> substrate-aware, or substrate-agnostic. Joining those declarations against the topology model on
> a migration target turns a hopeful grep into a *computed* blast radius; a declaration lint
> requires the stance, so the one mechanism whose assumption is implicit cannot hide from the join.
> Worked once on the plane the fleet actually migrated: retiring a poll plane for a push plane, the
> join named every mechanism that baked in "workers pull from a shared sorted set" — the
> queue-depth metric, the stale-job sweep, the cancellation cleanup, the priority-preservation
> requeue — before the change landed.
>
> *Property: a migration's blast radius is computed from declared stances, not grepped for by hand.*

## Introduce new authority safely: Audit → Drain → Promote

<!-- section-terms: make-lint-blocking, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: a-valid-future-invariant-is-not-always-a-valid-present-admission-rule | A valid future invariant may not be a valid present admission rule. | terms: make-lint-blocking, thesis-alignment -->
A valid future invariant is not always a valid present admission rule. A legacy tree may violate
the rule everywhere on the day you first decide the rule is worth having. Turning it blocking
immediately does not improve the estate; it merely breaks all work already in flight. So introduce
a new obligation over an inherited estate in three beats — and the order is the safety.

> ### The move — Audit → Drain → Promote {#audit-drain-promote}
>
> - **Audit.** Install the proposed rule as evidence only. It reports every existing violation and
>   blocks no commit, so it never breaks an in-flight agent.
> - **Drain.** A fix wave repairs the existing findings under sufficient tests and other evidence to
>   preserve intended behavior, converting the legacy code to the new convention behavior-for-behavior.
> - **Promote.** Once the estate satisfies the obligation, grant the mechanism blocking authority so
>   new violations cannot re-enter through the governed boundary.

<!-- point: audit-drain-promote-is-an-authority-migration-pattern-not-a-dependency | Audit->Drain->Promote is an authority-migration pattern, not a Modeling->Alignment dependency. | terms: thesis-alignment, thesis-modeling -->
This is an **authority-migration pattern**, not a universal Modeling→Alignment dependency. The
representation or checker may already exist — you can hold permissions, tests, and gates without an
explicit system model. The point is temporal: establish what the future obligation will be, bring
inherited reality into conformance, then make that obligation authoritative. Cloudflare reached the
same **approved → enforced** order independently, landing each policy control advisory and flipping
it to enforced once the tree is clean, so the promotion never breaks work in flight
[cite:reimann2026codex]. [ref:squash-zero-promote] draws the three beats as one lint is worn into a
legacy tree.

<!-- label: squash-zero-promote -->
<!-- figure: assets/squash-zero-promote.svg | *Audit, Drain, Promote.* A new lint lands audit-only — every finding reported, no commit blocked, so it never breaks an in-flight agent. A fix wave drains it to zero, and only then is it promoted to blocking, so the class of smell can never return. -->

## Measure what the map still cannot explain

<!-- section-terms: missing-model-metric, coverage-model-mapping -->

<!-- gloss-only: Missing model metric | A code->model discovery instrument that walks each test to the production code it exercises and asks whether any of that code traces up to a model node; a test that reaches none is an orphan, and the orphan set names where a model is still missing. The dual of coverage: you drive it down by adding a model, not a test. -->

<!-- point: model-induction-has-a-stopping-problem | Model induction has a stopping problem; traceability run backward finds the edges. | terms: missing-model-metric, node-coverage -->
Model induction has a stopping problem: a representation can describe everything it knows without
telling you what it omitted. Traceability can provide a useful discovery instrument. Walk
model → code and you can ask whether represented claims are exercised by tests. Walk code → model
and you can ask which exercised behaviors trace to no relevant model claim. A test whose code
reaches no model node is an **orphan**; cluster the orphans by the unmodelled code they share, and
you hold a candidate list of the models you have not written yet.

<!-- point: treat-orphans-as-candidates-not-automatic-defects | Treat orphans as candidates, not automatic defects. | terms: missing-model-metric, orphan-test -->
Treat the orphans as **candidates**, not automatic defects. Some expose a genuinely missing model;
some are missing *anchors* to a model that already exists; some sit legitimately below the model's
chosen grain. The instrument earns its keep only if it distinguishes those cases well enough to
produce an actionable backlog — otherwise it flags every unmodelled line and drowns you.

<!-- point: the-method-level-point-is-instrumented-choice-of-the-next-model | Use the omissions to choose the next modeling investment. | terms: missing-model-metric -->
DocAble used such a reverse trace to drive a brownfield modeling loop; {{part:5}} reports the drain
and its limitations, including the honest caveat that this is a solo field report, not a controlled
before-and-after. The method-level point is smaller: **when you can instrument what the
representation omits, use the omissions to choose the next modeling investment instead of modeling
whatever happens to attract attention.** A companion mechanism closes the loop from the other side,
projecting your test coverage onto the model's nodes so an untested invariant shows up as a visible
gap rather than hiding inside a line-coverage percentage — the catalogue names it
[coverage-to-model mapping](appendix-b-coverage-model-mapping.html).

## Price the investment

<!-- section-terms: right-level-of-enforcement, teetering-tower, engineering-capital -->

<!-- point: price-the-expected-loss-against-the-cost-of-building-and-carrying-the-response | Price the expected future loss against the cost of building and carrying the response. | terms: right-level-of-enforcement, broken-cost-estimator -->
A recurring gap does not automatically deserve machinery. Price the expected future loss against
the cost of building *and carrying* the response. Frequency and consequence are useful first
approximations; detectability, reversibility, legal exposure, and the stability of the obligation
can move the decision substantially. Cheap, rare failures often remain judgment. Cheap, frequent
failures may justify a lint, script, or reusable procedure, because repeated agent and human
attention is itself cost. Consequential failures justify a **larger investment and a stronger
assurance response**: better representation, prevention by construction where possible, stronger
sensing and validation, and authority at an appropriate boundary. Catastrophic or legally
intolerable failures need not recur even once to earn that investment.

<!-- point: draw-the-two-factors-as-axes-and-the-judgment-gets-a-shape | Draw cost against frequency and the sizing judgment gets a shape. | terms: right-level-of-enforcement -->
[ref:governance-sizing-matrix] crosses how much a failure costs against how often it happens, and
each cell names the proportionate move — the smallest change that still closes the class. The
reflex to reach for a control on every failure is the expensive habit; float the larger scheme,
and let the cost of the failure justify building it.

<!-- label: governance-sizing-matrix -->
<!-- table: *The Sizing Matrix.* Failure cost against failure frequency, with the proportionate move named in each cell — the smallest change that still closes the class. Reach grows with the product of the two. Leave the cheap-and-rare corner to judgment, since a mechanism there is the first brick in the teetering tower. A cheap-and-frequent failure earns a lint or script, because the tokens agents burn working around it are real cost. In the costly column, prefer architectural prevention — make the mistake impossible — but keep a gate as the immediate floor and permanent backstop. The costly-and-frequent corner earns both. A final row: any cell escalates one tier when the failure is hard to detect, hard to reverse, or legally intolerable. [short: Sizing the mechanism] -->
| Cost × frequency | Rare failure | Frequent failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Cheap** | Leave it to judgment. A mechanism here is the first brick in the teetering tower. | A cheap lint or script. It trips agents often, and the tokens they burn working around it are real cost. |
| **Costly** | A runbook with a named escape; a blocking gate once the failure's shape is decidable. | **Prevention first where feasible:** a structured model that makes the mistake impossible, with a gate as the immediate floor and permanent backstop. This corner earns both. |
| **Any cell — if hard to detect, hard to reverse, or legally intolerable** | Escalate one tier. | Escalate one tier. |

<!-- point: price-the-upkeep-capital-depreciates-and-can-be-retired | Price the upkeep — engineering capital depreciates and can be retired. | terms: engineering-capital, teetering-tower, right-level-of-enforcement -->
Price the **upkeep** too. A model must be reconciled, a validator recalibrated, a lint maintained,
a skill updated. Engineering capital depreciates. A control that once retired meaningful cost can
become technical overhead when the obligation disappears or the maintenance burden exceeds the
return — the first brick in the teetering tower. **Build the smallest asset that retires the
recurring cost, and be willing to retire the asset when it stops paying.** Migration at fleet scale
puts the same discipline under a bigger multiplier: a fleet-wide change that once drew in hundreds
of teams for weeks can be scoped by one engineer over a few days, the tooling targeting and
scheduling the work while agents implement it concurrently [cite:spotify2026honk]. Scale does not
undo the incremental discipline; it raises the payoff.

<!-- point: the-estate-was-matured-in-place-not-rewritten | The estate was matured in place, not rewritten from a clean slate. | terms: brownfield-migration, governed-environment -->
DocAble's month-long retrofit is the deep case behind this chapter — an agent told to type Python
that reached for `string` everywhere, a 300,000-line prototype pared to 200,000 modeled lines lint
by lint and model by model. {{part:5}} follows the migration itself; here the relevant point is
that the estate was **matured in place** rather than rewritten from a clean slate. That is the move
any team can run at any starting rung: begin from the environment you already own — a drifted wiki,
a heap of spaghetti, whatever partial models and controls you already hold — inventory both what it
represents and what it already governs, and mature both in place until an agent can reason through it.

### Example — DocAble
A janky 300,000-line prototype started from a heap of spaghetti — the lowest rung — and was matured

waiting for a clean estate to arrive. {{part:5}} follows the month in full.

The move is *bootstrap-governance*: begin from the environment you already own — a drifted
wiki, a heap of spaghetti, whatever partial models and controls you already hold — and mature it in
place, one rung at a time, until an agent can reason through it, rather than waiting for a clean one
to arrive.
