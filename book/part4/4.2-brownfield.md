# Migrating to MAGE

<!-- point: part-4-puts-the-method-to-work | This Part puts the method to work. | -->
You have the mindset, the governed environment, and the models; this Part puts them to
work, and it starts where most readers actually stand.

<!-- point: brownfield-is-migrating-legacy-spaghetti-to-a-model-based-environment | Brownfield migrates legacy spaghetti to a model-based environment. | terms: brownfield-migration, greenfield -->
<!-- index-def: greenfield -->
<!-- index-def: brownfield-migration -->
Everything so far has described the method as though the hard part of starting were the existing system. That is only one kind of difficulty. A greenfield system has little inherited technical structure, but it may also have little settled intent. A brownfield system may be architecturally tangled while its obligations are understood in exquisite detail.

Treat those as separate axes. *How much system already exists?* determines what representation you inherit. *How much do you know about what the system should become?* determines how aggressively you should specify and govern it.

[ref:migration-2x2] crosses the two axes and names the proportionate first move in each corner. Read down a column and the move shifts with how much code you inherit; read across a row and it shifts with how settled the intent is.

<!-- label: migration-2x2 -->
<!-- figure: assets/migration-2x2.svg | *Two axes fix where a migration starts.* How much system already exists sets the representation you inherit; how much of the intent is settled sets how hard you should specify and govern. The four cells name the proportionate first move — explore, model early, recover then explore, recover then govern. The settled column is shaded stronger than the uncertain one because governance intensity should track how much you know: the more settled the intent, the more specification and enforcement the start earns. -->

A greenfield effort with a detailed contract, regulatory obligations, and known interfaces sits in the top-right corner and can model heavily from the start. A product experiment driven by a customer's vague dissatisfaction sits in the top-left and should not — build enough to learn, preserve the constraints you already trust, and promote discoveries into models as they stabilize. Greenfield gives you freedom from inherited implementation; it does not give you foreknowledge.

Brownfield fills the bottom row: much of the system already exists, often along with years of requirements, tests, operational experience, and institutional knowledge. Where the intent is still open, you recover the latent structure and then explore; where it is settled, you recover and then govern. Either way the work is to recover, reconcile, and strengthen those latent models rather than inventing the system anew.

<!-- point: two-doors-into-migration-vibe-coder-and-established-org | Two doors open on this chapter: the vibe-coder and the established org. | terms: brownfield-migration, model-as-map, demoable-vs-productizable -->
Readers reach the bottom row through one of two front doors. The doors diverge at the very
first move, then converge on the same path.

Through the **first door** comes the vibe-coder. You have a demoable MVP an agent built fast,
and no wiki, no schemas, no ADRs — nothing written down to build a model *from*. The engine
room runs, but no one has said what it is supposed to do. Your first move cannot be to surface
a model, because none is recorded. It is to induce the first model *from the running code* and
retrofit governance onto it afterward. DocAble came through this door: it vibe-coded a plausible
product, then migrated it into a governed one. That order is the credential here, not a
confession. {{part:5}} diagnoses the gap you are crossing — a vibe-coded novel system is
[demoable, not productizable](5.1-the-problem-and-the-bar.html) until an engineer gives it
the account it never had. That chapter names the gap; this one prescribes the path across it,
MVP to product.

Through the **second door** comes the established org. You have at least a wiki, at most ADRs,
schemas, CI config, and ownership files — *latent models* already sitting in your artifacts,
waiting to be extracted — and you are just as curious how to migrate. Your first move is not to
induce a model from nothing. It is to surface the model already latent in the surfaces you own,
then strengthen and govern it. The rest of this section follows that door in detail, because its
distance is shortest, but the two doors meet quickly. Martin Davidson makes the point sharply:
enterprises still run on structures built for a world in which writing code was expensive, even
after that assumption stopped holding [cite: riggins2026] — which is why the established org so often
owns latent models it has never been pressed to surface.

Neither door scaffolds a full governed environment before useful work begins. Nor should a greenfield team. MAGE is not a demand to finish the specification, model, tests, schemas, linters, and gates before the first feature appears. Bootstrap what you already know is worth preserving; then let implementation and use expose what you did not know. The governed environment grows with the system.

What differs by setting is the starting stock. A safety-critical or contract-driven project may begin with extensive explicit requirements and therefore justify substantial modeling and validation before implementation. A product search may begin with only a few non-negotiable constraints and measurements. A brownfield estate begins with the accumulated evidence of its own history, however poorly represented. The method is constant; the amount of structure justified at the start is not.

<!-- point: you-probably-already-have-latent-models | You probably already have latent models. | terms: model-as-map, brownfield-migration, governed-environment -->
The unhelpful way to read that estate is against the state of the art: you look at a company
that already runs a model-based platform, count what you lack, and conclude the gap is the
lesson. It is not. You are not starting from nothing, and you do not have to start from an
estate you envy first. You are starting from what you already have — and you probably already
have *latent models.* Your architecture document, your API specification, your deployment
manifests, your ADRs, your schema definitions, your ownership files, and your CI configuration
are each a partial model of the system: each keeps some structure an engineering purpose needs
and drops the rest. None is authoritative today — each drifts, and none is mechanically tied to
the code. So MAGE's first brownfield move is not to build a model estate from scratch. It is to
survey the representations you already own and decide *which of them should become authoritative*
— which partial model, made trustworthy and joined to the code, would answer a question you keep
re-answering by hand. That is where you start Monday morning: from the Git history, the CI config,
the wiki, the docs, and the agents you already run, not from a platform you have to acquire before
the method can begin.

<!-- point: brownfield-starts-from-the-wiki-you-already-have | Brownfield starts from the wiki you already have. | terms: governed-environment, brownfield-migration -->
This chapter follows one of those latent models the whole way, and it picks the wiki on purpose,
because the distance is shortest. That codebase comes wrapped in documentation: tests of uneven
quality, an architecture known mostly by reputation, and a body of writing everyone consults but
nobody quite trusts. Usually that writing is a wiki. It matters because of the distance involved. The
path from code to a complete executable model is long. The path from code to a *better
wiki* is short. The wiki already exists, engineers already know how to use it, and everyone
already agrees it should describe the system more accurately than it does. So most
engineering organizations already own the *raw material* of a governed engineering environment,
one where engineering knowledge and policy live in shared models, tools, and checks instead
of in a few people's memory. They just call it the wiki — and it is raw material, not a finished
environment. This chapter starts there. It turns
a drifted wiki into a linked and increasingly trusted picture of the system, then carries
that picture toward the stronger models and mechanisms the earlier Parts built.

Industry is reaching the same conclusion about the surfaces an organization already owns.
Red Hat observes that a developer portal's value was never its UI but the curated context
underneath — service and API catalogs, golden-path templates encoding architecture and
compliance, ownership, CI/CD signals — and that agentic engineering turns that existing
human-facing surface into an execution substrate simply by adding a programmatic (MCP)
access layer: "the portal isn't dying; it's becoming infrastructure"
[cite:redhat2026developerportals].

<!-- point: mage-rests-on-two-moves-modeling-and-alignment | MAGE rests on two moves: modeling, then alignment. | terms: thesis-alignment, governed-environment -->
Brownfield adoption applies both of MAGE's theses incrementally — the daily loop's first two
beats, run rung by rung: repair the map first — make the system's intent and structure explicit
in a representation an agent can reason through — then install the mechanisms that keep the map
and the code from drifting apart.

> ### Where this chapter fits
>
> This chapter builds both from what you already have. The wiki is an accessible first model;
> links and audits give it traceability; lints and completion checks hold the repaired
> relationship in place; selected high-value claims later graduate into structured, executable
> models.

<!-- point: brownfield-is-climbing-the-capability-ladder-from-where-you-stand | Brownfield is climbing the capability ladder from where you stand. | terms: brownfield-migration, governed-environment -->
Part 2 taught the **capability ladder** — the climb from context selection, through externalized
knowledge and structured relationships, up to explicit invariants, model-derived analysis, and
drift enforcement. Part 2 teaches that ladder; this chapter is how you climb it when you start
from a brownfield estate instead of a blank page. So brownfield does not ask "how do I install
MAGE." It asks two smaller questions: *where on the ladder am I now, and what is the cheapest
next rung that solves a real reasoning problem?* You do not begin by building a complete
executable model. You start at the lowest rung that pays, and you strengthen the representation
only when a recurring engineering question earns the next one.

*In other words:* don't climb for the view. Take the next rung when a question you keep
re-answering by hand would stop recurring once the representation could answer it for you.

## Start with the model you already have

<!-- section-terms: brownfield-migration, governed-environment -->

<!-- point: treat-a-drifted-wiki-as-an-immature-model | Treat a drifted wiki as an immature model. | terms: model-as-map -->
Do not dismiss a drifted wiki as failed documentation. Treat it as an immature **model** —
a cheaper representation of the system that keeps the structure some engineering purpose
needs and drops the rest.[note: The wiki is an informal model — a reduced representation you reason over, not yet the machine-readable, mechanically-maintained kind Part 2 admits as executable.] A wiki qualifies. It is already a lightweight
knowledge graph, which means you do not start at the bottom of the capability ladder Part 2
defined — you already stand on its lower rungs. Its pages are nodes, its links and backlinks
are edges: the externalized knowledge and structured relationships Part 2 put at the base of the
climb. Its tags and categories carry loose metadata, and its search makes all of it navigable.
The semantics are weak, but the graph is already there — the crude version of the model you
want, and the rung you climb from.

> ### Inset — Informal, structured, or executable: which kind of model? {#inset-model-stages}
>
> "Model" spans a range of strengths, and this chapter walks up it — the same climb Part 2's
> capability ladder lays out at finer resolution. These three bands coarsen its eight rungs:
> informal sits at the lower rungs, executable at the top.
>
> - **Informal model.** A reduced representation you reason over — a diagram, a prose spec, a wiki. Its
>   claimed properties are legible to a human; nothing mechanical maintains them. *The wiki starts here,*
>   on the ladder's externalized-knowledge and structured-relationship rungs.
> - **Structured model.** A machine-readable declared shape — typed fields, invariants, dependencies — that a
>   tool can parse. Legible now to an agent and a check, not only a reader: the ladder's system-model and
>   explicit-invariant rungs.
> - **Executable model.** A structured model a tool actually *consumes* — to derive context, generate checks,
>   or gate drift — and that is kept tied to the running system. This is the strong sense Part 2 admits, the
>   ladder's top: model-derived analysis, traceability, drift enforcement.
>
> A wiki is a real model at the first strength — do not wait for the third to start.

<!-- point: what-the-wiki-lacks-is-a-disciplined-join-to-the-territory | The wiki lacks a disciplined join to the territory. | terms: traceability, map-and-territory -->
What the wiki lacks is a disciplined join to the territory. A page describes a service but
never names the code that implements it. A test guards an invariant but never links the
decision that says why the invariant matters. A subsystem runs in code with no explaining
page at all. The graph floats beside the repository instead of joining it.

<!-- point: the-first-brownfield-move-is-to-connect-the-wiki-not-replace-it | First move: connect the wiki, don't replace it. | terms: traceability -->
So the first brownfield move is not to replace the wiki. It is to connect it. Establish
traceability as a round trip: the important wiki claims point down to the code and tests
that realize them, and the important code and test regions point back up to the pages that
explain their purpose. This is traceability in the book's specific sense — a two-way join,
not a heap of hyperlinks.[note: The earlier Parts develop the stronger form of this join: every model node points to the code it governs, and that code points back to the model node that explains it.] [ref:wiki-linkage] draws the join, and draws why the wiki you have
and the model you want are the same shape at different strengths.

<!-- label: wiki-linkage -->
<!-- figure: assets/wiki-linkage.svg | *The wiki is the model you already have.* Pages, links, and backlinks already form a graph; the first move joins it down to code and tests, the same round-trip join a structured model carries. Audit, Synchronize, Govern, and Extend mature the left panel into the right. -->

<!-- point: a-round-trip-join-lets-an-audit-sort-every-gap-into-three-cases | Round-trip links let an audit sort every gap three ways. | terms: missing-model-metric, traceability -->
Once the links run both ways, an audit can sort every gap into one of three cases:

1. a missing page or model;
2. a missing anchor to a page that already exists;
3. code that sits legitimately below the representation's grain — too fine or too generic
   to earn a page of its own.

The distinction keeps the audit honest. The goal is not one wiki entry per function. The
goal is a map whose claimed surface agrees with the territory it represents.

### The minimum package

<!-- section-terms: governed-environment, governance-package -->

<!-- point: four-small-additions-turn-the-wiki-into-an-engineering-environment | Four additions turn the wiki into an engineering environment. | terms: governed-environment -->
Four small additions turn the wiki from optional documentation into the start of an
engineering environment. Add all four, or the wiki stays a surface nobody trusts: together
they are the **minimum viable governed environment**, the smallest thing that earns the name.

- **Bidirectional links.** Wiki pages name the code and tests that back their claims; code
  and tests name the pages that explain their purpose.
- **Agent briefs.** Agents are told how to reach the wiki, how to walk its links and tags,
  and when a page's claims should shape the task in front of them.
- **Design templates.** New work names the wiki pages it touches, and records when a
  missing page has to be created.
- **Definition-of-Done checks.** Before work is accepted, the affected pages are read
  against the current repository, not trusted because an agent reports it updated them.

<!-- point: never-add-metadata-without-adding-consumers | Never add metadata without adding consumers. | terms: governance-package -->
One rule governs all four: **never add metadata without adding consumers.** A tag, a
backlink, an ownership field, a machine-readable mirror — each earns its place only when an
audit, a brief, a retrieval step, an analysis, or a gate reads it. Metadata with no reader
is one more surface free to drift. Spotify built exactly this ground before pointing any
agent at its estate: a machine-readable catalog of services and owners, each fact wired to a
check that reads it so it cannot drift unread.

### Audit, synchronize, govern, extend

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: the-migration-runs-in-four-stages-each-a-useful-stopping-point | Four stages, each a useful stopping point. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The migration runs in four stages. Each one reaches a useful stopping point, so an
organization does not have to arrive at executable models before the work starts paying for
itself.

<!-- point: audit-builds-the-graph-and-stays-an-audit-not-a-gate | Audit builds the graph and stays advisory. | terms: governance-conversion, missing-model-metric -->
**Audit.** Spend a bounded set of agent sessions building the graph. Inventory the major
wiki regions, find the code and tests each one claims to describe, add the links in both
directions, and create or propose pages for the large implementation regions with no
explaining home. The exit criterion is coverage of the surface that matters: every major
wiki region links to the code and tests that realize it, and every major code and test
region is linked, marked below the wiki's grain, or recorded as owing a new entry. This is
an audit, not a gate. The wiki is known to drift; making it mandatory before repairing it
would only promote old errors to official instructions.

<!-- point: synchronize-drains-drift-with-a-boy-scout-rule | Synchronize drains drift with a boy-scout rule. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**Synchronize.** Once the graph exists, drain the drift with ordinary feature and
maintenance work. An agent that touches linked code or tests reads the attached pages,
checks their claims against the implementation, fixes the nearby factual errors, adds the
missing links, and flags the disagreements that need a human to settle. This is a boy-scout
rule, not a whole-wiki rewrite: leave the linked surface no less accurate than you found it.
The exit criterion is a fresh audit that shows the important wiki claims and the repository
substantially in agreement, with no major orphan left unclassified.

<!-- point: govern-promotes-the-wiki-from-optional-context-to-required-input | Govern promotes the wiki from optional context to required input. | terms: thesis-alignment, make-lint-blocking -->
**Govern.** After the wiki earns trust, promote it from optional context to required input.
Agent briefs now call for task-specific wiki analysis before implementation. Design
templates name the pages the work should touch. The Definition of Done requires the relevant
claims to be checked against the repository at HEAD. The promotion order is the one this
chapter later applies to lints: audit first, drain the findings, then make it mandatory.
This is the Alignment Thesis applied one increment at a time — an obligation that used to
depend on someone remembering to update the wiki becomes part of the environment's
acceptance criteria.

<!-- point: extend-adds-structured-fields-only-where-a-consumer-exists | Extend adds structured fields only where a consumer exists. | terms: governed-environment, executable-source-of-truth -->
**Extend.** A trusted wiki can stay a human-facing model for as long as that serves. Where
the value justifies the cost, selected pages go further. Add structured correctness
criteria, invariants, dependencies, ownership, allowed transitions, evidence obligations,
and links to the validators or tests that discharge them. Mirror those fields in a
machine-readable form once a real consumer exists. The data can then drive task-specific
context retrieval, drift analysis, test-obligation derivation, semantic lints, or admission
gates. The exit criterion is not "the wiki holds structured metadata." It is sharper: every
structured field added during extension drives at least one retrieval path, analysis,
generator, validator, or gate.

The four stages compress to one operating line you can keep at the bench: **link the surface
→ drain the drift → earn trust → make synchronization mandatory → extend selected claims
into executable models.** That single sequence is the drill; the companion
[From-Drifted-Wiki-to-Trusted-Model](appendix-d-from-drifted-wiki-to-trusted-model.html)
operator card holds it whole for the day you run it.

## The starting point and the destinations

<!-- section-terms: brownfield-migration, quality-case-traceability -->

<!-- point: name-the-starting-point-and-both-destinations-before-moving | Name the starting point and both destinations before moving. | terms: brownfield-migration -->
Name the starting point and both destinations before you move.

The starting point is familiar: some spaghetti, a drifted wiki, thin or uneven tests, and
invariants that live only in one person's head — the person you hope never retires.

<!-- point: the-first-destination-is-a-trusted-linked-map | The first destination is a trusted, linked map. | terms: traceability, map-and-territory -->
The first destination is a trusted map. The major wiki claims link to the code and tests
that realize them; the major implementation regions link back to the pages that explain
their purpose; agents read and maintain those joins during ordinary work. In a bounded
subsystem, this can begin paying off within weeks — long before the far destination arrives.

<!-- point: the-far-destination-is-models-code-and-a-traceable-quality-case | The far destination: models, matching code, a traceable quality case. | terms: quality, quality-case-traceability, map-and-territory -->
<!-- index-def: quality -->
<!-- index-def: quality-case-traceability -->
The far destination is the full model-based environment the earlier Parts built — an
aspirational maturity state, not a normal stopping point. It is not complete representation
of the codebase; it is adequate representation of the claims your quality case depends on —
the views that bear on performance, correctness, security, or privacy you actually mean to
argue. You want code that reflects those models, so the map equals the territory over the
relations the model claims to represent. Zoom in on the map and you see county lines, not Switzerland. And you want **traceability** for your quality case, the ability to state the
argument automatically. For any property you claim, you can point to the code that
implements it, the static analysis that enforces it, the protocol you model-check it
against, the tests that confirm it across the inputs you try, and the record of every change
and why it was made. With that in hand, soft and hard governance hold the whole thing in
place and keep it current.

<!-- point: the-wiki-path-gives-the-three-approaches-somewhere-to-meet | The wiki path gives the three approaches somewhere to meet. | terms: top-down-refinement, bottom-up-induction, lint-cover-induce -->
The wiki path does not replace the three migration approaches that follow. It gives them
somewhere to meet.

- **Top-down** work contributes the intended architecture, the requirements, and the
  correctness claims.
- **Bottom-up** induction finds the structure the implementation actually holds.
- **Lint and coverage** make the code legible and safe enough for the two to converge.

The wiki is the first shared surface where those views can be compared. Some organizations
stop at a trusted, linked, human-facing map. Others keep going, pulling the highest-value
claims into typed, executable, drift-gated models. Either way, a real migration uses all
three approaches, so take them one at a time.

## Migration strategies: the moves both doors share

<!-- section-terms: brownfield-migration, governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: migration-strategies-are-org-playbooks-both-doors-run | The strategies are organizational playbooks both doors run. | terms: brownfield-migration -->
Whichever door you came through, the migration runs on a small set of organizational
strategies. These are not architectural idioms about how to structure the system you build.
They are playbooks for how an *estate moves* while it keeps running. Five carry most
migrations, and one shape holds all five. Each box below names the doors it serves; the
strategy the two doors converge on — where the vibe-coder's induced model and the established
org's surfaced model meet — is Brownfield-Model-Extraction.

> ### Migration Strategy — Strangler-Fig · *serves both doors; the shape the others run in*
>
> - **Problem.** A live estate cannot be rewritten in one cutover. You have to move it while
>   it keeps serving traffic.
> - **When to use.** Any re-paving of a running path — a deployment substrate, a queue plane, an
>   ungoverned module. Less a strategy you pick than the shape the others move in.
> - **Approach.** Martin Fowler's three beats. **Coexist:** build the new path beside the old,
>   landed behind a switch that is off by default. **Route around:** shift traffic and trust one
>   increment at a time, the old path staying the fallback. **Eliminate:** once nothing depends
>   on the old path, delete it — code, config, and the failure class it carried — keeping an
>   archived copy for a cold rollback.
> - **Tradeoffs.** Buys safety and reversibility at the price of a period running two paths. It
>   needs a real toggle and a proof the new path is green before it takes traffic.
> - **Example (DocAble).** The serverless re-platforming. A stateful job pipeline moved off
>   long-lived polling workers and a hand-built autoscaler onto instances that scale to zero
>   behind a managed push queue. The push plane grew beside the poll plane behind a default-off
>   toggle, proved green on a staging namespace running identical code, then took the traffic;
>   the old cluster and its scaler were deleted, preserved on an archive branch. This chapter's
>   own **Audit → Drain → Promote** drill is the same shape in miniature — a lint coexists
>   audit-only, a fix wave routes the tree onto the clean path, then the lint turns blocking.

> ### Migration Strategy — Brownfield-Model-Extraction · *both doors converge here*
>
> - **Problem.** You inherit a system that *implies* models nobody has drawn, and you cannot
>   model all of it at once.
> - **When to use.** The default path for most readers. Extract just enough model to stand up
>   one governance mechanism on one slice, then repeat.
> - **Approach.** Carve one capability. Give it a model — top-down from intent, bottom-up from
>   code, or lint-cover-induce where the code is too messy to read (the three approaches below).
>   This is where the two doors meet: the vibe-coder induces that first model from the running
>   code, the established org surfaces it from the wiki, and from there both run the same loop.
>   Wire one mechanism to the model — a boundary lint, a drift gate — leave the rest, and let the
>   **Missing-Model Metric** name the next slice. Keeping the model alive with a drift gate is a
>   step *inside* this loop, not a separate strategy.
> - **Tradeoffs.** Slow and un-heroic; you never get a "whole system modeled" milestone. But
>   every slice pays before the next begins, and the orphan metric keeps you from choosing slices
>   by only what you happen to see.
> - **Example (DocAble).** A zone model extracted from the codebase replaced four drifting
>   allowlists with one typed field and unlocked three lints. The Missing-Model-Metric loop then
>   drove a subsystem's orphan rate down wave by wave and caught real shipping bugs the passing
>   tests had missed — a subprocess exit code misfiled, a chunk leaked on redelivery, a
>   last-write-wins editor race. {{part:5}} runs the full account.

> ### Migration Strategy — Governance-Conversion · *serves both doors*
>
> - **Problem.** Failures recur. Patching the instance leaves the class open for the next agent
>   to rediscover.
> - **When to use.** The moment a failure appears a *second* time — the recurrence is the signal
>   — or the moment you catch yourself saying "this must never happen again."
> - **Approach.** Run the six-step conversion loop: a failure, a diagnosis one level below the
>   symptom, a generalization to the class, the smallest mechanism that closes the class, the
>   retirement of the class, and the engineering capital left behind. The output varies with the
>   failure — a type, a lint, a gate, or a banked human decision rule, where judgment rather than
>   machinery is what recurs. Each conversion mints the control the next one needs, so the capital
>   compounds: deposit a little governance every sprint and the estate governs more of itself each
>   month.
> - **Tradeoffs.** Spends judgment once, up front, to save it forever; the discipline is refusing
>   the quick patch. Soft guidance that aims versus a hard mechanism that holds is a real choice —
>   not every failure earns a wall.
> - **Example (DocAble).** An agent told to "use types" satisfied the letter and made everything
>   a `string`. The conversion was to induce a structured model per dense primitive region and
>   drive every commodity lint to blocking as it hit zero — a month of governance conversion at
>   brownfield scale, told below in the field note and in full in {{part:5}}.

> ### Migration Strategy — Surface-Before-Authority · *both doors, especially the established org*
>
> You cannot gate what the environment does not yet represent, and premature enforcement breaks
> in-flight work. So promote every implicit rule in one order: **represent** it (a model, a
> metadata field, a lint landed audit-only) → **drain** its findings to zero → **promote** it to
> authority (a blocking gate, a required input). Never add metadata without a consumer; never flip
> a lint blocking while findings remain — flipping first would break every in-flight agent's commit
> at once, so the ordering *is* the safety. DocAble runs it as the wiki's Audit → Synchronize →
> Govern → Extend stages and as the Audit → Drain → Promote lint-landing drill this chapter
> elevates below. Cloudflare reached the same **approved → enforced** order independently: it lands
> each policy control advisory, then flips it to enforced once the tree is clean, so the promotion
> never breaks work in flight [cite:reimann2026codex].

> ### Migration Strategy — Pilot-Cell · *both doors*
>
> A whole-org or whole-system transform is too risky and too slow to learn from. Stand up one
> governed enclave instead — a single subsystem, service, or bounded corpus — run the full
> discipline there, measure, and generalize only what the pilot proves. A cheap pilot that kills a
> bad path is a win, and the measurement gates the real decision. DocAble piloted the serverless
> move on one narrow subsystem, explicitly not a broad migration, gated on a missing cost-and-latency
> measurement, and ran the Missing-Model Metric over its single best-modeled subsystem before
> generalizing.

## Approach one: top-down, from a whiteboard

<!-- section-terms: top-down-refinement, model-drift -->

<!-- point: top-down-starts-coarse-and-refines-to-diminishing-returns | Top-down starts coarse, refines until diminishing returns. | terms: top-down-refinement, model-as-map -->
<!-- index-def: top-down-refinement -->
Start coarse and refine. Your first model can be as crude as "on this input, the test
suite passes and we get the right output" — and you should be able to write that today.
From there you ask what happens *between* input and output: insert a file, and out comes a
score, a spreadsheet, whatever your program produces; insert a picture, and out come the
cats and antelopes it found. Refine until the model captures the abstractions you need,
and stop when you hit diminishing returns — the territory is the territory, and the map
only has to be detailed enough to carry the assurance you are after.

Siemens runs the industrial-scale version of this move: an engineer states intent in plain
words and a deterministic solver, not the reasoner, returns the verdict on whether the part
survives the load — routing the decision through an owned model, not the code.

<!-- point: every-model-code-mismatch-means-one-of-three-things | Each model-code mismatch is refine, remodel, or bug. | terms: model-drift, generative-validation -->
You can seed this from a literal whiteboard: photograph the diagram, hand it to the agent,
and have it produce a data-flow diagram in whatever representation you like. Then iterate
against the code — "what nodes am I missing, what flows am I missing?" — and tighten the
policies as the model sharpens: static assertions ("we never call `exec`"), then the
invariants of each module, then the properties preserved across module interactions. Every
disagreement the tightening turns up resolves one of three ways — refine the model, remodel
at a different grain, or fix a real bug (see the inset). All three are legitimate moves, and
shifting a legacy codebase from "the tests pass" to "these properties hold across all
behaviors" surfaces disagreements by design.

> ### Inset — When the model and the code disagree {#inset-three-outcomes}
>
> Tighten a model against real code and it will, sooner or later, contradict the code. A contradiction is not
> a failure of the method; it is the method working. It means exactly one of three things:
>
> - **The model is wrong.** It claims something the code never promised. Refine the model.
> - **The model is at the wrong grain.** It is asking a question at a level of abstraction that cannot see the
>   answer. Build a different model at the grain the property lives at.
> - **The code is wrong.** The model is right, the code disagrees, and the modeling just found a bug.
>
> The discipline is to decide *which* before you act. Fixing code when the model was wrong bakes in a false
> claim; refining the model when the code had a bug hides the bug behind an adjusted spec.

## Approach two: bottom-up, inducing the model from code

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | Approach two: bottom-up, inducing the model from code — one tight argument (define what 'correct' means, pick the abstraction that enforces it cheaply, then a worked audit-trail example); 14 words over across 3 short paragraphs, and breaking the drone-climb-to-worked-example arc would cost the reader more than the length does. -->

<!-- section-terms: bottom-up-induction, fitness-function -->

<!-- point: bottom-up-induces-the-model-from-code-upward | Bottom-up induces the model from code upward. | terms: bottom-up-induction, model-as-map -->
<!-- index-def: bottom-up-induction -->
The second approach starts from the code and flies upward. Ask the agent to induce the
model: "look at the codebase and help me develop the model inductively." Where the first
approach starts from your coarse whiteboard understanding and refines *down* toward the
code, this one starts on the ground and rises. Think of standing on a street and flying a
drone up: the individual buildings blur, the street grid appears, then the highways, then
the cities. As you climb, the levels of abstraction reveal themselves — and whether you
came top-down or bottom-up, you are trying to meet in the middle, at the level that lets
you enforce the properties you care about.

<!-- point: the-abstraction-level-follows-from-defining-correctness | Define correctness first; it fixes the abstraction level. | terms: fitness-function, correctness-conditions -->
<!-- index-def: fitness-function -->
Which level that is depends on the assurance you need. Finer models expose more
implementation detail and often support stronger local claims, but the right grain is the
coarsest one that can express the property you need — and a finer model costs more to write
and to run without always buying a stronger guarantee. For low assurance, just run the tests — the coarsest model is
"this input, that output, checked dynamically." For the strongest guarantees, you need
elaborate analyses over the control-flow graph and every function's inputs and outputs —
expensive, because the checker must search every acre of the whole county. So begin by
writing down what *correct* even means, because correctness is not one thing: it means one
thing for security, another for semantics, another for privacy, and the definition tells
you the abstraction level you need. Memory safety demands a fine-grained model that sees
every access — and you will pay for it. A semantic property is often far cheaper.

<!-- point: a-high-abstraction-property-is-cheap-to-enforce | A high-abstraction property is cheap to enforce. | terms: fitness-function, wiring, ban-lint -->
Think of an audit trail on a spreadsheet — an accountant proving the books were not cooked:
"every modification is stamped." You do not model every function to enforce that. You just require that every code path which modifies the
ledger calls the stamp routine on the way out — a property about the *order* of two calls
in the control-flow graph, not about what either call does. That is a high level of
abstraction and therefore cheap to enforce. The companion catalogue ships this exact
pattern as a product sensor: a lint that checks every format-mutating routine is wired to
its provenance stamp, so an unstamped mutation cannot ship. The catalogue names this
mechanism the [provenance-stamp wiring lint](appendix-c-f10-wiring-lint.html). Proving the
stamp routine *itself* is correct would need a much finer model — but if a human review or a test covers
that, the coarse model is enough. Choosing the fitness function, then the abstraction that
enforces it cheaply, is the new job, and it varies by codebase. It is yours to think
through.

## Approach three: lint, cover, then induce

<!-- section-terms: lint-cover-induce, code-smell -->

<!-- point: clean-the-code-before-inducing-a-model-from-it | Clean the code before inducing its model. | terms: lint-cover-induce, code-smell, loop-engineering -->
<!-- index-def: lint-cover-induce -->
Both approaches assume you can make sense of the code — and often you cannot, because it is
a mess. Agents induce models from code more reliably when the code presents named
abstractions, rationale, and shallow control structure. Deep nesting has to be held in
working memory, and an agent pays for that context just as a human pays in confusion. So before you
extract models from spaghetti, improve the code — and there is a loop for that, the same
loop from [Engineering the Loop](3.1-the-agent-stack.html). Define the inputs, the judgment, the output, and the metric. The
metric here is a **smell detector**.

<!-- point: lints-measure-smells-then-fix-them-under-test-coverage | Lints measure smells; fix them under test coverage. | terms: code-smell, lint, line-coverage -->
<!-- index-def: code-smell -->
<!-- index-def: lint -->
A smell is code that works but will break the moment it is touched, because no one can
reason over it. You measure smells with cheap static analyses — **lints** (the dryer-lint
kind, the stuff you pull out to make things tidy) — that parse the syntax tree and count
the tells: dense bare `int`s and `string`s, deep nesting, a giant dispatch, unnamed
many-element tuples — the presumptive smells of hand-maintained code. Exempt the legitimate
cases explicitly, since a generated parser or a real dispatch table can each be large for a
good reason. So run the lints, and have the
agent fix them in a behavior-preserving way — which means you need tests. If you have none,
start there: have the agent write tests for line or branch coverage, module by module.
Coverage supplies a floor for refactoring; the behavioral assertions and invariant checks
inside those tests determine how trustworthy the floor is. Use the tests and the lints
together as your success metric.

### Audit, Drain, Promote

<!-- section-terms: make-lint-blocking, thesis-alignment -->

> ### The move — Audit → Drain → Promote {#audit-drain-promote}
>
> A new rule enters as an *observation,* not a wall. Three beats, and the order is the safety:
>
> - **Audit.** The rule lands advisory. It reports every existing violation and blocks no commit,
>   so it never breaks an in-flight agent.
> - **Drain.** A fix wave drives the violations to zero under test coverage, converting the legacy
>   code to the new convention behavior-for-behavior.
> - **Promote.** Only now does the rule turn blocking, so the class it names can never return.
>
> One migration truth sits underneath: **a valid future invariant cannot always be imposed as a
> valid present invariant.** The rule you want tomorrow would break the tree today; the sequence is
> how you get from one to the other without an outage. It carries both of the book's theses in three
> beats — the audit establishes the modeled discrepancy, the promotion gives the obligation authority
> — which is why it is worth keeping as a phrase you say at the bench: *audit, drain, promote.*

[ref:squash-zero-promote] draws the same three beats as one lint is worn into a legacy tree.

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | Squash, zero, promote — the reported count is inflated by the figure's descriptive a11y caption; the prose body pairs the make-it-blocking move with the audit-only-first ordering the figure draws, and reads as one arc that ends the three-approaches thread. -->

<!-- figure: assets/squash-zero-promote.svg | *Audit, Drain, Promote.* A new lint lands audit-only — every finding reported, no commit blocked, so it never breaks an in-flight agent. A fix wave drains it to zero, and only then is it promoted to blocking, so the class of smell can never return. -->

<!-- point: promote-each-lint-to-blocking-once-it-reaches-zero | Promote each lint to blocking once at zero. | terms: make-lint-blocking, thesis-alignment, coverage-model-mapping -->

Worn in this order, the practice compounds. Once a lint is blocking, the success metric becomes
"all tests pass *and* that lint still passes" — no more unnamed tuples, no more imports buried
mid-function — and each promotion bounds what any later agent can reintroduce, the Alignment
Thesis worn into a legacy tree one lint at a time. The catch worth stating: a lint only catches
the syntactic class it recognizes, so a smell reintroduced in a shape the lint does not match
still needs a human to see it. What you have earned when the wave finishes is a codebase with
enough shape that model induction has something to grip — classes and enums and named structs
where spaghetti hid the structure. A companion mechanism closes the loop from the other side: it
projects your test coverage onto the model's nodes, so an untested invariant shows up as a visible
gap rather than hiding inside a line-coverage percentage. The catalogue names it
[coverage-to-model mapping](appendix-b-coverage-model-mapping.html).

### The metric that points at what you missed

<!-- section-terms: missing-model-metric, coverage-model-mapping -->

<!-- gloss-only: Missing model metric | A code->model discovery instrument that walks each test to the production code it exercises and asks whether any of that code traces up to a model node; a test that reaches none is an orphan, and the orphan set names where a model is still missing. The dual of coverage: you drive it down by adding a model, not a test. -->

<!-- point: the-missing-model-metric-points-at-unmodelled-code | The missing-model metric points at unmodelled code. | terms: missing-model-metric, coverage-model-mapping -->
Induction gives you a first model, but it never tells you where it stops. You built the map
of the concurrency-critical spine and the durable-state contracts; the rest of the code sits
below the horizon, and nothing in the model announces its own edges. You need an instrument
that points at the blank spots. The **Missing Model Metric** is that instrument, and it runs
as the dual of coverage: **operationally, it reverses the traceability walk** — coverage walks
model→code, this walks code→model.

#### Coverage, run backwards

<!-- section-terms: missing-model-metric, node-coverage -->

<!-- point: an-orphan-test-reaches-code-tracing-to-no-model | An orphan test reaches code tracing to no model. | terms: missing-model-metric, orphan-test, node-coverage -->
Coverage walks one direction: model to code. For each thing you modelled, find its code and
ask whether a test touches it — that finds the *modelled but untested*. The Missing Model
Metric walks the other way, code to model. For each test, reach the production code it
exercises and ask whether any of that code traces up to a model node — an invariant, a state
machine, a service edge. A test whose code reaches no model node is an **orphan**. Cluster
the orphans by the unmodelled code they share, and you hold a candidate list of the models
you have not written yet.

<!-- point: you-move-this-metric-by-adding-a-model | You move this metric by adding a model. | terms: missing-model-metric, orphan-test -->
The duality is what makes the metric a modeling instrument rather than a
testing one.

*Coverage measures implementation. Orphan rate measures understanding.*

You move a coverage number by adding a test. You move this one by adding a
**model**. An orphan is not a hole in your test suite; it is a hole in your map. So the metric
seeds the modeling backlog directly: it reads out where the induced model still owes the
system a view.

#### Data from the case study

<!-- section-terms: missing-model-metric -->

<!-- point: the-pilot-found-a-granularity-gap-not-a-scolding | The pilot found a granularity gap, not failure. | terms: missing-model-metric, granularity-gap, orphan-test -->
A pilot makes the shape concrete. Run it over the most heavily-modelled slice you have — for
this system, the dispatch and orchestration subsystem, across {{missing_model_pilot_test_count}}
tests — and you would expect near-zero orphans, because if any subsystem traces cleanly this one
should. It reported **{{missing_model_pilot_percent}} of those tests as orphans:** more than
half the tests in the best-modelled corner reach production code that traces to no invariant,
state machine, or service edge. The percentage counts *tests*; the clustering that follows
regroups those orphan tests by the unmodelled *code* they share.
A second pass sharpened that from a scolding into a map. Every orphan reached code the model
*did* know — anchored at the coarse structural level, where a component owns a directory — but
that carried no fine behavioral model. The structural map was complete; the behavioral map
covered the concurrency-critical spine and stopped. The metric had found a **granularity gap**,
and it named exactly where to close it: three genuine candidate models — the durable-state data
layer, the subprocess-and-cost-rollup pipeline, the chunk merge-and-validation gate.

<!-- point: the-metric-separates-missing-model-missing-anchor-and-below-grain | It separates missing model, missing anchor, below-grain. | terms: missing-model-metric, symbol-anchor, orphan-test -->
What earns the instrument its trust is that it discriminates. Two of the clusters it surfaced
were *not* missing models. One was code below the model's grain — typed-id aliases, config
loaders, enums — code that carries no behavior of its own to model. The other was a missing
*anchor*: code that belongs to a node the model already has, just lacking the link back to it.
The metric separates the three: missing model, missing anchor, and legitimately below the grain.
Without that discrimination it would flag every unmodelled line and drown you. With it, the
orphan clusters read as a work queue you can act on.

<!-- point: run-on-its-own-codebase-the-metric-caught-real-bugs | Run on its own codebase, it caught bugs. | terms: missing-model-metric, dogfooding -->
That single reading is the introduction; the metric earns its keep only when you *run it as a
loop* — drain an orphan cluster by adding its model, re-measure, take the next cluster the
number surfaces. [Operating MAGE](4.5-operating-mage.html) shows that loop as an operating
instrument, the orphan rate falling wave by wave as the behavioral map fills in. I ran it on my
own system as the last rung of the road in [The Road to MAGE](5.3-the-road-to-mage.html), where
it drove that loop, closed the first several clusters, and caught real shipping bugs the passing
tests had missed — the brownfield recipe applied by the codebase to itself.

<!-- point: a-real-migration-uses-all-three-approaches-together | A real migration uses all three approaches together. | terms: top-down-refinement, bottom-up-induction, lint-cover-induce -->
You will need all three approaches in combination, run inside the strangler shape — the old
estate keeps working while the governed one grows up around it. There is no magic in them, only
hard work and judgment. But applied together, and scaled to the quality of your starting point,
they land you at the destination: the right models, encoding the right views, with the
right correctness criteria, traceable to where each is enforced — statically, dynamically,
or by a model check.

### The brownfield loop, in five moves

The three approaches run as one repeating loop, and it is short enough to keep on a card:

1. **Find the stable abstractions** — the spine the system's quality case actually rests on,
   not every function.
2. **Model only those** — top-down from intent, bottom-up from code, meeting at the grain the
   property lives at.
3. **Drift-gate the model** — a lint or check that fails when code and model disagree, worn in
   Audit → Drain → Promote order.
4. **Measure the orphans** — run the Missing-Model Metric; each orphan cluster is the next
   model you owe.
5. **Repeat** — the metric hands you the next stable abstraction, and the loop turns again.

This is the *modeling* loop, and it is the ladder-climb made concrete — one rung per turn, taken
when a recurring question earns it, never for the view; the *wiki* track that feeds it — Audit,
Synchronize, Govern, Extend — is compressed on the companion [From-Drifted-Wiki-to-Trusted-Model](appendix-d-from-drifted-wiki-to-trusted-model.html) operator card.

I owe you honesty about the evidence here. This is a solo field report, not a controlled

### How much governance is enough

<!-- section-terms: right-level-of-enforcement, teetering-tower -->

<!-- point: govern-by-cost-times-frequency-of-the-failure | Govern by failure cost times its frequency. | terms: right-level-of-enforcement, broken-cost-estimator -->
Which raises the real question: how much governance is enough? There is no formula. There
is a judgment, and it turns on two factors multiplied together — how much a failure *costs*
you, times how *often* it happens. The costlier and the more frequent the
failure, the harder the mechanism it earns — a blocking lint, a gate, a structured model that
makes the mistake impossible. Low times low you leave to judgment; the middle band is where
taste lives, and where you will spend it.

<!-- point: size-by-cost-times-frequency-architecture-before-controls | Size by cost times frequency; architecture before controls. | terms: right-level-of-enforcement -->
Draw the two factors as axes and the judgment gets a shape. [ref:governance-sizing-matrix]
crosses how much a failure costs against how often it happens, and each cell names the
proportionate move — the smallest change that still closes the class. Two of the cells carry
the part people skip. In the costly column, **prefer architectural prevention — make the
mistake impossible** — but use a gate as the immediate floor and a permanent backstop where
architecture is slow to arrive or cannot fully foreclose the risk. And the costly-and-frequent
corner earns *both* — the structured model that forecloses the mistake and a gate that holds
the line while the model is still spreading. The reflex to reach for a control on every failure
is the expensive habit; float the larger scheme, and let the cost of the failure justify building it.

|---|---|---|
| **Cheap** | Leave it to judgment. A mechanism here is the first brick in the teetering tower. | A cheap lint or script. It trips agents often, and the tokens they burn working around it are real cost. |

The litmus for when to stop falls out of the same two axes.

> ### Inset — When to stop: price the upkeep {#inset-sizing}
>
> Price the mechanism's own upkeep before building it. A control guarding a failure that is both rare and
> cheap costs more attention to maintain than the failure ever costs you — the first brick in the teetering
> tower [The Skills](4.4-the-skills.html) warns against. Reach for the smallest change that closes the whole
> class, and float the larger scheme until its cost is justified.

### Example — Spotify
Migration at fleet scale stays incremental: you target a change, schedule it across the estate,
drain it, and keep a human on the exceptions. Spotify is the evidence it holds at organizational
size — a fleet-wide migration that once drew in hundreds of teams and ran for weeks is now scoped
by one engineer over a few days, the tooling targeting and scheduling the work while agents
implement it concurrently and the engineer watches the fleet and handles the exceptions rather
than typing each change [cite:spotify2026honk]. What to imitate is the placement, not the tool:
put the scarce human decision upstream — *what, where, when, whether* — and let the environment
carry the change onward, the same move the strategies above run at a single-subsystem grain. The
takeaway is that scale does not undo the incremental discipline; it raises the payoff, so the move
that repays weeks on one subsystem repays far more across a fleet.

## Field note — the DocAble retrofit month

*What follows is one engineer's account of running this chapter's recipe on his own system — lived
experience, not a controlled result. Read it as evidence for the procedure above, not more of the
procedure.*

<!-- section-terms: brownfield-migration -->

The retrofit itself — an agent told to type Python that reached for `string` everywhere, a
300,000-line Rube Goldberg machine pared to 200,000 modeled lines in a month — is told in full
in {{part:5}}. What matters here is the method that produced it.

<!-- point: turn-on-every-commodity-lint-and-squash-to-zero-blocking | Turn on every commodity lint, squash to blocking. | terms: lint, make-lint-blocking, static-analysis -->
I did it the way this chapter describes. I turned on every commodity lint my language's
ecosystems ship — hundreds of off-the-shelf smell checks — and for several weeks burned a stack
of coding-agent subscriptions squashing them, making each blocking as it hit zero so it could
never come back. I knew the coarse architecture I wanted — a clean model-view-controller
decomposition, a reactive web stack friendly to auto-scaling — and said "pursue this model," but
had to get there iteratively: find the dense primitive regions, induce structure there, inch
toward the target.

<!-- point: induce-a-typed-model-per-dense-primitive-region-iteratively | Induce a structured model per dense primitive region. | terms: dense-primitive-region, bottom-up-induction -->
A dense primitive region is a stretch of code doing many low-level operations by hand — raw
library calls, strings and ints passed around untyped, tree-walking spelled out inline — with
no abstraction that owns them. I found them the crude way, reading for the clots: where the
primitive calls piled up thickest, the missing model was loudest. Inducing structure means
extracting a model that owns those primitives — one class the raw calls route through — and
rewriting the region to go through it. You work region by region, because each induced model
gives the next something to lean on, and the target architecture assembles out of the pieces.
Each lint I drove to zero was one primitive smell given a name and a home; the
model-view-controller decomposition is the same move at the coarse grain. Now I have the
models, the architecture, the code that implements it, and enforcement holding all three
together every time the code changes.

<!-- point: the-retrofit-was-governance-conversion-at-brownfield-scale | The retrofit was governance conversion at brownfield scale. | terms: governance-conversion -->
In MAGE terms, that month was governance conversion at brownfield scale: the recurring
failures and the same rediscoveries, over and over, became the models, links, lints, and
gates that now shape every later task.[note: Governance conversion turns a recurring failure
class into a mechanism the environment applies for you, so a later agent meets the check
instead of rediscovering the failure.] Shopify reached the same loop from the other side,
turning each recurring agent failure into a change to the shared substrate rather than a
better prompt.

<!-- point: bootstrap-governance-begins-from-the-environment-you-already-own | Bootstrap governance from the environment you already own. | terms: brownfield-migration, governed-environment -->
That retrofit is one instance of a move any team can run at any starting rung: you do not
wait for a clean environment, you begin from the one you already own and mature it in place
until an agent can reason through it.

### Example — DocAble
The retrofit above is that instance: a janky 300,000-line prototype started from a heap of
spaghetti — the lowest rung — and was matured in place, lint by lint and model by model, until
