# Validating Change

<!-- point: how-do-we-know-a-change-is-acceptable | Every engineering process must answer how it knows a change is acceptable. | terms: independent-evidence -->
Every engineering process eventually reaches the same question: How do we know this change is
acceptable? Classical software engineering answers that through some combination of code review,
testing, and experienced engineering judgment. Those remain essential, but they become
insufficient once implementation becomes abundant. When autonomous systems can propose dozens of
substantial changes in the time a human once wrote a single feature, confidence must increasingly
come from explicit engineering evidence rather than repeated human inspection.

MAGE broadens what counts as engineering evidence. Executable models expose engineering intent.
Invariants define what must remain true. Validators and gates check those obligations
automatically. Domain-specific metrics measure whether important properties have improved or
degraded. Together these mechanisms allow a governed engineering environment—not merely the
reviewing engineer—to determine whether a proposed change satisfies its obligations. Notice that
agents do not disappear. They simply become the reason this engineering question has become urgent
rather than the subject of the chapter itself.

## What does it mean to validate a change?

<!-- section-terms: independent-evidence -->

<!-- point: validate-is-assemble-evidence | Validating a change means assembling evidence that it satisfies its obligations. | terms: independent-evidence -->
To validate a change is to assemble evidence that it satisfies its obligations — and to refuse it
otherwise. The classical answers assemble that evidence one way: a person reads the diff, a suite
of example tests reproduces known outputs, and an experienced engineer signs off. That is honest
work and it catches a great deal. But every part of it scales with human attention, and attention
is exactly what runs out when changes arrive faster than anyone can read them.

<!-- point: evidence-is-independent-of-the-author | Strong evidence is independent of whoever proposed the change. | terms: independent-evidence -->
The evidence that survives that pressure has one property in common: it is **independent of
whoever proposed the change.** Never admit a change on its author's word — human or agent —
generate evidence built to falsify the claim, and let through only what survives the search. This
is the central concept of the chapter, and MAGE draws that evidence from three sources beyond the
reviewer's eye. An **executable model** exposes what the change was supposed to preserve and can
itself judge whether it did. **Engineering metrics** measure whether the properties that matter —
fidelity, accessibility, correctness, coverage — improved or degraded. **Deterministic validators**
— tests, linters, formal checks, gates — consume both, mechanically and on every change. The three
sections that follow take them in turn.

## The model as oracle

<!-- section-terms: generative-validation, property-test-models-the-output, thesis-modeling -->

<!-- point: generate-to-falsify-a-model-supplied-spec | Generate inputs to falsify a specification the model supplies. | terms: generative-validation -->
<!-- index-def: generative-validation -->
Most tests reproduce a dot. You pick an input, you write down the output you expect, and the
test asserts the two match. That is honest work, of course, and it catches a great deal; but
it says nothing about the input sitting right next to the one you chose. There is a different
move, and it names a whole family of techniques: **generate inputs and search for a
counterexample to a specification.** Property-based testing, fuzzing, and the synthesis of
fuzzing with a structured system model belong to that family. They share one common loop —
**generate, execute, judge, and shrink or diagnose** — but they differ materially in three
places: the *generator* that makes the inputs, the *oracle* that judges them, and the *search*
that steers toward failure. Drawing those three axes sharply is what keeps the family from
collapsing into a vague "testing with randomness" — and what keeps it from flattening the other
way, into a claim that the three techniques are identical.

<!-- point: generative-test-unites-both-theses | A generative test unites the Modeling and Alignment theses. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, generative-validation -->
That move is the whole book worn as a test. The specification the generator hunts is a model
— the Modeling Thesis, that the system's correctness lives in an explicit model and not in
prose. And the generator re-hunts a counterexample on every change — the Alignment Thesis,
that the environment keeps re-checking the agent's work against that model automatically,
forever, rather than trusting a one-time review. A generative-validation test is those two
theses standing in one place: a compact model, and a machine that never stops trying to break
it.

<!-- point: engineered-model-enables-deterministic-validation | An engineered model supports deterministic validation; a scraped knowledge graph does not. | terms: thesis-modeling, mbse -->
This is why an *engineered* representation validates where code and documentation alone cannot. A
model in this sense is not a Wikipedia-style knowledge graph — facts scraped and associated,
consulted by probabilistic lookup, true on average. It is an engineered artifact with declared
types, closed sets, and stated invariants, built to support **deterministic** reasoning: given the
model, a check returns the same verdict every time, and that verdict has a definite meaning. Code
carries the behavior but not the intent; prose carries the intent but nothing a machine can check.
The engineered model carries intent in a form a validator can consume — which is exactly what makes
it a source of evidence rather than a description. The richer the model, the stronger the validation
it permits, because the oracle a check can appeal to is only ever as rich as the model behind it.

### The move: a generator hunts a counterexample to a model

<!-- point: generative-test-hunts-a-counterexample | A generative test hunts a counterexample across the space. | terms: property-test-models-the-output, generative-validation -->
<!-- index-def: property-test-models-the-output -->
Before the three members, the shared shape. An example-based test pins a single point: given
*this* input, expect *that* output. A generative test does something else — it states the
property the output must satisfy across the whole space of inputs, and hands a generator the
job of hunting a counterexample. Where an example gives the machine one labelled dot to
reproduce, a generative test gives it the law the whole space obeys and a search for the place
the code breaks it.

<!-- point: a-property-has-no-case-to-special-case | A broadly quantified property makes one-input special-casing less effective. | terms: property-test-models-the-output, tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
That difference is exactly the one that matters against an agent. Faced with a failing
example, an agent will do the locally-expedient thing: special-case the path that makes *that*
input pass. The example goes green and the bug survives, now hidden behind a branch cut to fit
the test. A broadly quantified property blunts that move. There is no single case to patch:
special-case the one input the generator surfaced and its neighbours — which the generator keeps
drawing — still exercise the law, so the branch-cut that satisfied the example fails the next
generated case. (The agent is not powerless here; it can weaken the generator or overfit the
property itself. Those are the failures the coverage discipline later in this chapter is built to
catch.) This is why the strategy that catches the *class* beats the one that catches the
example, and why the generative family is what the [lessons chapter](5.6-governance-conversions.html)
means when it says to pin the invariant, not the example. [ref:example-vs-generative] draws the difference.

### Fuzz + MBSE: the structured model becomes the oracle

<!-- section-terms: generative-validation, producer-dialect-corpus -->

The richest form of validation-from-a-model turns the model itself into the judge. "Fuzz + model"
is this book's practical label for one point in a well-populated space: adversarial
generation judged against a structured system model as oracle. The underlying techniques overlap
established model-based testing and the grammar-based and stateful-fuzzing traditions — Inset A
places it. The label names a combination, not a new category.

<!-- point: the-typed-model-becomes-the-oracle | The structured model itself becomes the oracle. | terms: mbse, oracle -->
This is the synthesis. Take the wild inputs of fuzzing and, instead of
asking only "did it crash," judge the outcome against the structured model the fleet already reasons
through. The model names the *stable point in the specification*: a closed set of legal outcome
classes, an invariant predicate, a transition table. Point the malformed bytes at the model's own
entry point, and classify what comes back against that declared set. A clean rejection of an
illegal input is a *pass* — the model correctly refused it. An outcome outside the legal set — an
unexpected exception, a silently corrupted structure, a state the transition table forbids — is a
*fail*, for the claims the model explicitly represents. The oracle is now as rich as the model is,
and the inputs are as wild as a fuzzer's. So the trade-off the simpler validators live under is
*weakened*, though not abolished: the richer oracle is a model you now have to build and keep true
to the system.

Two instances make this concrete.

<!-- point: sort-outcomes-against-the-closed-legal-set | Sort adversarial outcomes against the model's closed legal set. | terms: mbse, closed-vocabulary -->
The first is the **format model**. Feed adversarial bytes to the structured document model's read
entry point and sort the result into a small, closed set of legal outcome classes — parsed
cleanly, rejected cleanly with a typed error, and so on. The set *is* the specification. An
outcome the set does not name is the failure, and because the set is the model's own declaration
rather than a hand-written assertion per seed, the same oracle judges every input the fuzzer can
throw.

<!-- point: producer-dialect-is-the-adversarial-input | Let a rival producer's legal dialect be the input. | terms: producer-dialect-corpus, stable-spec-point -->
<!-- index-def: producer-dialect-corpus -->
The second turns the fuzzer's usual approach inside out. Call it the **producer-dialect corpus.** Instead of *mutating toward* malformed bytes,
round-trip a real document through a genuine third-party producer — a different office suite, a
different PDF writer, a different export path — and let that producer's *legal-but-unusual
dialect* be the adversarial input. Every producer emits its own accent within the format's
grammar: unusual-but-valid object orderings, obscure-but-permitted structures, features the
specification allows and your own writer never uses. Where the specification-point fix reasons
*inward* from a failing seed to the stable rule it violated, the producer-dialect corpus reasons
*outward* — it generates inputs that occupy the whole specification-allowed producer space, so the
model is tested against the full breadth of what the format legally permits rather than the narrow
slice your own tooling happens to emit. Strictly it is corpus-based interoperability testing —
differential robustness against real dialects rather than mutational fuzzing — a sibling technique
within the generative-validation family; but it needs no mutation engine at all, and that is what
makes it distinctive: the world's producers are the generator.

<!-- point: harvest-producers-before-hand-rolling-a-grammar | Harvest real producers before hand-rolling a grammar. | terms: producer-dialect-corpus -->

> **Harvest producers before you hand-roll a grammar.** A grammar you write yourself covers the
> dialects *you* thought to encode. The format's independent producers — every rival tool that writes
> it — have already generated the legal-but-unusual space for you, a breadth no single authored grammar
> reaches. Reach for a from-scratch generator only where no real producer exists.

> ### Inset — What are model-based and stateful testing?
>
> Write down a small model of what the system should do — a set of legal outcomes, or a state machine of legal
> transitions — and use *that* as the judge for generated inputs or generated sequences of calls. A stateful test
> drives a queue through generated sequences of enqueue and dequeue and, after each step, checks a model
> invariant: a dequeued item was previously enqueued, and order is preserved.
>
> **Model-based testing** generates from, or checks against, an explicit model. **Stateful property testing**
> generates sequences of operations and checks a model invariant after each one. **Grammar-based** and
> **stateful** fuzzing sit in the same tradition — they steer generation with a structure rather than flat random
> bytes. These are established techniques with a substantial literature.
>
> This book's "**fuzz + model**" names one point in that space: adversarial generation judged by a structured
> system model as the oracle. It is a practical label for a combination, not a new category — it overlaps
> model-based testing and the grammar/stateful-fuzzing traditions rather than standing apart from them.
>
> You can now recognize when a test's judge is a *model* rather than a per-input assertion, and place "fuzz +
> model" inside the model-based-testing family rather than beside it.

<!-- point: search-interleavings-against-the-invariant-predicate | For a concurrency invariant, search interleavings against the predicate. | terms: interleaving-search, concurrency, invariant -->
The synthesis reaches one level up when the specification is not a format but a **concurrency
invariant.** There the "input" is an interleaving of concurrent steps, and the oracle is the
invariant predicate evaluated over the model's states — no two workers hold the same lease; a job
never leaves a terminal state; a queued item is eventually served. The **shape of the invariant**
decides which search earns its cost: a **local, state predicate** over structured inputs earns a
property test, while a **temporal or interleaving-dependent invariant** earns a systematic or
bounded search over interleavings — bounded by depth and model size, so "every reachable
interleaving" always means within a stated bound (Inset B lays out the three ways to generate
schedules). The payoff, learned the hard way, is that *naming the invariant predicts where the
bug is.* Writing the predicate down forces you to state the exact condition that must hold, and the
search then drives straight at the interleaving that violates it — a defect a strong-but-static
unit suite walks right past. This is generative validation standing on the same
[invariants-over-models](3.2-models-and-the-semantic-gap.html) ground the rest of the book is built on.

> ### Inset — Three ways to generate schedules
>
> For a concurrency bug the "input" is an **interleaving** — the order in which concurrent steps happen to run.
> You find the bad interleaving by generating schedules, and there are three ways to do it, of rising cost and
> rising thoroughness.
>
> * **Randomized scheduler (a property test for concurrency).** Perturb timing randomly and check the invariant
>   after each run. Cheap, catches shallow races, and misses the rare interleaving that randomness never happens
>   to hit.
> * **Systematic concurrency testing.** A controlled scheduler steers execution through distinct interleavings on
>   purpose — covering orderings deliberately rather than hoping randomness reaches them.
> * **Exhaustive or bounded state exploration.** Enumerate the reachable interleavings up to a **bound** — a depth,
>   a step count, a model size — and check the invariant on each. The strongest, but always bounded: "every
>   reachable interleaving" means within the stated bound, never literally all of them for an unbounded system.
>
> You can now name which schedule-generation method a concurrency check uses, and read the mainline routing rule:
> the invariant's shape — a **local, state predicate** versus a **temporal or interleaving-dependent** one —
> decides which of the three earns its cost.

## Choosing the validation technique

<!-- section-terms: generative-validation, soft-vs-hard-governance -->

<!-- point: validators-consume-models-and-metrics | Deterministic validators consume the models and metrics as machine-read checks. | terms: generative-validation, oracle -->
Models expose intent and metrics measure degree; **validators** are the machine-read checks that
consume both and return a verdict. Tests, linters, format validators, formal checks, and the gates
that wire them into admission are all deterministic sensors — run them twice on the same input and
they agree. The generative family gives the strongest of these: three techniques that are one move
worn three ways, differing in the *generator*, the *oracle*, and the *search*. [ref:generative-family]
lays the family out on one page. I suggest reading each row left to right — oracle, then failure,
then generator — because the oracle is what the technique *is* and the generator is only how it
gets there.

<!-- label: generative-family -->
| Technique | What the oracle is | What counts as a *failure* | How inputs are generated |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Property-based testing** | A **declared invariant** the author writes — a law the output must obey for every input: parse-then-serialize preserves semantic equivalence; a remediated document contains no less content than the original, measured over the system's content inventory; a sort yields an ordered permutation. | The stated law is **false** on some generated input. | A **structured generator** synthesizes well-typed domain values; on a failure it **shrinks** the input to the smallest one that still breaks the law. |
| **Fuzzing** | An **implicit robustness contract** — "never crash, never corrupt," and "reject cleanly whatever the format forbids." In its **baseline coverage-guided** form the oracle is coarse but real: the process survives, and the output re-parses. (Modern fuzzers enrich this oracle well past crash-detection — see Inset E. And a clean re-parse does not by itself prove no silent corruption; pair it with a semantic or structural invariant where one exists.) | A **crash, hang, resource blow-up, or silent corruption** on adversarial or spec-edge input, even where no explicit law was stated. | **Malformed or adversarial bytes** — truncations, bit-flips, corrupted offsets, garbage appended — reaching inputs no well-typed generator would ever emit. |
| **Fuzz + model-based engineering** | The **structured model itself** — the same model the fleet reasons through — becomes the oracle. It names the *stable point in the specification*: the closed set of legal outcome classes, the invariant predicate, the state-transition table. | The generated input drives the model into a state it declares **illegal** — an outcome outside the legal set, a transition the table forbids, an invariant predicate that goes false. | The same adversarial generation as fuzzing, aimed at a **model-declared surface**: the model's own read entry point, a state machine's driving conditions, a flow between services. |

Three sharp distinctions fall out of that table, and stating them keeps property testing and
fuzzing from blurring together.

<!-- point: oracle-richness-and-input-wildness-trade-off | Oracle richness and input wildness trade off. | terms: oracle, mbse -->
First, **oracle richness and input wildness trade off, and the third member refuses the
trade.** The richer the oracle, the tamer the inputs: a property test knows exactly what
correct means, and yet it asks the question only of inputs a type would allow. The wilder the
inputs, the coarser the oracle: fuzzing reaches bytes nothing else reaches, and yet it can
notice only a crash. Fuzz-with-a-model is the synthesis that takes both halves: wild inputs,
judged against a rich model-declared oracle. It weakens the trade-off rather than abolishing it. The
wild inputs now meet a richer oracle, at the standing cost of building and maintaining that
oracle. This is the same two-thesis pattern from the opening: the model supplies an oracle for
the claims it explicitly represents, and the search never stops hunting a counterexample to it.

<!-- point: failure-means-false-law-or-crash | Failure means a false law or a crash. | terms: oracle, never-crash-contract -->
Second, **the word *failure* means different things, and conflating them is the classic
mistake.** For a property test, a failure is a stated law turning out false. For a fuzzer, a
failure is the program leaving its safe envelope, crashing or corrupting, in a place where
*no* law was ever stated. Blur the two and you get the two symmetric errors: a fuzzer that
only checks for a crash when it should have been asserting an invariant, and a property test
fed only tame inputs when the real risk was the malformed byte at the boundary.

<!-- point: stable-spec-point-bridges-fuzzing-and-model | The stable spec point bridges fuzzing and the model. | terms: stable-spec-point, mbse -->
Third, **the specification's stable point is the bridge between fuzzing and the model.** When
a fuzzer finds a crash, there are two ways to fix it. You can patch the one input that broke
— and leave every neighbouring input that would break the same way. Or you can trace the
failure back to the point in the format's specification that the input violated, and fix
*that* — so the fix covers every input the specification allows, not the single seed that
happened to trip it. Fixing to the stable specification point is what promotes a raw fuzzer
into a model-based one: the model *is* the stable point, written down.

### Property-based testing: the invariant is the oracle

<!-- section-terms: property-test-models-the-output, thesis-modeling -->

<!-- point: author-writes-law-generator-shrinks | The author writes the law; the generator shrinks the counterexample. | terms: property-test-models-the-output, shrinking, oracle -->
In property-based testing the author writes the law and the machine hunts the counterexample.
The law is a statement about *every* output, not a single one: parse-then-serialize preserves
semantic equivalence; a remediated DocAble document contains no less content than the one that
went in, measured over the content inventory; a sort produces a permutation of its input in
non-decreasing order. Each of these is a compact, checkable model of what a correct output looks
like — a law, not a list. The generator draws values from an **authored domain model** — the
typed space the law is meant to hold over — and it can be tuned to reach the boundary and
adversarial-but-valid values, not only mild ones. That authored domain is the real line between a
property test and a fuzzer: values from a domain model the author describes, versus arbitrary
byte-level mutation. (Inset D gives the mechanics, including what shrinking does.)

<!-- point: property-is-the-modeling-thesis-as-a-test | The property is the Modeling Thesis worn as a test. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
This is the same two-thesis pattern the opening drew: the property is a compact model the agent
reasons over instead of enumerating cases, and the generator re-hunts a counterexample on every
commit. In a real system this is no footnote
technique. A property-based framework can run across every test project, and the discipline is to
reach for it the moment a class's contract is expressible as a law rather than a list of examples.
The counterexample it hands back is often a genuine defect the author never thought to write an
example for. That is why you ask the machine to search the space instead of guessing at its
corners.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [property-based tests](appendix-c-property-tests.html).

> ### Inset — What is property-based testing, and what does shrinking do?
>
> Ordinary tests pin one input to one expected output. A property-based test states a **law the output must obey
> for every input in a domain**, then lets a generator hunt a counterexample. For a sort, the law is "the output
> is an ordered permutation of the input"; the framework generates hundreds of lists and checks that law on each,
> so you never had to guess which list would break the code.
>
> The generator draws from an **authored domain model** — a description of the values the law ranges over
> (bounded integers, well-formed documents, lists of them) — not from arbitrary bytes. That is the real line
> between a property test and a fuzzer, and it is why a property generator can be *tuned* to include boundary and
> adversarial-but-valid values while still staying inside the domain.
>
> When the law fails, the framework **shrinks**: it walks the failing input down to the smallest one that still
> breaks the law, so the counterexample you debug is a two-element list, not a thousand-node document with one
> bad byte buried inside. The minimized case you get back is the shrinker's work, not luck.
>
> You can now recognize a property test by its shape — a quantified law plus a domain generator — and read a
> minimized counterexample as the generator having searched the space and then boiled the failure down. This is
> the oracle-rich, domain-bounded end of the generate–execute–judge–shrink loop; it says nothing about inputs
> outside the authored domain.

### Fuzzing: the contract is "never crash, never corrupt"

<!-- section-terms: fuzzing -->

<!-- point: fuzzing-asks-a-coarse-question-of-wild-inputs | Fuzzing asks a coarse question of wild inputs. | terms: fuzzing, never-crash-contract, oracle -->
Property testing asks a rich question of inputs drawn from an authored domain. Fuzzing asks a
coarser question of arbitrary, mutated ones. In its baseline coverage-guided form the oracle is
the implicit robustness contract every input-parsing surface signs whether or not anyone wrote
it down: *do not crash, do not hang, do not corrupt, and reject cleanly whatever the format
forbids.* Modern fuzzing enriches that oracle — sanitizers, differential comparison, semantic
assertions, metamorphic relations — but the baseline is the honest starting point, and Inset E
unpacks it. A malformed DocAble upload — a PDF truncated mid-object, a slide deck with a corrupted
offset table, a document with megabytes of garbage appended — is precisely the input a generator
of *valid* documents will never produce, and precisely where the crash lives. The fuzzer mutates
and adversarially perturbs its way into that space and watches for the process to fall over or the
output to fail to re-parse.

<!-- point: fix-a-crash-to-the-spec-point | Fix a crash to the spec point, not the seed. | terms: stable-spec-point, fuzzing -->
The multiplier is discipline about the fix. When a fuzzer surfaces a crash, the temptation is to
handle the exact bytes it found. The stronger move is to trace the failure to the point in the
format's specification that the input violated, and fix *that* — because the specification is
stable while any one producer's quirks are not, so a fix aimed at the specification point closes
every input the format allows, not the single failing seed. One fix for the whole class, not a
pile of one-off patches.

<!-- point: coverage-makes-we-fuzzed-it-a-claim | Coverage makes 'we fuzzed it' a measured claim. | terms: fuzzing, line-coverage -->
A campaign that merely runs has explored nothing, so the honesty layer matters: the
host test-runner can auto-collect line and branch coverage over each fuzz campaign and track it
against a baseline, so "we fuzzed it" becomes a claim with a number behind it rather than a vibe.
A coverage plateau — the campaign no longer finding new edges — is *one* saturation signal, not
proof of saturation: it can hide input-grammar variants or semantic states the generator never
produced in the first place (Inset E and the coverage section return to this). Its deeper
treatment belongs to the [metrics chapter](3.5-metrics.html), which owns coverage.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [fuzz campaigns](appendix-c-fuzz-campaigns.html).

> ### Inset — What does coverage-guided fuzzing measure?
>
> A fuzzer throws malformed and mutated bytes at a parsing surface and watches for it to fall over. *Coverage
> guidance* makes the throwing smart: the fuzzer keeps any input that reaches new code and breeds more variants
> from it. Feed a PDF reader truncated and bit-flipped files; when one input reaches a new branch in the
> object-stream parser, the fuzzer notices and mutates that one further.
>
> The **baseline oracle is coarse** — did the process crash, hang, or blow up memory. Line and branch coverage
> play two roles at once: the *search* signal (which code the inputs reached, steering the mutation) and the
> *saturation* signal (has the campaign stopped finding new edges). A coverage plateau is only one saturation
> signal — it can hide input-grammar variants or semantic states the generator never produced, so a flat curve is
> not proof the space is explored.
>
> Modern fuzzers enrich the oracle well past crash-detection: **sanitizers** catch memory and undefined-behavior
> errors mid-run; **differential** oracles require two independent implementations to agree; **semantic
> assertions** and **metamorphic relations** check that related inputs produce related outputs. A clean re-parse,
> by itself, does not prove no corruption — a mangled output can still parse.
>
> You can now read "we fuzzed it" as a claim with a coverage number behind it, and know to ask which oracle the
> campaign used — crash-only, or something richer. What counts as *enough* coverage belongs to the metrics
> chapter, not here.

<!-- point: fuzz-plus-mbse-is-placed-earlier | The fuzz-plus-model technique is developed above, under The model as oracle. | terms: generative-validation, mbse -->
The third member — fuzz plus a model, where the structured model itself becomes the oracle — is the
richest deterministic validator, and this chapter develops it earlier, under *The model as oracle*, because its whole point is that the *model* is the judge. The format model, the
producer-dialect corpus, and the interleaving search against a concurrency invariant all live there.

### When to reach for which

<!-- point: pick-the-technique-from-the-signal-in-the-code | Pick the technique from the signal in the code under test. | terms: generative-validation -->
The family gives a compact decision aid, and the axes of the opening table are the decision. Read
the signal in the code under test, as [ref:generative-decision] sets out; the row names the
technique and the oracle it buys.

<!-- label: generative-decision -->
| Signal in the code under test | Reach for | The oracle it gives you |
|---|---|---|
| A **stated law** over structured inputs — a round-trip, a content-preservation guarantee, an ordering | **Property test** | The law you can already write down |
| An **untrusted-bytes boundary** with a never-crash contract and no per-input law worth stating | **Coverage-guided fuzzer** — and fix its findings to the stable point in the specification, not the failing seed | The robustness contract: don't crash, hang, or corrupt |
| A **structured model** with a declared legal-outcome set or invariant | **Fuzz plus the model** — point wild inputs at the model's surface, judge against its declared classes; when a real third-party producer exists, let its dialect be the corpus | The model, for the claims it explicitly represents |
| A **concurrency invariant** | **State-model variant** — a property test for a local/state predicate, a systematic or bounded interleaving search for a temporal/interleaving-dependent one | The invariant predicate over the model's states |

## Coverage: did the validation explore the claim?

<!-- section-terms: grammar-coverage, coverage-model-mapping -->

<!-- point: metrics-are-evidence-of-improve-or-degrade | Metrics turn "did it get better or worse" into standing evidence. | terms: coverage-model-mapping, measure-one-level-deeper -->
A validator answers a yes/no obligation; a **metric** answers a question of degree, and degree is
its own kind of evidence. Did content fidelity hold — does the remediated document still say
everything the original did? Did accessibility conformance improve or regress? Did latency,
correctness, or a domain-specific quality measure move the right way? These are not pass/fail laws
so much as measured properties, tracked against a baseline so a change that quietly degrades one of
them is caught by the number rather than by a reviewer's memory. The book develops these as a
flagship of its own — the [metrics chapter](3.5-metrics.html) owns the general treatment, including
the discipline to **measure one level deeper** than the raw percentage. This section takes only the
one metric native to generative validation, and hands the rest back.

<!-- point: generation-adds-a-native-coverage-question | Generation adds one coverage question native to it. | terms: grammar-coverage, measure-one-level-deeper -->
Once you have a generator, a new question appears that example tests never had to ask: *did the
campaign explore the space that matters, or did it just spin?* A campaign that merely runs has
explored nothing, so coverage is the honesty layer that turns "we fuzzed it" into a claim with a
number behind it rather than a vibe.

<!-- point: grammar-coverage-measures-inputs-not-code | Grammar coverage measures the inputs, not the code. | terms: grammar-coverage -->
The native cut is **grammar coverage.** Line and branch coverage measure the *code* the inputs
reached. Grammar coverage measures the *inputs* themselves: did the generator exercise every
production of the input grammar — every structural variant, every mutation kind, every dialect the
corpus is supposed to contain? It is branch coverage moved to the input side, and it answers the
question the gap analyses of a real fuzzing effort keep circling back to: *is the corpus rich
enough?* Three distinct measures live under that one name, and they are not interchangeable:
**grammar-production coverage** (did the generator exercise every production of an explicit input
grammar), **mutation-operator coverage** (did it apply every kind of mutation), and
**producer/dialect coverage** (did the corpus carry every real producer's accent) — the last has
no explicit grammar at all, so a producer corpus is measured by dialect coverage, not by
productions (Inset C). A campaign that never generates a document with headers and footers, or
never a cross-sheet formula, has such a hole no amount of line coverage will reveal, because
the untested lines were never reached by any generated input in the first place.

> ### Inset — What does grammar coverage count?
>
> Code coverage asks which *lines* the inputs reached. Grammar coverage asks which *kinds of inputs* the
> generator actually produced. A document fuzzer that never once generates a file with headers and footers has a
> grammar-coverage hole — and no amount of line coverage will reveal it, because the lines that handle
> headers-and-footers were never reached by any generated input in the first place.
>
> Three distinct measures hide under the one name, and they are not interchangeable: **grammar-production
> coverage** (did the generator exercise every production of an explicit input grammar), **mutation-operator
> coverage** (did it apply every kind of mutation), and **producer/dialect coverage** (did the corpus carry every
> real producer's accent). A hand-written grammar has productions to count; a corpus harvested from real producers
> has no explicit grammar at all, so it is measured by dialect coverage instead.
>
> You can now ask of any campaign not just "what code did it cover" but "what inputs did it cover," and pick the
> right one of the three input-side measures for the generator you actually have.

<!-- point: model-claim-coverage-is-the-saturation-oracle | Model-claim coverage is the family's real saturation oracle. | terms: coverage-model-mapping, requirements-based-coverage, measure-one-level-deeper -->
Line and branch coverage of a campaign is the saturation signal — the honesty layer that keeps "we
fuzzed it" from being an empty claim. But the coverage that ultimately matters for generative
validation is **model claims** — did the generated inputs actually drive the invariants,
transitions, and edges the model declares, measured as the metrics chapter's requirements-based
coverage over the traceability graph? That is the connective tissue: the same "measure one level
deeper" move the metrics chapter teaches, reused here as the saturation oracle for validation from
a model. [ref:generative-loop] draws the loop, closing on that coverage.

## Deterministic admission

<!-- section-terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: generative-validation-is-hard-governance | Generative validation is hard governance, the Alignment Thesis mechanical. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, thesis-alignment -->
These checks are deterministic sensors, machine-read — and they **become hard governance when their
verdicts control admission**, wired as a commit gate that refuses the merge. The same fuzz campaign
run only on a schedule, reporting to a dashboard, is a sensor but not yet a gate; it holds the line
only once its verdict can block the commit. Wired that way, a generator that re-hunts a
counterexample every commit is the Alignment Thesis made mechanical — the environment refusing to
trust the last green checkmark, and checking the model again, forever.

## Why this matters in the agentic era

<!-- section-terms: independent-evidence, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: agentic-era-changes-how-much-evidence | Autonomous development changes how much evidence we require, not what evidence is. | terms: independent-evidence, thesis-alignment -->
Autonomous development does not change *what* engineering evidence is. A model exposed intent, a
metric measured degradation, and a validator refused a bad change long before any agent proposed
one. What changes is *how much* of that evidence we now require, and where the burden of producing
it falls. When one reviewer wrote one feature a week, human inspection could carry most of the
load; the models and validators were a supplement. When a fleet proposes changes faster than any
person can read them, inspection cannot scale to meet the volume, and assurance has to shift onto
the explicit representations and the automated checks that consume them.

<!-- point: evidence-must-outrun-inspection | Evidence must now be generated faster than a human can inspect. | terms: independent-evidence -->
So the chapter's central concept — *independent evidence*, generated to falsify a claim and admitting
only what survives — stops being a supplement and becomes the load-bearing floor. The reviewing
engineer does not disappear; the human gate on consequential decisions is one of the strongest
validators there is. But the engineer's judgment is spent designing the models, choosing the
metrics, and setting the gates, rather than re-reading every diff. The environment does the
re-checking, on every change, forever. That is what lets confidence keep pace with abundance.

## Industrial example

<!-- section-terms: generative-validation, independent-evidence -->

<!-- point: independent-evidence-is-not-a-docable-idiosyncrasy | Placing the grader outside the author is a general move, not a DocAble idiosyncrasy. | terms: independent-evidence -->
The move is one a team can copy: generate evidence built to falsify a claim, and admit only the
claim that survives. It is not a DocAble idiosyncrasy. One of the field guide's teams reaches for
exactly this move at the sharp end of the trust question — an autonomous coding fleet — where the
author of a change is the last party you would trust to grade it.

<!-- worked-examples: independent-evidence -->
### Example — Docker
**The concept it illustrates.** *Independent evidence* — the grader must sit outside the reasoner
whose work it judges. Docker's autonomous coding fleet builds the separation into its architecture:
the agent that writes a change never grades it.

**What Docker did.** An independent reviewer with its own model invocation, a test suite the team
determinizes eagerly so its verdict is repeatable, and a human gate stand between every pull
request and main. The fleet may open a pull request; it may not merge one. "That's the trust
boundary, and it's deliberate" — merge, architecture, scope, and prioritization stay human. The
sharpest statement of the philosophy is theirs: reviewing outcomes while skipping the changes
themselves "is not a security model, it's a prayer."

**What to imitate.** Separate the author of a change from its grader, and put the grader — a
reviewer, a determinized suite, a human on the consequential call — where the author cannot reach
it. The separation is the mechanism; the specific tools are interchangeable.

<!-- takeaway -->
The shared move is *independent evidence*: never admit an agent's claim on its own word — generate
evidence built to falsify it, and let through only what survives the search. Docker is evidence the
move scales to a full autonomous fleet on a live product; DocAble runs the whole family at full
depth against its own hard problem, proving a remediated document says no less than the original.
Part V tells the DocAble story in full, and [MAGE in the Wild](6.6-where-mage-fits.html) sets Docker
beside five other systems as evidence the practice travels.
