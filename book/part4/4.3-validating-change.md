# Validating Change

<!-- point: how-do-we-know-a-change-is-acceptable | Every engineering process must answer how it knows a change is acceptable. | terms: independent-evidence -->
Every engineering process eventually reaches the same question: How do we know this change is
acceptable? Classical software engineering answers that through some combination of code review,
testing, and experienced engineering judgment. Those remain essential, but they become
insufficient once implementation becomes abundant. When autonomous systems can propose dozens of
substantial changes in the time a human once wrote a single feature, confidence must increasingly
come from explicit engineering evidence rather than repeated human inspection.

<!-- point: validation-is-only-as-strong-as-the-obligation | Validation is only as strong as the obligation behind it. | terms: independent-evidence -->
Validation is only as strong as the obligation behind it. If the requirement is settled, a
validator can ask whether the change satisfies it. If the requirement is still a hypothesis —
whether users want the feature at all, which workflow they prefer, which tradeoff they will
accept — no test suite can manufacture that knowledge. In that regime the evidence is
experimental: usage, comparison, observation, or direct customer feedback. The method can govern the
experiment and hold its non-negotiable constraints; it cannot turn an unknown product decision
into a mechanical truth.

## What validation means

<!-- section-terms: independent-evidence -->

<!-- point: validate-is-assemble-evidence | Validating a change means assembling evidence that it satisfies its obligations. | terms: independent-evidence -->
To validate a change is to assemble evidence that it satisfies its obligations — and to refuse it
otherwise. The classical answers assemble that evidence one way: a person reads the diff, a suite
of example tests reproduces known outputs, and an experienced engineer signs off. That is honest
work and it catches a great deal. But every part of it scales with human attention, and attention
is exactly what runs out when changes arrive faster than anyone can read them.

<!-- point: evidence-is-independent-of-the-author | Strong evidence is independent of whoever proposed the change. | terms: independent-evidence -->
The evidence that survives that pressure has one property in common: it is **independent of
whoever proposed the change.** Never admit a change on its author's word — human or agent —
generate evidence built to falsify the claim, and let through only what survives the search. This
is the central concept of the chapter, and the method draws that evidence from three sources beyond the
reviewer's eye. An **executable model** exposes what the change was supposed to preserve and can
itself judge whether it did. **Engineering metrics** measure whether the properties that matter —
fidelity, accessibility, correctness, coverage — improved or degraded. **Validators** — the
machine-read checks that consume both — evaluate that evidence on every change. The sections that
follow take the model first, because it is the sharpest source: a judge that is itself a model.

## The model as oracle

<!-- section-terms: generative-validation, property-test-models-the-output, thesis-modeling -->

<!-- point: models-are-the-shared-substrate | Explicit models are the shared substrate humans and agents reason through, and the model that helps produce a change later helps judge it. | terms: thesis-modeling, mbse -->
The Modeling Thesis explains why validation now leans on the model. Explicit models are not
documentation, and they are not merely prompts for agents. They are engineering representations that
humans and agents reason *through* instead of reasoning directly over the implementation. Once such
representations exist, they become shared substrates for generation, validation, traceability,
measurement, and governance. The same model that helps an agent produce a change later helps a
validator judge whether that change is acceptable.

<!-- point: engineered-model-enables-deterministic-validation | An engineered model supports repeatable validation; a scraped knowledge graph does not. | terms: thesis-modeling, mbse -->
This is why an *engineered* representation validates where code and documentation alone cannot. A
model in this sense is not a Wikipedia-style knowledge graph — facts scraped and associated,
consulted by probabilistic lookup, true on average. It is an engineered artifact with declared
types, closed sets, and stated invariants: given the model, a check returns the same verdict every
time, and that verdict has a definite meaning. Code carries the behavior but not the intent; prose
carries the intent but nothing a machine can check. The engineered model carries intent in a form a
validator can consume — which is what makes it a source of evidence rather than a description. The
richer the model, the stronger the validation it authorizes, because the oracle a check can appeal
to is only ever as rich as the model behind it.

<!-- point: generative-test-hunts-a-counterexample | A generative test hunts a counterexample across the space. | terms: property-test-models-the-output, generative-validation -->
<!-- index-def: property-test-models-the-output -->
<!-- index-def: generative-validation -->
That sharpens the difference between two kinds of test. An example-based test pins a single point:
given *this* input, expect *that* output. It reproduces a dot the author already knew, and says
nothing about the input sitting right next to the one they chose. A generative test does something
else — it states the property the output must satisfy across the whole space of inputs, and hands a
generator the job of hunting a counterexample. Where the example gives the machine one labelled dot
to reproduce, the generative test gives it the law the whole space obeys and a search for the place
the code breaks it. [ref:example-vs-generative] draws the difference.

<!-- label: example-vs-generative -->
<!-- figure: assets/example-vs-generative.svg | *A Point vs a Space.* The example pins a point; the generative test models the space. One reproduces a dot the author already knew; the other states the law every dot obeys and lets a generator find where the code breaks it. -->

<!-- point: a-property-has-no-case-to-special-case | A broadly quantified property makes one-input special-casing less effective. | terms: property-test-models-the-output, tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
That difference is the one that matters against an agent. Faced with a failing example, an agent
will do the locally-expedient thing: special-case the path that makes *that* input pass. The example
goes green and the bug survives, hidden behind a branch cut to fit the test. A broadly quantified
property leaves no single case to patch — the generator keeps drawing neighbours that still exercise
the law, so the branch-cut that satisfied one input fails the next generated case. This is why the
strategy that catches the *class* beats the one that catches the example, and it is what the
[lessons chapter](5.4-failures-that-became-infrastructure.html) means by *pin the invariant, not the
example.*

<!-- point: generative-test-unites-both-theses | A generative test unites the Modeling and Alignment theses. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, generative-validation -->
A generative test is the whole book worn as a check. The specification the generator hunts is a
model — the Modeling Thesis, that correctness lives in an explicit model, not in prose. And the
generator re-hunts a counterexample on every change — the Alignment Thesis, that the environment
keeps re-checking the work against that model automatically. A compact model, and a machine that
never stops trying to break it.

<!-- point: choose-the-technique-by-the-claim | The techniques share one loop and differ by generator, oracle, and search; choose by the shape of the claim. | terms: generative-validation, oracle -->
The techniques that generate evidence this way — property-based testing, fuzzing, and the synthesis
of the two with a structured model — share one loop: **generate, execute, judge, shrink or
diagnose.** They differ in three places only: the *generator* that makes the inputs, the *oracle*
that judges them, and the *search* that steers toward failure. They are not rungs on a maturity
ladder. Choose the one whose generator and oracle match the engineering claim ([Inset 1](#inset-technique-comparison)
lays them side by side; [Inset 2](#inset-pbt) is the refresher on the property end).

> ### Inset — Example, property, fuzzing, model-based: what changes? {#inset-technique-comparison}
>
> Four ways to produce evidence, one shared loop — **generate → execute → judge**. They differ in
> where the inputs come from, where the verdict comes from, and what each is best at.
>
> | Technique | Inputs come from | Oracle comes from | Best at |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Example test** | engineer-chosen examples | the expected output, written down | known cases, regressions |
> | **Property test** | an authored input domain | an invariant the output must obey | laws over many valid cases |
> | **Fuzzing** | mutation and adversarial generation | a robustness contract, plus richer auxiliary oracles | malformed and edge inputs |
> | **Model-based / stateful** | model-generated actions and states | an explicit behavioral model | sequences, protocols, state |
>
> Read a row left to right and the oracle is what the technique *is*; the generator is only how it
> gets there. The boundary is not even sharp: some systems combine an explicit input-language grammar
> with semantic constraints, generating high-diversity inputs while keeping tight control over what
> each one *means* [cite: fandango2025] — a hybrid that sits between the property and fuzzing rows.
>
> **The bottom line:** these are not rungs on a maturity ladder. Choose the one whose generator and
> oracle match the engineering claim — which is the whole of the method applied to a single test.

> ### Inset — What is property-based testing, and what does shrinking do? {#inset-pbt}
>
> Ordinary tests pin one input to one expected output. A property-based test states a **law the
> output must obey for every input in a domain**, then lets a generator hunt a counterexample
> [cite: claessen2000quickcheck]. For a sort, the law is "the output is an ordered permutation of the
> input"; the framework generates hundreds of lists and checks that law on each, so you never had to
> guess which list would break the code.
>
> The generator draws from an **authored domain model** — a description of the values the law ranges
> over (bounded integers, well-formed documents, lists of them), not from arbitrary bytes. That is
> the real line between a property test and a fuzzer, and it is why a property generator can be
> *tuned* to reach boundary and adversarial-but-valid values while staying inside the domain.
>
> When the law fails, the framework **shrinks** — it walks the failing input down to the smallest one
> that still breaks the law, so the counterexample you debug is a two-element list, not a
> thousand-node document with one bad byte buried inside. The minimized case is the shrinker's work,
> not luck.
>
> **You can now** recognize a property test by its shape — a quantified law plus a domain generator —
> and read a minimized counterexample as the search having boiled the failure down. This is the
> oracle-rich, domain-bounded end of the loop; it says nothing about inputs outside the authored
> domain.

## Generate falsifying evidence

<!-- section-terms: generative-validation, stable-spec-point, producer-dialect-corpus -->

<!-- point: the-family-is-three-answers-not-three-rungs | The generative family is one move worn three ways — three answers to three signals. | terms: generative-validation, oracle -->
The move — generate inputs and search for a counterexample — answers three different signals in the
code under test, and the signal, not a technique's sophistication, picks the member.

- **A stated law over valid inputs** earns a **property test**: a round-trip, a
  content-preservation guarantee, an ordering. The generator explores the *valid* domain; the oracle
  is the law you can already write down ([Inset 2](#inset-pbt)).
- **An untrusted-bytes boundary with a never-crash contract** earns a **fuzzer**: it pushes outside,
  or toward the hostile edges of, the domain, where no per-input law is worth stating. The oracle is
  the robustness contract — don't crash, hang, or corrupt ([Inset 3](#inset-fuzzing)).
- **A structured model with a declared legal-outcome set or invariant** earns **fuzzing judged
  against that model.** Point wild inputs at the model's own surface and classify what comes back
  against its declared set. The oracle is now as rich as the model and the inputs as wild as a
  fuzzer's ([Inset 4](#inset-stateful) carries the sequence-and-state form).

<!-- point: oracle-richness-and-input-wildness-trade-off | Oracle richness and input wildness trade off; the model-based synthesis weakens the trade. | terms: oracle, mbse -->
Those three sit on one trade-off. The richer the oracle, the tamer the inputs a property test allows;
the wilder the inputs, the coarser the oracle a fuzzer can afford. Fuzzing-against-a-model takes both
halves — wild inputs, a rich model-declared oracle — at the standing cost of building and keeping
that model true to the system. It weakens the trade-off rather than abolishing it.

<!-- point: fix-a-crash-to-the-spec-point | Fix a crash to the stable point in the specification, not the failing seed. | terms: stable-spec-point, fuzzing -->
When a fuzzer surfaces a crash there are two ways to fix it. Patch the exact bytes it found — and
leave every neighbouring input that would break the same way. Or trace the failure to the point in
the format's specification the input violated, and fix *that*, so the fix closes every input the
specification allows, not the single seed that tripped it. Fixing to the stable specification point
is what promotes a raw fuzzer into a model-based one: the model *is* that stable point, written down.

<!-- point: harvest-producers-before-hand-rolling-a-grammar | Harvest real producers before hand-rolling a grammar. | terms: producer-dialect-corpus -->
> **Harvest producers before you hand-roll a grammar.** Every rival tool that writes your format has
> already generated its own legal-but-unusual dialect — unusual object orderings, obscure permitted
> structures, features the specification allows and your own writer never emits. Round-trip real
> documents through those producers and let their dialects be the adversarial corpus; the world's
> producers are a generator no single authored grammar reaches. Build a from-scratch generator only
> where no real producer exists.

<!-- point: the-shape-of-the-invariant-determines-the-search | The shape of the invariant determines the search. | terms: interleaving-search, concurrency, invariant -->
The move generalizes when the thing generated is a *sequence* rather than a datum. A **local, state
predicate** over structured inputs earns a property test; a **temporal or interleaving-dependent
invariant** earns a systematic or bounded search over interleavings. The shape of the invariant
determines the search — and naming the predicate is what predicts where the bug is, because writing
the exact condition down lets the search drive straight at the interleaving that violates it, a
defect a strong-but-static unit suite walks right past. This stands on the same
[invariants-over-models](3.2-models-and-the-semantic-gap.html) ground the rest of the book is built
on ([Inset 4](#inset-stateful) shows the sequence, state, and interleaving forms).

> ### Inset — What fuzzing adds {#inset-fuzzing}
>
> Three ideas, in rising order:
>
> - **Property testing** generates valid-but-varied members of a modeled domain.
> - **Fuzzing** pushes outside that domain, or toward its hostile edges — truncations, bit-flips,
>   corrupted offsets, megabytes of garbage appended: inputs no well-typed generator would emit, and
>   exactly where the crash lives.
> - **Coverage-guided fuzzing** keeps any input that reaches new execution territory and breeds more
>   variants from it, so the campaign steers itself toward unexplored code.
>
> **Search signal is not oracle.** Line and branch coverage tell the fuzzer *where it got* — that is
> the search signal. They do not say the behavior was *correct*, and a coverage plateau is one
> saturation signal, not proof of saturation: it can hide input variants the generator never produced.
> The baseline oracle stays coarse — did the process crash, hang, or blow up memory — and a clean
> re-parse does not by itself prove no corruption; a mangled output can still parse.
>
> Real fuzzers enrich that crash/hang oracle: sanitizers catch memory and undefined-behavior errors
> mid-run, differential oracles require two implementations to agree, and metamorphic relations check
> that related inputs produce related outputs.
>
> **You can now** read "we fuzzed it" as a claim with a coverage number behind it, and ask which
> oracle the campaign used — crash-only, or something richer.

> ### Inset — Stateful and concurrency validation {#inset-stateful}
>
> Write down a small model — a set of legal outcomes, or a state machine of legal transitions — and
> use *that* as the judge. The single-datum rule generalizes as the generated thing grows:
>
> ```
> Single-input property   input → program → invariant
> Stateful property       operation sequence → state transitions → invariant after each step
> Concurrency property    interleaving → concurrent execution → invariant over shared state
> ```
>
> A stateful test drives a queue through generated sequences of enqueue and dequeue and checks a model
> invariant after each step: a dequeued item was previously enqueued, and order is preserved. A
> concurrency test makes the *interleaving* the input and checks an invariant over shared state — no
> two workers hold the same lease; a job never leaves a terminal state.
>
> **Search strength rises with cost:** random schedules sample interleavings; controlled schedulers
> explore them deliberately; bounded state exploration enumerates them within an explicit bound, so
> "every reachable interleaving" always means within a stated depth or model size, never literally all
> of them for an unbounded system.
>
> **You can now** recognize when a test's judge is a *model* rather than a per-input assertion, and
> match the schedule-generation method to the invariant's shape — a local state predicate versus a
> temporal, interleaving-dependent one.

## Did the search cover the claim?

<!-- section-terms: grammar-coverage, coverage-model-mapping -->

<!-- point: generation-adds-a-native-coverage-question | Generation adds one coverage question native to it. | terms: grammar-coverage, measure-one-level-deeper -->
Once you have a generator, a question example tests never had to ask appears: *did the campaign
explore the space that matters, or did it just spin?* A campaign that merely runs has explored
nothing, so coverage is the honesty layer that turns "we fuzzed it" into a claim with a number behind
it. 
The degree question — *how much* coverage is enough — belongs to the [metrics treatment in
Operating MAGE](4.5-operating-mage.html), which owns it, including the discipline to **measure one level deeper**
than a raw percentage. This section takes only the cut native to generation.

<!-- point: input-space-coverage-vs-code-coverage | Coverage of the code is not coverage of the input space; a validator cannot falsify a claim in a region its generator never represents. | terms: grammar-coverage, coverage-model-mapping -->
The native cut is that **coverage of the code is not coverage of the input space.** Line and branch
coverage measure the *implementation* the inputs reached. Input-space coverage measures the *inputs
themselves* — did the generator ever produce a document with headers and footers, a cross-sheet
formula, the semantic region the claim is about? A campaign that never generates such an input has a
hole no amount of line coverage will reveal, because the handling lines were never reached by any
generated input in the first place. This is the chapter's tie to the book's larger concern with
missing models: **a validator cannot falsify a claim in a region its generator never represents**
([Inset 5](#inset-input-space) separates the two coverages).

<!-- point: model-claim-coverage-is-the-saturation-oracle | Model-claim coverage is the family's real saturation oracle. | terms: coverage-model-mapping, requirements-based-coverage, measure-one-level-deeper -->
Line and branch coverage keep "we fuzzed it" honest, but the coverage that ultimately matters is
whether the generated inputs actually drove the invariants, transitions, and edges the model
*declares* — model-claim coverage, measured over the traceability graph. That is the same
measure-one-level-deeper move the metrics treatment teaches, reused here as the saturation oracle for
validation from a model. [ref:generative-loop] draws the loop, closing on that coverage.

<!-- label: generative-loop -->
<!-- figure: assets/generative-loop.svg | *The Coin Is the Model.* Fuzzing and property testing are two faces of one loop: a **generator** makes inputs, an **oracle** judges each outcome, a **counterexample** feeds back to shrink or diagnose, and **coverage** asks whether the generator ever reached the claims the model makes — closing the loop by refining the generator. The two faces draw their oracle from **one specification family expressed at different strengths** — an authored property, an implicit robustness contract, a structured model — not from one shared artifact. -->

> ### Inset — Coverage of the generator is not coverage of the code {#inset-input-space}
>
> **Code coverage** asks what implementation the campaign reached. **Input-space coverage** asks what
> kinds of cases the campaign generated. You need both.
>
> A document fuzzer that never once generates a file with headers and footers has an input-space hole,
> and no amount of line coverage will reveal it: the lines that handle headers-and-footers were never
> reached by any generated input in the first place. High line coverage *elsewhere* tells you nothing
> about the semantic region no input ever visited.
>
> Three input-side measures hide under the name and are not interchangeable: grammar-production
> coverage (did the generator exercise every production of an explicit grammar), mutation-operator
> coverage (did it apply every kind of mutation), and producer/dialect coverage (did the corpus carry
> every real producer's accent — the case with no explicit grammar to count).
>
> **You can now** ask of any campaign not just "what code did it cover" but "what inputs did it
> cover," and pick the right input-side measure for the generator you actually have.

## Deterministic admission

<!-- section-terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: evidence-validator-gate-roles | A sensor produces evidence, a validator evaluates it, a gate controls admission; a test harness bundles the first two, and CI may gate on the verdict. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, thesis-alignment -->
These checks are **repeatable** — run one twice on the same input and it agrees — and they keep Part
III's roles distinct even when one artifact carries several. A **sensor** produces evidence; a
**validator** evaluates it against the obligation; a **gate** controls admission on the verdict. A
test harness bundles sensing and validation in one executable — it both runs the input and judges the
result — and a continuous-integration pipeline may *additionally* gate on that verdict, refusing the
merge. The bundling is convenient, not a collapse of the roles: naming which role a check is playing
is what lets you reason about where evidence is produced, where it is judged, and where it is enforced.

<!-- point: generative-validation-becomes-hard-governance-with-authority | A check becomes hard governance when its verdict is given the authority to block admission. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, thesis-alignment -->
A check **becomes hard governance when its verdict is granted the authority to control admission.**
The same fuzz campaign run only on a schedule, reporting to a dashboard, is a sensor with no
authority — it observes but cannot refuse. Wire its verdict into the commit gate and it holds the
line. A generator that re-hunts a counterexample every commit, given that authority, is the Alignment
Thesis made mechanical: the environment declining to trust the last green checkmark, and checking the
model again.

## Why agentic velocity changes the economics

<!-- section-terms: independent-evidence, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: validation-techniques-are-not-new | None of the validation techniques is new; abundant implementation changed their role, not the algorithms. | terms: independent-evidence, thesis-modeling -->
None of the techniques in this chapter is new. Property-based testing, fuzzing, model-based testing,
and their relatives have long histories in software engineering. The method does not replace them
with something novel; it gives them a different role. As coding agents make implementation
inexpensive, engineering effort shifts toward constructing trustworthy evidence about the resulting
system — so the old techniques become more central than ever, not because the techniques changed, but
because the engineering problem did.

<!-- point: agentic-era-changes-how-much-evidence | Autonomous development changes how much evidence we require, not what evidence is. | terms: independent-evidence, thesis-alignment -->
Autonomous development does not change *what* engineering evidence is. A model exposed intent, a
metric measured degradation, and a validator refused a bad change long before any agent proposed one.
What changes is *how much* of that evidence we now require, and where the burden of producing it
falls. When one reviewer wrote one feature a week, human inspection could carry most of the load. When
a fleet proposes changes faster than any person can read them, inspection cannot scale to the volume,
and assurance shifts onto the explicit representations and the automated checks that consume them.

<!-- point: evidence-must-outrun-inspection | Evidence must now be generated faster than a human can inspect. | terms: independent-evidence -->
So the chapter's central concept — *independent evidence*, generated to falsify a claim, admitting
only what survives — stops being a supplement and becomes the load-bearing floor. The reviewing
engineer does not disappear; a human gate on a consequential decision is one of the strongest
validators there is. But the engineer's judgment is now spent designing the models, choosing the
metrics, and setting the gates, rather than re-reading every diff. The environment does the
re-checking, on every change. That is what lets confidence keep pace with abundance.

## Industrial example — Docker

<!-- section-terms: independent-evidence -->

<!-- worked-examples: independent-evidence -->
<!-- point: independent-evidence-is-not-a-docable-idiosyncrasy | Placing the grader outside the author is a general move, not a DocAble idiosyncrasy. | terms: independent-evidence -->
Here is the move; one industrial team converged on it independently. Docker's autonomous coding fleet
builds the author–grader separation into its architecture: the agent that writes a change never
grades it. An independent reviewer with its own model invocation, a test suite the team determinizes
so its verdict is repeatable, and a human gate stand between every pull request and main. The fleet
may open a pull request; it may not merge one. The philosophy is theirs, sharply put: reviewing
outcomes while skipping the changes themselves "is not a security model, it's a prayer."

<!-- takeaway -->
The shared move is *independent evidence*: never admit a change on its own author's word — generate
evidence built to falsify it, and let through only what survives the search. Separate the author of a
change from its grader, and put the grader where the author cannot reach it; the separation is the
mechanism, and the specific tools are interchangeable. Docker is evidence the move scales to a full
autonomous fleet on a live product; DocAble runs the whole family at depth against its own hard
problem — generating strong evidence that a remediated document says no less than the original,
without claiming the certainty of a proof. Part V tells the DocAble story in full, and
[MAGE in the Wild](6.5-mage-in-the-wild.html) sets Docker beside other systems as evidence the
practice travels.
