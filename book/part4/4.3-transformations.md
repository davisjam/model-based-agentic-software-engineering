# Everything Is a Transformation

<!-- point: frame-every-task-as-a-series-of-sized-transformations | Frame every task as sized input-to-output transformations. | terms: transformation, sizing-the-leap -->
<!-- index-def: transformation -->
<!-- index-def: sizing-the-leap -->
Whatever its internal architecture, an
agent is easiest to govern when each step has an explicit input, an explicit output, and a checkable
transformation between them. So frame every task you hand it as a series of transformations — and let
the skill of using an agent be sizing each transformation to the model and to the guarantee you need.
This chapter is about that sizing, and about how the three skills are all built from it.

> *A note for the curious — what is the machine underneath?* You can size transformations without any of
> this, but here is the shape. At each step, the foundation model maps its current token context to a
> distribution over possible next tokens; the harness turns those repeated predictions into a longer
> action sequence. Nobody wrote that mapping by hand — its billions of internal dials were tuned
> automatically, over a mountain of text, until its output matched the patterns in the data. The
> architecture that let this approach scale to today's models is the *transformer*, introduced in the
> 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" [cite: vaswani2017attention]. Its name points at an attention
> mechanism, not at a rule that you should design your tasks as transformations — that habit earns its
> place on its own, above.

## Size the leap to the model and the guarantee

<!-- section-terms: sizing-the-leap, compounding-failure-probability -->

<!-- point: tune-abstraction-and-leap-size-to-model-and-guarantee | Tune abstraction and leap to model and guarantee. | terms: sizing-the-leap, conditioning-the-search -->
Give a model an input and either examples of a transformation or a precise description of it,
and it will apply that transformation to something new — as long as the new thing is close
enough to the examples, or the rule is precise enough. Better models generally support a bigger
*leap*, but task fit and representation can dominate nominal strength. A strong model turns abstract
instructions into working code; a weak one turns the same instructions into a dumpster fire, or
nothing that compiles — and even a strong model stalls when the task sits far from anything it has
seen. So you tune two things together: the level of abstraction you speak at, and the size of the leap
you ask for — against the model's strength and against how much guarantee you want.

<!-- point: keep-leaps-short-because-failure-probability-compounds | Keep leaps short because failure probability compounds. | terms: compounding-failure-probability, reasoning-trace -->
<!-- index-def: compounding-failure-probability -->
The trade has a sweet spot, and you find it by counting the stages you can see from outside the model —
the independent decisions, the state transitions, the artifacts produced, the seams where you can check a
result. (You cannot count a model's internal "reasoning steps"; those externally visible stages you can.)
Want high guarantees? Ask for smaller leaps, and check the result of each one as it lands. Want speed? Ask
for bigger leaps, and accept that you trade away some of that per-step trust. Neither direction is free:
smaller leaps add coordination and admission overhead but can save rework, while bigger leaps cut handoffs
yet make a failure harder to localize and check. The right size is big enough to use the model's full
power, but not so big that it becomes a long chain of dependent decisions with no check between them — and
every additional dependent step is another place for state to drift (the toy arithmetic of how that risk
accumulates is in the inset below). Size the transformation to the task's complexity and the model's scale,
and you keep the failure probability where you can live with it.

> *A note for the curious — how does per-step risk add up?* Give each step an independent one-percent
> chance of going wrong. Two steps in a row are almost always fine. A hundred are not: the chance that at
> least one step fails is 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%. Treat that as a toy — real steps are not independent, and
> their error rates are neither equal nor constant — but the direction is the lesson. A long, unaudited
> chain accumulates risk faster than intuition expects, which is the reason to check results as they land
> rather than at the very end.

Read the arithmetic forward and it becomes a design rule. Because independent per-step risk
compounds, the fix is not a better model at the end of a long chain — it is a **shorter chain**:
size each transformation small enough that a checkable boundary sits at every seam, so the
compounding never gets room to run. A short transformation is not just less likely to fail; it is
a place you can *check*, and that is what caps the risk.

## The right-sized leap, and the wrong-shaped one

<!-- section-terms: sizing-the-leap, transformation -->

Two transformations from this book show the lens at work — one sized right, one shaped wrong.

<!-- point: sizing-the-leap-down-buys-cheap-auditable-typed-edits | Sizing the leap down buys cheap, auditable edits. | terms: sizing-the-leap, closed-vocabulary -->
The remediation core could ask a strong model for the whole leap: here is a document, hand back an
accessible one. That is a single enormous transformation, and a strong enough model can, of course,
sometimes make it. But it is expensive — the model reasons over the entire document on every call —
slow, and unauditable, because you cannot see which change it made or why. So the system sizes the leap down.
It scopes the work to a closed vocabulary of small typed edits — set the alt text on this image, set
the role of this block, reorder these children — the same edit language the editor speaks in Part 5.
The agent is asked for one bounded edit at a time; each call reasons over one scoped task instead of
the whole document, and each edit comes back typed, stamped, and reversible. The
transformation-sizing lens changes a decision the loop-and-model lens does not:
that lens says *call the model to remediate*; the sizing lens says *call it for one edit at a time* —
which, in DocAble, made the operation cheaper, faster, and auditable.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [remediation verbs](appendix-c-remediation-verbs.html).

<!-- point: a-well-sized-transformation-of-the-wrong-shape-still-fails | A well-sized transformation of wrong shape still fails. | terms: transformation, wrong-shape -->
Sizing is only half of it. The transformation also has to be the right *shape* for the task and the
model. I learned this the expensive way. For two weeks I sized a transformation beautifully and
pointed it at the wrong problem — pulling a PDF's reading order out of the geometry of its bounding
boxes, and handing that geometry to cheap vision models, which are bad at geometry. The model
cheerfully made a doomed shape better and better. A well-sized transformation of the wrong shape is
still the wrong transformation. So the skill has two halves: sizing the leap, and finding the one
leap that fits both your task and your model — then abandoning the one that does not.

Once you see everything as a sized transformation, the three skills reveal a common skeleton.
Each holds a set of models the agent reasons over, and each names the steps — the transformations
— that carry an input to an output. What you supply, in your own context, is the breakdown: the
playbooks and runbooks for the steps your work actually takes.

<!-- point: the-catalogue-reads-like-a-symptom-to-mechanism-lookup | The catalogue reads as symptom-to-mechanism lookup. | terms: transformation, governance-catalogue -->
That last point runs through the whole book: each skill
takes a general reasoning task and breaks it into a series of transformations over inputs into
outputs, with steps the agent can follow. This is not a coincidence of design — it is a useful operating
model for working with agents: represent a long task as a sequence of explicit, checkable transformations.
It is why the companion catalogue can be *read* like a lookup table: you arrive with a symptom and leave
with the mechanism, because the entry is the transformation between them. Work with agents through this
lens — every task a sized sequence of input-to-output steps — and you will find yourself a much happier
engineer.
