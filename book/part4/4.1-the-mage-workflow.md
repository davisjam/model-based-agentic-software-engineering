# The MAGE Workflow

<!-- point: mage-has-three-recurring-moves-model-align-convert | MAGE has three recurring moves: Model, Align, Convert. | terms: modeling-principle, alignment-principle, governance-conversion -->
MAGE has three recurring moves: **Model, Align, Convert**. Model the engineering knowledge or intent
that should survive the current reasoning step. Give stable obligations authority at a boundary where
they can actually be evaluated. When work repeatedly exposes missing knowledge, evidence, judgment, or
authority, convert the lesson into durable structure. Then run the cycle again. Each pass asks where
consequential judgment is still being purchased per realization: what judgment should be better
supported, what should be given authority, and what should deliberately remain open.

<!-- point: the-ordering-is-methodological-not-a-logical-dependency | The ordering is methodological, not a logical dependency. | terms: modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->
The ordering is methodological. Local controls can act before a rich system model exists. MAGE asks
about representation first because explicit models enlarge the engineering questions the environment
can answer. Choose the representation that makes the question tractable, then attach authority where
the obligation is stable enough and the evidence adequate enough to justify it.

<!-- box-family: canonical -->
> ### The MAGE cycle
>
> Model the intent. Give stable obligations authority. Convert recurring judgment into durable engineering structure.
>
> **Repeat.**

## One Turn at a Time

<!-- point: a-turn-begins-with-a-need-and-honesty-about-where-uncertainty-lives | A turn begins with a need; keep the uncertainty explicit. | terms: modeling-principle -->
**Need.** A turn begins with something you want to learn, change, preserve, or make true. Sometimes
that need is already precise: an endpoint must require authentication; this transformation must
preserve content; this state transition must never occur. Sometimes it is not: onboarding feels
confusing, operators think the system is slow, customers may want a different workflow. Do not treat
the second kind as a defective specification waiting for an agent to elaborate. It is an unresolved
engineering question.

MAGE does not require certainty before work begins. Keep the uncertainty explicit. Preserve what is
already known; explore what is not. A non-negotiable security boundary may
deserve authority immediately while the product behavior inside that boundary remains experimental. As
evidence accumulates, promote stable claims into requirements, models, invariants, and — where
justified — authoritative mechanisms.

Be precise about what you know, experimental about what you do not, and make learning durable as it
stabilizes.

<!-- point: model-at-the-least-unnecessary-detail-not-to-reach-the-top-of-a-ladder | Model at the least detail that answers the question. | terms: modeling-principle, governed-environment -->
**Model.** Start from the engineering question and choose a representation whose semantics make that
question tractable. A structural model may expose dependency or reachability; a behavioral model may
expose legal states and transitions; an ownership model may expose control and transfer; a decision model
may expose what is permitted; a measurement model may expose a quantity against a bound; and a provenance
model may expose lineage and evidence. Use only the distinctions the question requires. The representation
earns its cost when later work benefits enough to justify building and maintaining it.

Do not confuse explicitness with certainty. A model can represent the current behavior, the
constraints already known, and the measurements needed for exploration while leaving the proposed
behavior provisional. Modeling an unsettled hypothesis can help reason about it; giving that
hypothesis binding authority merely because it is machine-readable is a different move. **Uncertain
intent should not acquire premature authority.**

<!-- point: exploration-is-an-instrument-of-inquiry-and-delegability-comes-from-the-whole-environment | Exploration is an instrument of inquiry; delegability comes from the whole environment. | terms: modeling-principle, governed-environment -->
**Explore or implement.** If the problem is still being discovered, implementation can itself become
an instrument of inquiry. Build cheap alternatives, measure them, put them in front of users, or
construct the smallest prototype that can falsify the assumption. Commodity intelligence reduces the
implementation cost of this exploration. That does not make the resulting code authoritative:
prototype to learn, then preserve what stabilizes in the representations and controls that deserve
to persist.

Once the task is sufficiently settled, let the fleet work against the representations, procedures, and
controls appropriate to it. Delegability — the degree to which a settled task can be safely handed to the fleet — comes from the **whole engineered environment**.

<!-- point: align-places-authority-at-the-earliest-boundary-with-the-semantics-to-decide-it | Align places authority at the earliest boundary that has the semantics to decide it, on evidence independent of the producer. | terms: alignment-principle, generative-validation -->
**Align and evaluate.** Decide which obligations deserve environmental authority and place that
authority at the earliest boundary where the obligation is legible and enforceable. Ask what role the
environment needs to play: constrain an inadmissible action, sense relevant state, validate evidence
against the obligation, or gate whether work may advance. One mechanism may play several roles. Some
obligations are local enough to decide at an action boundary; others require a whole work unit, a
system-level model, runtime evidence, or human judgment. The mechanism must have the semantics and
evidence needed to decide the obligation it carries.

Prefer evidence independent of the producing agent. Such evidence can be used to challenge and test
the producing agent's claim: re-run the check, derive the state, measure the property, search for
counterexamples, or send the result to an independent reviewer.

<!-- point: convert-when-the-future-return-justifies-the-upkeep | Convert the lesson when the future return justifies the upkeep. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**Convert.** Work will expose gaps. A repeated failure may reveal missing representation, a missing
obligation, inadequate observability, a weak validator, or authority placed at the wrong boundary. A
repeated manual judgment may reveal a procedure worth preserving. Diagnose the class rather than
reflexively adding another gate.

When the future return justifies the upkeep, make the lesson durable: improve the model, state the
invariant, add the missing evidence, mechanize repeatable judgment, remove an unsafe action, or
package a recurring procedure.

<!-- point: repeat-forms-capital-that-is-maintained-and-retired-not-merely-accumulated | Repeat: capital is formed, maintained, and retired. | terms: modeling-principle, alignment-principle, governance-conversion -->
**Repeat.** Models drift, controls become noisy, assumptions expire, and mechanisms can outlive the
failures they prevent. Maintenance, reconciliation, and retirement belong in the cycle. The objective
is a productive governed environment.

## Closing the Loop {#the-conversion-loop}

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, audits-into-lints -->

<!-- index-example: governance-conversion -->
<!-- point: when-judgment-repeats-ask-what-future-work-should-inherit | When judgment repeats, ask what future work should inherit from it. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**When judgment repeats, ask what future work should inherit from it.** The answer may be a model,
invariant, sensor, validator, constraint, gate, runbook, or skill. Some recurrences are too cheap or
unstable to encode.

<!-- index-def: audits-into-lints -->
<!-- point: capital-formation-not-mechanism-accumulation | Price the conversion against the recurring cost it retires. | terms: governance-conversion, audits-into-lints -->
Price the conversion against the recurring cost it retires. A repeated mechanical review may earn a
validator; repeated reconstruction may earn a model; a catastrophic failure may earn prevention before
recurrence.

<!-- point: the-improvement-loop-has-familiar-ancestors-the-changed-condition-is-abundance | The improvement loop has familiar ancestors; the changed condition is implementation abundance. | terms: governance-conversion -->
<!-- gloss-only: Jidoka | The Toyota Production System's stop-the-line move: halt the instant a defect appears, so nothing downstream builds on it; the manufacturing lineage of the sensor's fail-the-iteration reflex. -->
<!-- gloss-only: Kaizen | Continuous improvement as the Toyota Production System practices it: every recurring problem becomes a permanent countermeasure; the manufacturing lineage of MAGE's convert-a-failure-into-a-mechanism loop. -->
<!-- gloss-only: Poka-yoke | Mistake-proofing, from the Toyota Production System: shape the work so the defect cannot occur (the fixture that refuses a part mounted backwards); the manufacturing lineage of the constraint. -->
The improvement loop has familiar ancestors: mistake-proofing [cite: shingo1986zqc], *jidoka*,
continuous improvement, resilience engineering [cite: hollnagel2006resilience], and the postmortem
discipline of converting incidents into durable changes [cite: beyer2016sre]. Implementation abundance
changes the economics: autonomous work can expose gaps faster than human attention can repeatedly
absorb them, increasing the return on durable engineering structure.

## Guidance or Authority? {#guidance-or-authority}

<!-- section-terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->

<!-- index-def: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
<!-- point: guidance-aims-authority-binds | Guidance aims; authority binds consequences. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
Keep one distinction sharp. **Guidance aims; authority binds consequences.** Here, bind means that
the result no longer depends on the agent's cooperation: the environment can deny an action, reject a
result, or block admission. A brief, convention, example, or skill can strongly influence an agent,
but the agent can still misunderstand or ignore it. A type, permission, or validator-backed gate acts
outside that cooperation. A useful diagnostic for any step is to ask: *what consequential judgment does
this step still trust the agent to make?*

<!-- point: do-not-make-authority-the-automatic-destination-of-every-guidance | Do not make authority the automatic destination of every piece of guidance. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
Do not make authority the automatic destination of every piece of guidance. Authority is appropriate
when the obligation is sufficiently stable, the mechanism can evaluate it at the right semantic
boundary, and the cost of violation justifies the restriction. Guidance remains appropriate where
judgment is unsettled or where the cost of mechanization would exceed the failure it prevents.

<!-- point: must-never-happen-is-a-signal-not-proof-the-mechanism-follows-the-property | "Must never happen" names a candidate obligation; choose the mechanism the property allows. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
"Must never happen" identifies a candidate obligation. Find the property and the boundary where it can
be decided, then choose the mechanism appropriate to that property.

## Engineer the Judgment {#engineer-the-judgment}

<!-- section-terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->

<!-- point: mage-improves-recurring-work-two-ways-support-the-reasoner-or-give-obligations-authority | MAGE improves recurring work two ways: improve the conditions under which a reasoner judges, or give selected obligations authority so the consequence does not depend entirely on the reasoner. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
Not every important judgment should become deterministic. MAGE provides two complementary ways to improve recurring work. One is to improve the conditions under which a reasoner makes a judgment: give it better representations, domain knowledge, examples, procedures, tools, and context so that a satisfactory realization becomes more likely. The other is to give selected obligations authority in the environment, so that the consequence does not depend entirely on the reasoner choosing correctly.

<!-- point: the-distinction-is-two-effects-not-two-artifacts | The distinction is not between two kinds of artifact but between two effects: a model, skill, or piece of evidence can both support a reasoner and feed an authoritative mechanism. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
The distinction is not between two kinds of engineering artifact, but between two effects those artifacts can provide. A model can guide an agent and state properties consumed by a validator. A skill can improve judgment and invoke authoritative controls. Evidence can inform a decision or feed an admission gate. A governed engineering environment composes these roles rather than assigning each artifact permanently to one side.

<!-- point: the-boundary-is-not-fixed-judgment-can-acquire-authority-over-time | The boundary is not fixed: as a judgment becomes better understood, some of what depended on it can acquire authority, while other portions properly remain probabilistic. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
Nor is the boundary fixed. A judgment that initially resists authority may become better understood through repeated use, failure, and refinement. Its obligations may become clearer, suitable evidence may become available, or a qualitative distinction may become evaluable with sufficient reliability. At that point, some of what previously depended on judgment can acquire authority in the environment. Other portions may properly remain probabilistic. [ref:engineering-recurring-work] shows the recurring cycle.

<!-- label: engineering-recurring-work -->
<!-- figure: assets/engineering-recurring-work.svg | *Engineering recurring work.* A governed engineering environment combines support for probabilistic reasoning with authority over selected obligations; the same models, knowledge, procedures, skills, tools, and evidence may contribute to both. The dashed arrow marks a recurring MAGE move: as judgment becomes better understood, some obligations may become sufficiently clear and evaluable to receive authority. The return path observes outcomes and failures and improves the environment; where recurring judgment can be encoded economically, this is the governance conversion developed in Section 3.4. -->

The return path includes the governance conversion developed in Section 3.4. When an outcome or failure exposes missing representation, obligation, evidence, evaluation, or consequence, the organization can encode that missing structure so that future work inherits it. Not every observation warrants such conversion: some judgments remain contextual, some failures are not recurring, and some structure costs more than it returns. The worked examples through the rest of this Part are each marked GOVERNANCE CONVERSION and show that move at a different site — a representation, an obligation, evidence, an evaluation, a consequence, and a judgment deliberately left informal.

<!-- point: the-move-toward-authority-is-an-opportunity-not-a-required-progression | The move toward authority is an opportunity, not a required progression; the relevant unit is the obligation, not the task. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
The dashed arrow is an opportunity, not a required progression. MAGE does not assume that engineering knowledge should eventually become deterministic, or even authoritative. Some judgments remain qualitative because the underlying objective is contextual, plural, or difficult to observe independently. Others contain narrower obligations that can receive authority even while the larger judgment remains open. The relevant unit is therefore the obligation, not the task.

Consider the obligation to produce "good writing." No single construct called good writing admits a sound checker, so the broad judgment stays contextual and plural. Narrower obligations can still be drawn from it: some admit deterministic checks, others admit useful but probabilistic evaluation, and either can receive authority where its reliability and the consequence make that appropriate. A style guide supports the reasoner across all of them, whether or not the environment can enforce a given slice. Repeated experience can move the boundary — a preference recorded as prose may later become a precise rule or a measured evaluator — but the broad judgment need not, and often should not, be mechanized at all.

<!-- box-family: inset -->
<!-- inset-domain: GOVERNANCE CONVERSION -->
<!-- inset-size: small -->
> #### Inset — Judgment: the limits of "good writing" {#inset-gc-judgment}
>
> **Observed.** "Good writing" recurred as an objective for generated prose, but there is no single property *good writing* that a checker can decide, so a reviewer judged it case by case.
>
> **Changed.** Rather than mechanize the whole judgment, the environment converted only the slices that admitted it: deterministic checks for spelling, grammar, length bounds, and prohibited terms; probabilistic evaluators for narrower qualities such as whether a paragraph has an effective topic sentence. The style guide continued to carry the rest.
>
> **Result.** The checkable slices became enforceable; the broad judgment stayed with the style guide and human review — deliberately unconverted. Not every obligation warrants machinery, and treating this one as if it did would have manufactured a checker no evidence supported.

Modeling an artifact can turn empirical knowledge about its operating range into an obligation the environment can state and enforce. Evidence that a system works across many inputs supports judgment about its range; it does not by itself establish a bound. A model of the artifact can expose the relation that does, so a previously judgment-dependent property becomes precise enough to state and give authority. DocAble's residency model states such a quantitative invariant,

<!-- eq: \text{peak worker RSS} \le C -->

where, within the declared operating envelope,

<!-- eq: C = \text{rolling stream buffer} + \text{structural skeleton} + \text{handles} + \text{remediation working set} -->

and C does not grow with total media size. The architecture can enforce the assumptions behind that bound, while measurement checks whether the realized system behaves as the model predicts. What began as judgment supported by examples became a narrower property for which the system could provide substantially stronger evidence and authority.

<!-- box-family: inset -->
<!-- inset-domain: GOVERNANCE CONVERSION -->
<!-- inset-size: small -->
> #### Inset — Obligation: from memory evidence to a residency bound {#inset-gc-obligation}
>
> **Observed.** DocAble's memory safety rested on accumulated examples: simple presentations worked, real teaching decks worked, and eventually hundreds of documents from several universities worked. Those observations supported judgment about the operating range but could not say what a much larger media payload would do. A roughly 250 MB presentation exposed the boundary of that evidence.
>
> **Changed.** Modeling the artifact separated its structural skeleton from its media, exposing the relation that mattered — persistent worker memory need not scale with total media size — and turned an operating-range hunch into a stated residency invariant.
>
> **Result.** A narrower property the architecture can enforce and measurement can check, backed by far stronger evidence and authority than the earlier "many documents worked."

The stronger mechanism still does not establish that DocAble "uses memory well," any more than spelling checks establish good writing. It governs a property that became precise enough to state and enforce: within the modeled envelope, total media size does not determine peak worker memory. Other dimensions — pathological structure, an individual object beyond the supported bound, excessive concurrency, or something not yet modeled — remain outside that claim. The progression from examples to a bound therefore illustrates the dashed arrow: modeling can make previously judgment-dependent obligations feasible to give authority.

<!-- point: authority-is-not-determinism | Authority is not determinism: a deterministic checker can remain advisory, and a probabilistic evaluator can receive authority; the two dimensions should not be conflated. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
These examples also separate authority from determinism. A deterministic checker can remain advisory, while a probabilistic evaluator can receive authority if its error characteristics and the consequence make that appropriate. Deterministic enforcement provides stronger assurance for a covered decidable property; authority determines whether the environment can act on the obligation independently of the producing reasoner. The two dimensions should not be conflated.

## Prefer Structural Prevention {#structural-prevention}

<!-- section-terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, alignment-principle, governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: prefer-structural-prevention-when-the-action-space-can-honestly-be-closed | Prefer structural prevention when the legitimate action space can be closed; otherwise preserve the action space and add independent evidence. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, alignment-principle -->
Prefer structural prevention when the legitimate action space can be closed. A typed API, a sanctioned
mutation seam, a closed verb set, or a forbidden dependency can make an invalid move simply
unavailable, and the reviewer need not catch that class of mistake because the governed interface does not offer it.
Where prevention would exclude legitimate behavior, or the property only becomes visible after the
work runs, preserve the action space and add independent evidence instead.

<!-- point: place-the-strongest-honest-mechanism-where-the-property-permits-it | A property that can be made unrepresentable belongs in a constraint; one visible only after execution needs evidence and evaluation. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
Where an invalid state can be made unrepresentable without excluding legitimate behavior, prefer a
constraint. A property visible only after execution needs evidence and evaluation.

<!-- point: docable-used-both-sides-of-the-alignment-principle | DocAble used both sides at once: close the action space where it could, validate independently where a property was only legible after the change. | terms: alignment-principle, independent-evidence -->
DocAble put both sides to work at once. It narrowed document repair to a closed set of bounded,
typed edits, routed ordinary document-format mutation through sanctioned structured seams, and
rejected direct raw-library access from code outside those seams. It turned recurring review findings
into build-failing checks — prevention wherever the action space could be honestly closed. It then
validated content preservation as a post-condition on every run,
because "the output still says no less than the input" is a property that only becomes legible
*after* the transformation.

## Size Work to Reasoning and Assurance {#task-sizing}

<!-- section-terms: one-shot-scripting, supervised-autonomy, sizing-the-leap, transformation -->

<!-- index-def: one-shot-scripting -->
<!-- point: not-every-task-deserves-the-full-apparatus-of-governed-autonomy | Not every task deserves the full apparatus of governed autonomy. | terms: one-shot-scripting, supervised-autonomy -->
Not every agent task deserves the full apparatus of governed autonomy. A bounded transformation whose
relevant state fits comfortably in one pass, whose output is cheap to inspect or mechanically verify,
and whose failure is cheap to reverse can often be delegated directly. Write the contract, let the
agent produce the result, check it, and move on.

<!-- index-def: supervised-autonomy -->
<!-- point: two-things-shorten-the-leash-reasoning-burden-and-assurance-need | Size the task along two axes: reasoning burden and assurance need. | terms: supervised-autonomy, reasoning-horizon -->
Size the task along two axes: reasoning burden and assurance need. The first is **reasoning burden**: how much intermediate state the task
must preserve before it can produce a coherent answer. Large systems, cross-cutting changes, and long
sequences of dependent decisions push that burden upward. The second is **assurance need**: how
costly, irreversible, or difficult to detect a bad result would be. A five-line authorization change
may have a short reasoning horizon and still deserve strong validation; a large disposable prototype
may tolerate far less.

<!-- index-def: transformation -->
<!-- index-def: sizing-the-leap -->
<!-- index-def: compounding-failure-probability -->
<!-- point: size-work-as-transformations-with-checkable-boundaries | Size work as transformations with explicit inputs, outputs, and checkable boundaries. | terms: transformation, sizing-the-leap, compounding-failure-probability -->
Size work as a sequence of transformations with explicit inputs, outputs, and checkable boundaries.
Make each step large enough to exploit the model's capabilities but small enough that its result can
be meaningfully evaluated before the next step compounds it. Better representation can support larger
steps because it reduces the state the reasoner must reconstruct; stronger models may support larger
steps too. Neither removes the need for an evidence boundary where the consequence warrants one.

<!-- point: docable-sizes-the-leap-to-bounded-typed-edits-the-unit-of-delegation-lines-up-with-a-unit-of-evidence | DocAble sizes the leap to bounded typed edits, so the unit of delegation lines up with a unit of evidence. | terms: sizing-the-leap, transformation -->
DocAble's remediation edit language is the concrete pattern. Rather than ask a model to rewrite an
entire document opaquely, the system asks for bounded, typed edits — set this alt text, reorder these
children, change this role. Each edit can be stamped, reversed, and evaluated. The **unit of delegation should line up with a unit of evidence**.

<!-- point: task-shape-matters-as-much-as-task-size | Task shape matters as much as task size; a well-sized transformation of the wrong shape still fails. | terms: transformation -->
Task shape matters as much as task size. A perfectly bounded transformation aimed at the wrong
representation still fails. If a model performs poorly on geometry, carving the geometric problem into
smaller geometric calls does not rescue the framing. Change the representation or change the division
of labor. MAGE sizes work to the reasoner, the available representation, and the assurance required —
not to an arbitrary line count.
