# The MAGE Workflow

<!-- point: mage-has-three-recurring-moves-model-align-convert | MAGE has three recurring moves: Model, Align, Convert. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, governance-conversion -->
MAGE has three recurring moves: **Model, Align, Convert**. Model the engineering knowledge or intent
that should survive the current reasoning step. Give stable obligations authority at a boundary where
they can actually be evaluated. When work repeatedly exposes missing knowledge, evidence, judgment, or
authority, convert the lesson into durable structure. Then run the cycle again.

<!-- point: the-ordering-is-methodological-not-a-logical-dependency | The ordering is methodological, not a logical dependency. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
The ordering is methodological. You can constrain an agent with a permission, type, sandbox, or local
gate before you possess a rich model of the system. MAGE nevertheless asks about representation first
because explicit models change the scale of the questions the environment can answer. A permission can
forbid one action; an architectural model can make a property spanning many actions legible enough to
govern. The method therefore reaches first for the representation that makes the engineering question
tractable, then attaches authority where the obligation has earned it.

<!-- thesisbox -->
> ### THE MAGE CYCLE
>
> Model the intent. Give stable obligations authority. Convert recurring judgment into engineering capital.
>
> **Repeat.**

<!-- point: parts-two-and-three-supplied-the-vocabulary-part-four-the-operating-rule | Parts II and III supplied the vocabulary; Part IV supplies the operating rule. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, governance-conversion -->
Parts II and III supplied the vocabulary. Part IV supplies the operating rule: use these moves
proportionately, only where the problem has earned them.

## One turn at a time

<!-- point: a-turn-begins-with-a-need-and-honesty-about-where-uncertainty-lives | A turn begins with a need, and honesty about where the uncertainty lives. | terms: thesis-modeling -->
**Need.** A turn begins with something you want to learn, change, preserve, or make true. Sometimes
that need is already precise: an endpoint must require authentication; this transformation must
preserve content; this state transition must never occur. Sometimes it is not: onboarding feels
confusing, operators think the system is slow, customers may want a different workflow. Do not treat
the second kind as a defective specification waiting for an agent to elaborate. It is an unresolved
engineering question.

MAGE does not require certainty before work begins. It requires **honesty about where the uncertainty
lives**. Preserve what is already known; explore what is not. A non-negotiable security boundary may
deserve authority immediately while the product behavior inside that boundary remains experimental. As
evidence accumulates, promote stable claims into requirements, models, invariants, and — where
justified — authoritative mechanisms.

Be precise about what you know, experimental about what you do not, and make learning durable as it
stabilizes.

<!-- point: model-at-the-least-unnecessary-detail-not-to-reach-the-top-of-a-ladder | Model at the least unnecessary detail, not to reach the top of a ladder. | terms: thesis-modeling, governed-environment -->
**Model.** Ask what representation makes the engineering question answerable at the least unnecessary
detail. The answer may be a prose model, a diagram, a typed contract, a graph, a state machine, an
invariant, or a measured relation. You do not earn points for reaching the top of a modeling ladder.
You earn value when the representation prevents a later engineer or agent from reconstructing the same
fact from lower-level detail.

Do not confuse explicitness with certainty. A model can represent the current behavior, the
constraints already known, and the measurements needed for exploration while leaving the proposed
behavior provisional. Modeling an unsettled hypothesis can help reason about it; giving that
hypothesis binding authority merely because it is machine-readable is a different move. **Uncertain
intent should not acquire premature authority.**

<!-- point: exploration-is-an-instrument-of-inquiry-and-delegability-comes-from-the-whole-environment | Exploration is an instrument of inquiry; delegability comes from the whole environment, not a model alone. | terms: thesis-modeling, governed-environment -->
**Explore or implement.** If the problem is still being discovered, implementation can itself become
an instrument of inquiry. Build cheap alternatives, measure them, put them in front of users, or
construct the smallest prototype that can falsify the assumption. Commodity intelligence reduces the
implementation cost of this exploration. That does not make the resulting code authoritative:
prototype to learn, then promote what you learned into the representations and controls that deserve
to persist.

Once the task is sufficiently settled, let the fleet work against the representations and constraints
appropriate to it. Models help by externalizing relevant system knowledge; briefs and skills can
package recurring procedure; permissions, tools, validators, and gates bound what the environment will
permit or accept. Delegability comes from the **whole engineered environment**, not from a model
alone.

<!-- point: align-places-authority-at-the-earliest-boundary-with-the-semantics-to-decide-it | Align places authority at the earliest boundary that has the semantics to decide it, on evidence independent of the producer. | terms: thesis-alignment, generative-validation -->
**Align and evaluate.** Decide which obligations deserve environmental authority and place that
authority at the earliest boundary where the obligation is legible and enforceable. Some obligations
are local enough for a type, permission, or action hook. Others require a whole work unit, a
system-level model, runtime evidence, or a human judgment. The test is not whether the obligation can
be written down; it is whether the chosen mechanism has the semantics and evidence needed to decide
it.

Prefer evidence independent of the producing agent. Re-run the check, derive the relevant state,
measure the property, search for counterexamples, or route the result to a reviewer who did not
produce it. The agent's claim that the work is correct is evidence about what the agent intended, not
sufficient evidence that the environment should accept it.

<!-- point: convert-when-the-future-return-justifies-the-upkeep | Convert the lesson when the future return justifies the upkeep. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**Convert.** Work will expose gaps. A repeated failure may reveal missing representation, a missing
obligation, inadequate observability, a weak validator, or authority placed at the wrong boundary. A
repeated manual judgment may reveal a procedure worth preserving. Diagnose the class rather than
reflexively adding another gate.

When the future return justifies the upkeep, make the lesson durable. Improve the model. State the
invariant. Add the missing evidence. Turn a repeatable judgment into a validator. Remove an unsafe
action structurally. Package a recurring procedure so it no longer has to be re-explained. This is
governance conversion in the broader method: engineering work performed once changes the starting
conditions inherited by later work.

<!-- point: repeat-forms-capital-that-is-maintained-and-retired-not-merely-accumulated | Repeat: capital is formed, maintained, and retired — not merely accumulated. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, governance-conversion -->
**Repeat.** The point is not that every turn leaves more machinery behind. The point is that useful
judgment can become an asset rather than being consumed once. A model saves future reconstruction; a
constraint retires a failure class; a validator retires a recurring review judgment; a skill preserves
a procedure. Those assets form engineering capital.

Capital is not immortal. Models drift, controls become noisy, assumptions expire, and a mechanism
whose upkeep exceeds the failure it prevents has depreciated into overhead. Operating MAGE therefore
includes maintenance, reconciliation, and retirement as ordinary parts of the cycle. The objective is
not a larger governed environment. It is a more productive one.

[ref:precision-follows-knowledge] draws the shape the loop rides: precision is earned as knowledge
stabilizes, not assumed at the start.

## Closing the loop {#the-conversion-loop}

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, audits-into-lints -->

<!-- index-example: governance-conversion -->
<!-- point: when-judgment-repeats-ask-what-future-work-should-inherit | When judgment repeats, ask what future work should inherit from it. | terms: governance-conversion -->
Part III developed governance conversion in full. In practice, the reflex is short: **when judgment
repeats, ask what future work should inherit from it**. Sometimes the answer is a model; sometimes an
invariant, sensor, validator, constraint, gate, runbook, or skill; sometimes the recurrence is too
cheap or too unstable to justify encoding at all.

<!-- index-def: audits-into-lints -->
<!-- point: capital-formation-not-mechanism-accumulation | Conversion is capital formation, not mechanism accumulation. | terms: governance-conversion, audits-into-lints -->
This is capital formation, not mechanism accumulation. The useful question is what recurring cost the
new structure retires and whether the expected return exceeds its maintenance cost. A repeated review
of a mechanically decidable property is a candidate validator. A recurring reconstruction of system
structure is a candidate model. A rare inconvenience may deserve nothing more than a note. A
catastrophic one may deserve prevention before it ever recurs.

The cycle therefore does not say "second occurrence, add a lint." It says **recognize repeated
expenditure of engineering judgment and decide whether to turn it into an asset.**

<!-- point: the-improvement-loop-has-familiar-ancestors-the-changed-condition-is-abundance | The improvement loop has familiar ancestors; the changed condition is implementation abundance. | terms: governance-conversion -->
<!-- gloss-only: Jidoka | The Toyota Production System's stop-the-line move: halt the instant a defect appears, so nothing downstream builds on it; the manufacturing lineage of the sensor's fail-the-iteration reflex. -->
<!-- gloss-only: Kaizen | Continuous improvement as the Toyota Production System practices it: every recurring problem becomes a permanent countermeasure; the manufacturing lineage of MAGE's convert-a-failure-into-a-mechanism loop. -->
<!-- gloss-only: Poka-yoke | Mistake-proofing, from the Toyota Production System: shape the work so the defect cannot occur (the fixture that refuses a part mounted backwards); the manufacturing lineage of the constraint. -->
This improvement loop has familiar ancestors: mistake-proofing [cite: shingo1986zqc], *jidoka*,
continuous improvement, resilience engineering [cite: hollnagel2006resilience], and the postmortem
discipline of converting incidents into durable changes [cite: beyer2016sre]. MAGE does not claim those ideas as new. The
changed condition is implementation abundance: autonomous work can expose gaps faster than human
attention can repeatedly absorb them, increasing the return on converting sound engineering judgment
into durable structure.

## Guidance or authority? {#guidance-or-authority}

<!-- section-terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->

<!-- index-def: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
<!-- point: guidance-aims-authority-binds | Guidance aims; authority binds consequences. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
Keep one distinction sharp. **Guidance aims; authority binds consequences.** A brief, convention,
example, or skill can strongly influence an agent, but the agent can still misunderstand or ignore it.
A type, permission, validator-backed gate, or constrained API acts outside that cooperation.

<!-- point: do-not-make-authority-the-automatic-destination-of-every-guidance | Do not make authority the automatic destination of every piece of guidance. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
Do not make authority the automatic destination of every piece of guidance. Authority is appropriate
when the obligation is sufficiently stable, the mechanism can evaluate it at the right semantic
boundary, and the cost of violation justifies the restriction. Guidance remains appropriate where
judgment is unsettled or where the cost of mechanization would exceed the failure it prevents.

<!-- point: must-never-happen-is-a-signal-not-proof-the-mechanism-follows-the-property | "Must never happen" is a signal, not proof; the mechanism follows the property, not the rhetoric. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
A phrase such as *must never happen* is a useful signal, not proof that a blocking gate is the right
implementation. First ask what property must hold and where it can be enforced. Prevention may belong
in an API or type; detection may belong in a validator; some obligations remain human. The mechanism
follows the property, not the rhetoric.

## Prefer structural prevention where the action space closes {#structural-prevention}

<!-- section-terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, thesis-alignment, governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: prefer-structural-prevention-when-the-action-space-can-honestly-be-closed | Prefer structural prevention when the action space can honestly be closed; otherwise preserve the action space and add independent evidence. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, thesis-alignment -->
Prefer structural prevention when the action space can honestly be closed. A typed API, a sanctioned
mutation seam, a closed verb set, or a forbidden dependency can make an invalid move simply
unavailable — the reviewer never has to catch the mistake because the environment never offered it.
Where prevention would exclude legitimate behavior, or the property only becomes visible after the
work runs, preserve the action space and add independent evidence instead.

<!-- point: place-the-strongest-honest-mechanism-where-the-property-permits-it | The choice is not prevention-good-detection-bad; it is to place the strongest honest mechanism at the level where the property permits it. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
The choice is not "prevention good, detection bad." It is to place the strongest honest mechanism at
the level where the property permits it. This is the same constraint / sensor / validator / gate
vocabulary Part III used to separate prevention, observation, judgment, and admission — read here as
a placement rule rather than a menu. A property you can make unrepresentable wants a constraint; a
property legible only after execution wants a sensor, a validator, and, where the consequence
warrants it, a gate.

<!-- point: docable-used-both-sides-of-the-alignment-thesis | DocAble used both sides at once: close the action space where it could, validate independently where a property was only legible after the change. | terms: thesis-alignment, independent-evidence -->
DocAble put both sides to work at once. It narrowed document repair to a closed set of bounded,
typed edits, routed every mutation through one typed document model with the raw library banned, and
turned recurring review findings into build-failing checks — prevention wherever the action space
could be honestly closed. It then validated content preservation as a post-condition on every run,
because "the output still says no less than the input" is a property that only becomes legible
*after* the transformation. Neither side is the whole answer. The invalid move is made unavailable
where that is cheaper and more reliable than catching it; the property that resists prevention gets
independent evidence at the boundary that can decide it.

## Size the work to the reasoning and the assurance {#task-sizing}

<!-- section-terms: one-shot-scripting, supervised-autonomy, sizing-the-leap, transformation -->

<!-- index-def: one-shot-scripting -->
<!-- point: not-every-task-deserves-the-full-apparatus-of-governed-autonomy | Not every task deserves the full apparatus of governed autonomy. | terms: one-shot-scripting, supervised-autonomy -->
Not every agent task deserves the full apparatus of governed autonomy. A bounded transformation whose
relevant state fits comfortably in one pass, whose output is cheap to inspect or mechanically verify,
and whose failure is cheap to reverse can often be delegated directly. Write the contract, let the
agent produce the result, check it, and move on.

<!-- index-def: supervised-autonomy -->
<!-- point: two-things-shorten-the-leash-reasoning-burden-and-assurance-need | Two things shorten the leash: reasoning burden and assurance need. | terms: supervised-autonomy, reasoning-horizon -->
Two things shorten the leash. The first is **reasoning burden**: how much intermediate state the task
must preserve before it can produce a coherent answer. Large systems, cross-cutting changes, and long
sequences of dependent decisions push that burden upward. The second is **assurance need**: how
costly, irreversible, or difficult to detect a bad result would be. A five-line authorization change
may have a short reasoning horizon and still deserve strong validation; a large disposable prototype
may tolerate far less.

<!-- index-def: transformation -->
<!-- index-def: sizing-the-leap -->
<!-- index-def: compounding-failure-probability -->
<!-- point: size-work-as-transformations-with-checkable-boundaries | Size work as transformations with explicit inputs, outputs, and checkable boundaries. | terms: transformation, sizing-the-leap, compounding-failure-probability -->
Size work as a sequence of transformations with explicit inputs, outputs, and checkable boundaries.
Make each step large enough to exploit the model's capabilities but small enough that its result can
be meaningfully evaluated before the next step compounds it. Better representation can support larger
steps because it reduces the state the reasoner must reconstruct; stronger models may support larger
steps too. Neither removes the need for an evidence boundary where the consequence warrants one.

<!-- point: docable-sizes-the-leap-to-bounded-typed-edits-the-unit-of-delegation-lines-up-with-a-unit-of-evidence | DocAble sizes the leap to bounded typed edits, so the unit of delegation lines up with a unit of evidence. | terms: sizing-the-leap, transformation -->
DocAble's remediation edit language is the concrete pattern. Rather than ask a model to rewrite an
entire document opaquely, the system asks for bounded, typed edits — set this alt text, reorder these
children, change this role. Each edit can be stamped, reversed, and evaluated. The point is not that
small is always better. It is that the **unit of delegation should line up with a unit of evidence**.

<!-- point: task-shape-matters-as-much-as-task-size | Task shape matters as much as task size; a well-sized transformation of the wrong shape still fails. | terms: transformation -->
Task shape matters as much as task size. A perfectly bounded transformation aimed at the wrong
representation still fails. If a model performs poorly on geometry, carving the geometric problem into
smaller geometric calls does not rescue the framing. Change the representation or change the division
of labor. MAGE sizes work to the reasoner, the available representation, and the guarantee required —
not to an arbitrary line count.
