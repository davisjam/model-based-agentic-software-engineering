# The Dynamics of MAGE

MAGE can look static when described one principle at a time. {{chapter:modeling}} introduced models as projections of consequential properties; {{chapter:alignment}} introduced Alignment as the mechanisms that establish and preserve correspondence between those models and a realization. A working engineering system, however, is dynamic. Models are discovered, realizations change, new properties become consequential, and knowledge accumulated during one change constrains the next.

Engineers do not interact with agents through a single interface. On the Modeling side of [ref:engineer-agent-interaction], natural language, specifications, and models play different roles. Natural language supports inexpensive, fluid interaction: asking questions, expressing intent, exploring alternatives, and requesting changes. A specification makes obligations on an acceptable realization explicit. Models make selected properties and relationships explicit so that they can participate directly in reasoning about the system. These forms complement one another. A model does not eliminate conversation, and neither does a specification.

The return path is similarly varied. On the Alignment side, review, testing, and analysis provide different forms of evidence about the resulting realization. Review permits contextual human judgment. Testing observes selected executions. Analysis reasons about properties that may not be adequately established by sampled executions alone. The engineer interprets this evidence against the relevant specification and models, deciding whether the realization is acceptable, must change, or has exposed something that should change the engineer's understanding.

These interactions do not prescribe a fixed sequence of activities. Models make selected knowledge available to guide delegated work; review, testing, and analysis provide evidence for evaluating the result. Alignment uses that evidence to establish or preserve correspondence: an engineer may require a correction, or a gate may reject a change that violates an obligation. An engineer may move among these interactions repeatedly during a single change.

These channels differ in their governing force. Natural language and specifications remain useful throughout engineering, but preserving an obligation as the system changes requires additional machinery. A consequential property should survive beyond the prompt, conversation, or review in which it was first articulated. Once the property is sufficiently understood, Modeling makes it explicit and automated Alignment keeps the realization in correspondence with it. Engineers can therefore leave ordinary exchanges informal while making consequential properties durable.

Three ideas describe the resulting dynamics: alignment can operate actively or passively; models can be brought into governance incrementally around an alignment kernel; and the models themselves can emerge from engineering work rather than being specified completely in advance.

## Alignment Changes the System

Alignment is where delegation meets validation. Agents can perform the implementation, and the engineering environment establishes whether the resulting work satisfies the obligations that matter. It can also constrain actions before they occur. Active alignment changes the realization to establish correspondence with a model—“Make it so they match.” Passive alignment checks that subsequent changes preserve that correspondence. [ref:active-passive-alignment] shows the two modes and the handoff between them.

In active alignment, the current implementation may not satisfy a newly introduced model. The environment can expose the disagreement, agents can modify the implementation, and the result can be evaluated again. That loop continues until the realization satisfies the modeled obligations.

In passive alignment, a proposed change is evaluated against governing models and their obligations. The environment may reject the change, require evidence, constrain what an agent can do, or otherwise prevent the realization from drifting outside the modeled region.

Once active alignment establishes correspondence, the model and its checks remain available for later changes. That reusable structure is engineering capital: subsequent work inherits an obligation and machinery for evaluating it.

This matters because active alignment is not necessarily behavior preserving. Asking an agent to make a state machine explicit, replace an architectural boundary, separate storage from computation, or satisfy a new ownership model may require substantial changes to the realization. Those changes may satisfy the new model while damaging some other property that has not yet been made visible to the environment. That possibility creates an ordering problem.

## Establish a Kernel Before Expanding

The first models brought into governance should protect the properties that subsequent transformations must not be allowed to damage. Together, these models form the alignment kernel.

{{chapter:modeling}} organized models by the properties they represent. The kernel instead concerns their order of introduction: protect the properties that an upcoming transformation must preserve, then expand the governed surface under those existing constraints ([ref:alignment-kernel]).

Additional models extend the governed surface, but each new alignment operates subject to the constraints established by the models already in the kernel. The layers are not different classes of model; they show models such as those introduced in {{chapter:modeling}} becoming governing constraints over time. -->

The order is system-dependent. For a conventional information system, behavioral correctness and state management may belong in the initial kernel: later structural changes must preserve what the system does. For an embedded system, resource properties such as latency, memory, or energy may need to enter first. An implementation may contain unusual structure precisely because it satisfies a timing constraint. Asking an agent first to make an implicit state machine explicit could invite a reasonable-looking refactoring that removes those optimizations. If the timing model is already aligned and enforced, the agent remains free to restructure the implementation—but only within the performance envelope that must survive.

A newly aligned model can join the protected environment for the next episode. Adoption therefore proceeds incrementally; it does not require a complete model of the system before governance begins.

The kernel should grow only while additional models justify their cost. A model earns its place by making a consequential property cheaper to understand, predict, constrain, or evaluate than leaving that knowledge implicit. The kernel is large enough when the consequential degrees of freedom are under control.

## Greenfield and Brownfield MAGE

Incremental adoption is easiest to see in a brownfield system. A realization already exists. Engineers can observe it, identify consequential properties, induce models from the implementation and their own knowledge, actively align the system where correspondence is missing, and retain the resulting models and mechanisms in the GEE. The governed surface grows around a system that was already running.

A greenfield project might appear to invite the opposite strategy: construct the models first, align implementation to them, and only then allow the system to emerge. That is sometimes appropriate when obligations are already well understood. It is not the general MAGE prescription.

Software engineering exists in part because software changes. Requirements turn out to be incomplete. Apparently obvious abstractions prove wrong. Interactions become consequential only after something runs. A specification can reduce degrees of freedom without eliminating uncertainty about which choices should ultimately be fixed.

Early in a project, some degrees of freedom therefore have information value. Cheap implementation can be used to explore them. A loose specification can produce one or several prototypes; those prototypes expose missing requirements, useful abstractions, resource constraints, awkward interactions, and assumptions that were invisible on paper. The engineer then has better evidence about what should become explicit structure.

## What Cheap Implementation Changes {#what-cheap-implementation-changes}

Agentic implementation makes this strategy unusually cheap. In that setting, even vibe coding has a legitimate engineering role: it can serve as a kind of 3D printer for software, rapidly realizing a loose specification so that engineers can learn about the system they actually need. The mistake is not using cheap implementation. The mistake is confusing an exploratory realization with a sufficiently governed one.

Cheap implementation has another consequence: agents can produce unnecessary complexity at extraordinary speed. In my experience, coding agents readily introduce abstractions, architectural layers, and mechanisms that are reasonable in isolation but inappropriate for the system at hand. A design pattern may be sound, yet add more machinery than the problem warrants; a general solution may accommodate cases the system will never encounter. Empirical studies of AI-generated changes identify overengineering and poor contextual fit as reasons for rejection or reversion [cite: oukhay2026aicodedoesntstick;ogenrwot2026patchtrack]. Agents can also explain and defend these choices convincingly. The engineer must judge whether the proposed structure fits the actual requirements, existing architecture, and likely evolution of the system. Parsimony is therefore an active engineering responsibility: deciding not only whether a design works, but whether its complexity is justified.

Much of my work with agents comes down to deciding what is an implementation detail and what is not. I generally do not care what a variable is called or how an ordinary function is organized, provided the result satisfies its obligations. I do care whether the implementation crosses an architectural boundary, introduces a new dependency, or changes the behavior of a service that other components rely on. The difficulty is that these distinctions are not always apparent in advance. Sometimes an agent makes a perfectly reasonable choice, I look at the result, and discover that I care about something I had never thought to specify. That is part of engineering. The next step is to decide whether the newly discovered obligation should become explicit, so that future work does not have to discover it again.

A useful rule is to distinguish three kinds of decisions. If a choice follows mechanically from an established model, generate it rather than asking an agent to reason it out repeatedly. If several implementations satisfy the obligations, delegate the choice and let the agent explore them. If experience reveals a consequential decision that later work will face again, preserve it in the engineering environment. That may mean recording a model, adding a constraint, or establishing a check. The point is not to eliminate judgment, but to spend it where it is needed rather than repeatedly making the same decision.

Cheap implementation changes engineering experience in three related ways.

<!-- box-family: canonical -->

> ### Engineering phenomena — Implementation, comprehension, and connection amplification {#phenomena-amplification}
>
> **Implementation amplification: compressed exploration.** Commodity intelligence makes it cheap to implement, inspect, measure, and reject alternatives that previously would have been too expensive to try.
>
> **Comprehension amplification: compressed understanding.** Rapid implementation helps engineers discover what they mean. Working software exposes misunderstandings that requirements and discussion leave hidden; agents make it cheaper to repeat the cycle of expressing an intent, inspecting the result, and revising the representation. **You can only learn from what you can observe.**
>
> **Connection amplification: compressed experience.** Agents surface related failures, observations, and design consequences in dense succession, where conventional development would have separated them by days or weeks. Their proximity can make relationships visible: several local failures reveal one architectural gap; a measurement exposes a missing model; one change makes the consequence of another immediately apparent.
>
> The limitation matters. A change can satisfy its functional goal while worsening performance, security, or maintainability. Fast implementation therefore increases the importance of measuring beneath the visible outcome. The DocAble case in {{sec:interlude-one-problem-many-models}} illustrates this through Ousterhout’s advice to measure one level deeper [cite: ousterhout2018].

In DocAble, related failures sometimes arrived quickly enough that what first appeared to be separate defects became recognizable as symptoms of a missing architectural concept. The shared relationship became a system model, which in turn enabled static analyses that prevented several classes of failure. Speed had changed what the engineer could see.

The engineer still judges which alternatives are worth exploring and which apparent connections matter. When experience reveals a recurring lesson, governance conversion preserves it in the environment so that later work can use the resulting representation, procedure, or control.

<!-- box-family: canonical -->

> ### Engineering move — Governance conversion {#move-governance-conversion}
>
> A recurring failure, a repeated judgment, or a newly discovered relationship is converted into durable engineering structure: a representation, a body of evidence, a procedure, or an enforced obligation. {{sec:governance-conversion}} develops the move in full.
>
> It does not decide *what* deserves capturing; that judgment stays with the engineer. It names what follows the decision: the lesson stops living in one person's memory and starts living in the environment that governs the next change.

[ref:agentic-engineering-cycle] brings these recurring activities together. Alignment operates throughout the work, while governance conversion can preserve a consequential lesson wherever it arises.

The engineers supervising this process do not begin without understanding. They had enough understanding to state the problem, write the loose specification, judge the prototype, and recognize what it gets wrong. The task is to move consequential parts of that understanding out of human memory and into explicit representations that agents and the engineering environment can use. Because agents can perform both implementation and much of the mechanical work of model induction quickly, this transition need not be a long separate modeling phase.

Leave degrees of freedom open while variation produces useful information. Once a choice becomes sufficiently understood and consequential, represent it and, where justified, align the implementation to it. This applies to greenfield and brownfield engineering alike. The difference is where the first useful evidence comes from: exploratory implementations in one case, an existing system and its accumulated engineering knowledge in the other. {{sec:brownfield-engineering}} examines how to recover that knowledge from an inherited system.
