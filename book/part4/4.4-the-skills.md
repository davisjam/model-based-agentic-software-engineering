# The Skills

Up to this point we have treated MAGE as a human engineering process. An engineer models the system, aligns the environment, supervises the work, and converts recurring failures into durable engineering capital. Once that workflow is understood, an obvious question follows: which parts of it should remain human judgment, and which recurring judgments should themselves become reusable engineering mechanisms?

<!-- point: a-skill-is-governance-conversion-applied-to-practice | A skill is governance conversion applied to engineering practice. | terms: skill-soft-control, governance-conversion -->
A skill is governance conversion applied to engineering practice. Rather than repeatedly explaining the same engineering procedure to every new agent, we encode that procedure once as reusable guidance with clear trigger conditions. Skills do not replace engineering judgment; they preserve recurring judgment so every future task begins from a stronger engineering environment. So the first question is one of recognition: which recurring procedure deserves encoding? Reach for a skill when you keep re-explaining the same judgment, the moment that calls for it has a nameable trigger, and you would rather bank the procedure than re-derive it every session.

<!-- point: the-book-ships-three-skills-of-a-portable-kind | The book ships three skills of a portable kind. | terms: self-operate, self-governance, self-communicate -->
This book's companion repository ships three such skills — **self-operate**, **self-governance**, and **self-communicate**. A method left as prose has to be re-taught to every fresh agent by hand, and a discipline that lives in memory rather than in the environment is the first thing velocity drops. A book of method ought to ship with batteries, and this one does. The architecture behind the three ports to any harness that supports conditional context and tools, and each packages the methodology of the preceding chapters as something an agent triggers and follows. This chapter walks that architecture, with the three as its worked examples.

<!-- point: a-mastery-skill-packages-knowledge-and-a-decision-procedure | A mastery-skill packages a knowledge-base and a decision procedure. | terms: skill-soft-control, mastery-skill -->
These are an unusual kind of skill. You have probably seen plenty of *tool-skills* — a skill that teaches the model an interface: to do a job with this server or CLI, here is the shape of the call. What follows is a different animal: a *mastery-skill*, which packages a body of knowledge and a disciplined decision procedure for applying it — not an interface to one tool, but a way of doing the work well. That is why the three below take a chapter to walk, where a tool-skill would take a paragraph.

Teams are converging on the same reading of a skill: encode the procedure, and a class of work becomes delegable. Atlassian's Jira team delegates keep-the-lights-on work — flaky tests, feature-flag cleanup, vulnerabilities, accessibility — by turning each into an explicit work item carrying context plus workflow instructions, so a maintenance class becomes delegable once the tacit repair procedure is encoded, not because the agent got clever [cite:atlassian2026maintenance]. Twilio's is sharper: an unguided agent asked to "verify users" hand-rolls a six-digit one-time-password, while its skill redirects to a dedicated verification product and inherits rate limiting, fraud controls, and routing the operator never thought to request [cite:twilio2026skills].

> **Two kinds of skill.** A *tool-skill* teaches an interface — the calls, flags, and shapes for
> operating one tool. A *mastery-skill* packages a knowledge-base and a decision procedure for doing a
> task well, independent of any single tool. This book's three skills are mastery-skills;
> [appendix: appendix-skill-recipe] is the full recipe for building one.

<!-- point: the-three-skills-harden-run-and-explain-one-substrate | The three harden, run, and explain one substrate. | terms: self-operate, self-governance, self-communicate -->
The three are complements: one hardens the fleet, one runs it, one explains it — the same system seen from three angles, drawn in [ref:three-skills]. Read them by the job, not the name. You reach for whichever one matches the work in front of you — harden, run, or explain — and the skill is that job made installable.

## The construction recipe is a pattern of its own

<!-- point: every-skill-is-base-model-orthogonal-models-and-a-skill-md | Every skill: base model, orthogonal models, SKILL.md. | terms: per-model-template, model-zoo, orthogonal-models -->
The three feel of a piece because they were all built the same way. Three layers, every time. A **base model of the domain** at the bottom. **Orthogonal models** layered on top — cuts through the base along independent axes, each adding a dimension without disturbing the others. And a top-level **`SKILL.md` of principles** that ties the models together and tells the agent when to reach for which. Set the three skills side by side along those layers, as [ref:skill-construction-matrix] lays out, and the shared skeleton — drawn in [ref:skill-recipe] — shows through.

## Self-governance: the catalogue and the posture

<!-- section-terms: self-governance, governance-catalogue -->

<!-- point: recurrence-signals-a-missing-sensor-or-constraint | Recurrence signals a missing sensor or constraint. | terms: self-governance, sensor, constraint -->
<!-- index-def: self-governance -->
The first mastery-skill, self-governance, triggers on a simple signal: a bad thing happened more than once — sometimes just once, if it was bad enough. Recurrence is the clue that you are either failing to *detect* the problem (you need a sensor to produce evidence of it) or failing to *prevent* it (you need a constraint, a wall). You have not put the wall in the right spot, or you have not put a badge reader on the door.

<!-- point: the-skill-ships-a-catalogue-plus-a-minting-posture | The skill ships a catalogue plus a minting posture. | terms: governance-catalogue, ex-ante-governance, governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: governance-catalogue -->
At its center is a catalogue of governance mechanisms and worked examples — roughly eighty in this edition, and still growing as new failures surface new mechanisms. I did not know these were the mechanisms I would need until I started building and kept hitting failures. The higher the velocity rose, the more failures appeared, and new *kinds* of failure with them, and each mechanism I invented to kill one went into the catalogue. The hope is that this becomes ex-ante governance for *you*: the mistakes I made and solved become a tested starting catalogue rather than an empty page — install it and say "audit my codebase and help me find room for governance." You will find your own failures too, because you run a different model, different constraints, different customers, different operators, and all of it shapes your agent's behavior. So the skill ships not just the catalogue but a **posture** — the heuristics for minting new mechanisms well.

<!-- point: minted-rules-live-in-the-boot-context-mission-method-rules | Minted rules live in the boot context: mission, method, rules. | terms: governed-environment, ex-ante-governance -->
Where do the minted rules live? In the harness's always-loaded project context — the one document every agent boots with, so it is where governance either binds or does not. Every harness has one such surface; whatever it is called, give that surface three parts, coarse to fine.

> ### The boot-context document: Mission, Method, Rules
>
> - **Mission** — what the project optimizes for, the values that break a tie. *Mission orients.*
> - **Method** — the engineering principles that hold across any project of this kind, independent of the
>   domain. *The method teaches judgment.*
> - **Rules** — the specific, stably-numbered guardrails this codebase enforces, each a one-line
>   boot-context statement pointing at the deeper doc that carries it in full. *The rules bind.*
>
> A self-governance skill grows the third part as failures earn new rules.

If you want a scaffold to start your own, the [CLAUDE.md rule index](appendix-a-claude-md-rule-index.html) appendix ships a **starter** download built from a real, mature one.

<!-- section-terms: teetering-tower, right-level-of-enforcement -->

<!-- point: avoid-a-teetering-tower-of-governance | Avoid a teetering tower of governance. | terms: teetering-tower, right-level-of-enforcement, constraint -->
<!-- index-def: teetering-tower -->
The first heuristic is to **avoid a teetering tower of governance.** Do too much of this and tending the tower becomes the work itself: the agent side-quests into polishing the linter while the product waits. Three symptoms tell you the tower is teetering.

- **Upkeep crowds out product.** Agents spend more time servicing controls than doing product work.
- **The checks cry wolf.** False positives dominate — the controls flag more than they catch.
- **The guarded failure is cheap and rare.** The control defends against something both low-cost and low-frequency.

Each is a governance whose upkeep outweighs its benefit. When you see one, size the control the way [Migrating to MAGE](4.2-brownfield.html) sizes any governance — against the cost and the frequency of the failure it prevents — and when you say "this must *never* happen," remember you are choosing a wall, and every wall constrains the agent. A well-placed wall is pure gain; a wall across the only fire exit is a disaster the day there is a fire. Because of this the skill will not silently enforce a mechanism without checking with you — you are the engineer who knows the context.

<!-- point: a-passive-skill-needs-a-hook-to-fire-it | A passive skill needs a hook to fire it. | terms: skill-soft-control, hook-hard-control, reflection-hook -->
A skill on its own is *passive*: it sits and waits to be invoked, and an agent heads-down in the work will not stop to invoke it. A hook brings it to life — the improvement loop from [The MAGE Workflow](4.1-the-mage-workflow.html) run without waiting for a human to notice the recurrence. Fire it often enough that a repeated failure shows up while its cause is still fresh. The hook fires, the prompt lands, and the skill reaches into its catalogue of every way you might handle this class of failure. From that catalogue it **resolves the mechanically classifiable cases and surfaces the design forks** — "we keep hitting this; here are two ideas, which should I try?" — then waits. That hand-off is deliberate: the hook makes the reflection happen, the catalogue supplies the options, and the judgment call stays yours.

> ### From DocAble: reflect while the cause is fresh
>
> In DocAble the reflection ran as a recurring practice in the orchestrator loop — not on a fixed timer,
> but often enough that a repeat surfaced while its cause was still fresh — asking whether the last stretch
> of work hit a recurring failure or solved one problem several different ways. At agentic velocity you
> cover enormous ground between reflections, so a repeat inside one window was a reliable signal that a
> mechanism was owed. Solving it several ways is a don't-repeat-yourself smell in the code; hitting the same
> *process* wall repeatedly — "I failed to create a VM," again — is a process smell.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [reflection-facet substrate](appendix-a-reflection-facet-substrate.html).

Reflection is not only for defects. It is also where an uncertain requirement is allowed to change. A prototype may reveal that the original ticket framed the wrong problem; repeated user workarounds may expose a missing concept; several apparently unrelated feature requests may turn out to be one underlying need. Do not mechanize the old formulation merely because implementation has already begun. The reflective step asks whether the implementation is wrong, the model is wrong, or the question itself has changed.

Underneath the posture sit a few reflexes the skill applies on every touch. Three of them:

> ### Self-governance — three principles
>
> - **Convert, don't just repair — audit becomes lint.** When a failure smells class-level, do not
>   only patch the instance. Build the durable mechanism that kills the class. Today's audit finding —
>   a thing you noticed by reading — is tomorrow's lint, a thing the machine notices for you. A bug
>   in more than one file means "fix the sites *and* add the check," not "fix the sites and hope."
> - **Guidance aims; machinery holds.** A mechanism is soft or hard. A soft one — a brief, a house-rule
>   — can *aim* a probabilistic agent but cannot block it, and it rots. A hard one — a lint, a gate,
>   a typed seam — *holds the line* regardless of whether the agent cooperates. Know which you are
>   minting, and never claim a mechanism is enforced when you have only recommended it.
> - **Architecture before sensors.** Prefer making the error impossible over catching it after. A
>   structured model with one sanctioned seam beats a validator that scolds you for using the wrong one.
>   Where a failure is costly, do both — belt-and-suspenders is a feature. But reach for the wall
>   first, and remember that every wall you raise also constrains the agent, so place it with care.

Even a soft mechanism can be admitted like a hard one. NVIDIA treats a skill as a deployable capability, not a static prompt, and runs it through an eight-stage supply chain — source, review, scan, evaluate, skill card, sign, catalog, sync — where scanning looks for agent-specific threats (hidden instructions, trigger abuse, excessive agency, tool poisoning, purpose/access mismatch), not only conventional software risk [cite:nvidia2026verifiedskills].

## Self-operate: the lifecycles, made installable

<!-- section-terms: self-operate, lifecycle -->

<!-- point: self-operate-holds-the-lifecycles-of-running-a-repo | Self-operate holds the lifecycles of running a repo. | terms: self-operate, lifecycle, conditioning-the-search -->
<!-- index-def: self-operate -->
The second mastery-skill, self-operate, holds the lifecycles of running a repo. Its base model is the set of recurring repository lifecycles this project needed to operate — pull a ticket and build a feature, pull a ticket and fix a bug, optimize the codebase, handle resource exhaustion. Each is written down as a model, because that is how you constrain the agent's search and bias it toward doing the work the way you want. On top of the lifecycles sit the runbooks and the state machine an orchestrator loop turns on; that is the *dispatch → work → land → tombstone* loop from [Engineering the Loop](3.1-the-agent-stack.html), now written down for the operator to run.

<!-- point: self-operates-skill-md-carries-the-operating-principles | Self-operate's SKILL.md carries the operating principles. | terms: self-operate, done-is-a-claim -->
The layer that makes it a mastery-skill and not a folder of scripts is the SKILL.md — the principles that decide how each lifecycle is run. Four carry the weight:

- **Orient positive before you hunt a break.** Know the healthy baseline for each lifecycle first; most sessions are steady-state, and you cannot recognize a break until you can state the shape it broke from.
- **Determinize the runnable, brief the judgment.** Each step is one of three kinds — a machine can do it, a brief can carry the judgment, or the call must stay a person's. Type the step and the split becomes mechanical.
- **The lifecycle is a state machine, not a habit.** An agent's life is a sequence of states with legal transitions; a habit rots in the operator's head, a state machine names every state and the move out of it.
- **"Done" is a claim, not a fact.** An agent reporting a task complete describes its intent, not the state of your repository — so you re-run the gates yourself and let a fact answer where a claim was offered.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [self-operate (operator runbook skill)](appendix-a-operator-runbook-skill.html).

Those four are the shape of the skill; [Operating MAGE](4.5-operating-mage.html) is where they are run — the lifecycles, the failure map, the runbook split, and the metrics that steer them, at full length. Here the point is narrower and it is about the *architecture*: the same three-layer recipe that built self-governance builds the skill that runs the shop.

## Self-communicate: the surprising third example

<!-- section-terms: self-communicate, lexicon -->

<!-- point: self-communicate-governs-the-agents-prose-and-diagrams | Self-communicate governs the agent's prose and diagrams. | terms: self-communicate, documentation-hierarchy -->
<!-- index-def: self-communicate -->
The third skill, self-communicate, encodes a different craft through the same skeleton: how the agent explains itself, in prose and in diagrams. Its lever is that documentation is a soft mechanism for an agent but a genuine tool for a human, so the skill carries a standard for what the fleet writes and draws. It works on two legs — for **prose**, it names the kinds of technical writing and holds the agent to claims that stay precise and terse; for **drawing**, it hands the agent a default it will otherwise fumble, reaching for a diagram grammar rather than a hand-generated image. It also owns a **lexicon**: point it at your repository and it walks the material, surfaces the terms you use with specific meaning, and writes down one word per concept — catching both a concept wearing two words and one word doing two jobs.

<!-- point: the-third-skill-matters-as-proof-the-architecture-generalizes | The third skill matters as proof the architecture generalizes. | terms: self-communicate, mastery-skill -->
The technical-writing craft behind all this is deep, and it is not this book's subject — [appendix: appendix-skill-recipe] and the companion skill files carry it in full. What earns self-communicate its place *here* is smaller and more surprising: to MAGE the skill matters less as method than as proof. Operations and governance are close cousins — both are engineering disciplines an agent runs. Writing is a different animal, and the same three-layer skeleton carries it just as well. That is the point worth half a page. The architecture generalizes past the kinds of work you would expect it to fit, which is the strongest evidence that it is an architecture and not a coincidence.

## Worked examples

<!-- section-terms: self-governance, governance-catalogue -->

Shipping the method as skills is one project's answer to a pattern the industry is finding in parallel: encoded knowledge is an asset that compounds, and each site accumulates it. Read the two examples below as moves on a path a normal team can start walking, not as platforms to envy — Part VI's [MAGE in the Wild](6.7-where-mage-fits.html) sets six such systems side by side as evidence the moves travel.

<!-- worked-examples: institutional-knowledge-capital -->
### Example — Shopify
The skill is where a recurring session-judgment stops being re-derived and becomes a durable, reusable asset. Shopify runs that move at organizational scale: every agent session executes in public and becomes a searchable artifact, and a shared session is mined into a skill and a default that every later agent inherits. The copyable move is small — make the work visible, then mine the repeated judgment into a skill — and the environment gets incrementally smarter with each mining pass instead of every session starting cold.

### Example — Zenseact
The same capital can be owned by many teams at once. Zenseact federates the skill: a central team owns the runtime and the safety envelope, while each domain team contributes an agent as little more than a directory holding a config and a skill. Domain expertise accretes as reusable skills a router surfaces on demand, so the organization centralizes the mechanism and decentralizes the judgment. The copyable move is that split — one owned runtime, many team-owned skills — which lets specialist knowledge compound without a central team having to author it.

<!-- takeaway -->
The shared move is *institutional-knowledge-capital*: hard-won engineering knowledge becomes an encoded, reusable asset that compounds, so the next agent inherits it instead of re-deriving it. DocAble ships that capital as installable batteries — three mastery-skills plus a catalogue of governance mechanisms grown from real failures; Part V tells that story in full.

## The skills are transformations {#skills-are-transformations}

<!-- section-terms: transformation, model-zoo -->

<!-- point: each-skill-is-a-sequence-of-sized-transformations | Each skill is a sequence of sized transformations. | terms: transformation, model-zoo -->
Set the three skills side by side and one skeleton shows through, drawn in [ref:transformations-pipeline]. Each holds a set of models the agent reasons over, and each names the steps — the transformations — that carry an input to an output. Self-communicate stacks language and genre; self-operate maps an event to a design to a set of launches; self-governance turns a symptom into a mechanism. Three different jobs, one shape: a general reasoning task broken into a sequence of explicit, checkable steps an agent can follow. It is why the companion catalogue can be *read* like a lookup table — you arrive with a symptom and leave with a mechanism, because the entry between them is the transformation.
