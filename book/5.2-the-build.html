Part 5 — The Evidence  ::  Chapter 24

5.2 The Build

This chapter illustrates

✓ The Printer · ✓ The Governed Engineering Environment

The previous chapter set the bar. This one establishes the chronology and scale of the build against it: from a five-minute feasibility experiment to a production system developed at a rate no individual reviewer could follow line by line. Later chapters reconstruct what particular events taught. Here the purpose is simpler: establish what changed, when it changed, and how much engineering structure accumulated around the product.

Over roughly 20 weeks of focused work, a coding-agent fleet and one primary engineer produced the application, its supporting tests and tooling, its infrastructure, and the system models used to reason about it. Table 5.2-1 gives one late-study snapshot (2026-08-03). The numbers are descriptive, not productivity claims; lines of code and commits do not tell us how much value was produced. They do establish the condition that matters for the case: implementation volume had moved beyond anything one person could meaningfully inspect by reading every diff.

The counts are just counts. The number that matters is not among them. I inspected almost none of the code. That is the engineering condition this case investigates: what has to surround abundant implementation when direct human inspection no longer scales with it?

5.2.1 It started in a meeting

The project began in a committee meeting about the very deadline the last chapter described. I ran a small experiment: I opened a chat model, fed it screenshots from one of my own slide decks, and asked it to transcribe what it saw. It worked. The model recovered real semantic content from a rendered slide — one previously difficult capability, recovering useful semantic content from a rendered slide, working well enough to build on.

That positive result turned a feasibility question into an engineering project. It proved neither remediation nor conformance: the model did not produce a corrected file, preserve reading order, validate against a standard, or know anything about the institutional workflow around the artifact. The experiment showed that one hard component had become plausible. Everything else remained to be engineered.

The original plan was modest: hand a one-week alt-text prototype to a student or two. No students materialized. I opened an empty repository, put an agent fleet on the problem, and assumed a month would be plenty.

5.2.2 Seven stages, drawn afterward

The build moved through a recognizable sequence of stages — recognizable only afterward. Each was driven by a concrete pressure: a colleague's request, a user's complaint, a deadline, a failure. Figure 5.2-1 lays out the sequence as pressure and consequence, one stage at a time.

These stages are retrospective labels, not a preplanned roadmap.

NONE OF THIS LOOKED LIKE MAGE

The stages make the build look planned because hindsight supplies names and boundaries. It was not. At the time, the work appeared as ordinary engineering pressure: a context window exhausted during a task; an unattended agent became impossible to locate reliably; a cloud bill exposed an operating assumption; a deployment failed through a coupling no static check represented.

MAGE is the compression recovered afterward, not the roadmap followed at the time.

Some events exposed missing knowledge or representation. Some exposed missing evidence or authority. Others simply forced a design decision. The value of the chronology is that it lets the reader distinguish structures that were deliberately designed, structures forced by an incident, and structures that were later strengthened when their first version proved inadequate.

Commit volume rose rapidly as the fleet expanded and remained far above a rate one human could review conventionally. During hardening, the work record shifted toward models, validation, tests, and control machinery before feature work accelerated again. Appendix G gives the weekly curve and commit-size distribution. The important fact for the case is not the precise shape of that curve but the operating condition it produced: human attention could no longer be allocated one diff at a time.

5.2.3 The controls accumulate

The growing engineering environment is visible in one especially easy-to-count class of artifacts: project-specific lints and gate scripts. Figure 5.2-2 counts those artifacts at four snapshots. Both begin at zero after the prototype and rise sharply through mechanization and hardening.

The count is evidence of investment, not of quality. Seven hundred lints are not inherently better than seventy, and a mechanism that no longer retires meaningful risk can depreciate into overhead. Nor does the history permit a clean causal split between ex-ante design and ex-post conversion: some obligations were obvious enough to encode before failure; others were introduced directly beside a repair. The repository does, however, preserve concrete evidence of the latter practice. Paired fix-and-lint tags mark commits where a code repair landed with a check against recurrence — some two hundred of them — and a smaller set of lints explicitly name the dated incident that motivated them. For the data, see The Build →

Read the curve accordingly. It does not measure a causal rate of governance conversion or the amount of engineering capital in the repository. It shows that, as implementation scaled, the project invested heavily in repeatable environmental checks rather than attempting to scale human review proportionally.

5.2.4 The support ratio: the environment outgrew the product

Another coarse measure shows where the engineering effort accumulated. Divide the source counted as support apparatus — tests, modeling, orchestration, documentation, and governance tooling — by production source at four dated snapshots. The ratio begins below parity, crosses it during mechanization, peaks during hardening, and settles a little above three-to-one at the final snapshot. Figure 5.2-3 plots it.

Call this the support ratio. Do not read three-to-one as a prescription. Lines of code are a poor unit of engineering value, generated and hand-authored artifacts differ in cost, and a smaller environment that retires the same judgment would be preferable. The ratio instead records a revealed investment in this case: once implementation volume outran direct review, substantially more source accumulated in the machinery that represented, tested, observed, and governed the product than in the product path itself.

The engineering-capital interpretation is stricter still. Support code is not capital merely because it exists. It earns that name only while future work inherits useful capacity from it — less reconstruction, earlier detection, retired review, safer delegation, or cheaper recovery. Some of this apparatus will eventually depreciate. The figure shows the stock that accumulated, not its current return.

Repository history supplies one further consistency check. Add-and-delete motion peaked during the mechanization period, when much of the system was being structurally reworked, and fell sharply afterward. Appendix G gives the path-level counts and generated-source caveats. The pattern is consistent with a period of heavy restructuring followed by stabilization; it does not establish that MAGE caused lower churn, and raw line motion is not the book's theoretical definition of churn.[^churn-field-note]

[^churn-field-note]: That restructuring had a token cost worth recording as a field observation, not a result. At one point the fleet ran on four concurrent model subscriptions; once explicit models let each agent find what it needed instead of re-deriving it, the same velocity ran on two. Too many confounders sit under that number to read it as a law — model releases, task mix, and my own habits all shifted across the interval — but it is consistent with the mechanism the models were built to serve: keeping a task inside its context window is cheaper than paying to reconstruct what a compaction lost.

5.2.5 What the build required

The feature list is less revealing than where engineering judgment went. Across the stages, the recurring work was to frame what the system should mean by accessible; discover abstractions for unfamiliar formats and workflows; decide which problems were local and which exposed a missing representation or control; build independent evidence; bound and coordinate the fleet; and operate a system whose failures increasingly crossed code, infrastructure, and deployment boundaries.

By the end of the study period, DocAble could process Office and PDF documents and pass or substantially improve the institutional accessibility checks used during development. That is narrower than proving that a blind student will find every remediated artifact usable, and the case should keep the distinction explicit. The product was not the only thing that had grown. The engineering environment around it had become larger than the production source and had absorbed much of the repeatable work direct review could no longer carry.

Receipt. By the end of the study period a representative graduate deck processed in about a minute for about a dollar on a warm-start service, against the $20,000 a hand-remediated course would cost; direct development cost was about sixty thousand dollars, most of it salary. The assumptions behind these figures are collected in the evidence ledger: Appendix G.

Field note — The counterfactual

There is one counterfactual I can state more strongly than the repository data can establish. Within the actual constraints of this project—one primary engineer, an academic budget, and the development interval reported here—DocAble was not feasible for me without generative AI. That is not a claim that a conventional engineering organization could not have built the system. Given enough engineers and enough time, of course it could. The relevant counterfactual is the project that actually existed: under those constraints, I would not have attempted a production system at this scope without commodity implementation capacity.

The leverage was not merely faster typing. The fleet implemented features, debugged failures, constructed tests and validators, refactored large regions, rewired architectures, migrated deployment platforms, and repeatedly realized alternatives that could be exercised and discarded. Cheap implementation changed the exploration budget. An architecture that once would have been too expensive to build merely to discover that it was wrong could now be tried, measured, and abandoned.

In my lived experience, the second counterfactual is equally important: GenAI made DocAble feasible; the machinery that became MAGE made sustained delegation feasible. Once autonomous change volume exceeded anything I could inspect conventionally, raw model capability was no longer enough. Continuing at that rate required increasingly explicit representations of the system, independent evidence, constraints, validators, gates, and operating machinery through which the work could be understood and governed.

This is a within-case engineering judgment, not a controlled causal estimate. Part VI turns it into the research question that can be tested outside this case: how much of the engineering loop can commodity intelligence absorb when the surrounding environment is engineered to support it?

The next chapter asks how that change altered delegation itself.
