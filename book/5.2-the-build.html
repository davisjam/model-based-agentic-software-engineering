Part 5 — The Evidence  ::  Chapter 24

5.2 The Build

This chapter illustrates

✓ The Printer · ✓ The Governed Engineering Environment · ✓ Governance Conversion

The last chapter set the bar. This one is the receipt for clearing it: how the build actually went, from a five-minute experiment in the back of a committee meeting to a production system that, by the end, was writing almost all of its own code. Every governance idea in this book was learned at a specific point on that arc, in response to a specific failure. The methods are not a theory that came first. They are the sediment left by the work.

The scale is easy to state and hard to believe. Over about 20 weeks of focused effort, a fleet of coding agents and I produced 491,090 lines of production code, 1,501,907 lines of support apparatus, 35,323 lines of infrastructure-as-code, and 28,507 lines of system models bridging the two (counted at 2026-08-03; category definitions live in the evidence ledger). On a routine day six to eight agents ran in parallel and landed on the order of 200 commits — about 1,000 a week, sustained. I inspected almost none of the code. That one fact is the engineering problem this book exists to answer: how a fleet producing more code than any human can read stays trustworthy.

5.2.1 It started in a meeting

The project began in a committee meeting about the very deadline the last chapter described. While the meeting droned on, I ran a small experiment: I opened a chat model, fed it screenshots from one of my own slide decks, and asked it to transcribe what it saw. It worked. The model recovered real semantic content from a rendered slide — the core technical problem of document accessibility, solved well enough to build on. That single positive result converted a research question into an engineering project. What it did not do defined the gap the rest of the build had to close: it did not export a corrected file, add reading order, validate against any standard, or understand anything about the institution the file lived in.

The first plan was small and conventional — hand a one-week alt-text task to an undergraduate or two — but no student staffing appeared, so the project proceeded with an agent fleet rather than a conventional implementation team. I bought a subscription, opened an empty Git repository, and told myself one month should be plenty.

5.2.2 Seven stages, drawn afterward

The build moved through a recognizable sequence of stages — recognizable only afterward. Each was driven by a concrete pressure (a colleague's request, a user's complaint, a deadline, a failure), and each enlarged both the product and the machinery that kept the product trustworthy. Table 5.2-1 lays out the sequence.

Table 5.2-1. The seven build stages — each driven by a concrete pressure, each enlarging both the product and the machinery that governs it. From a feasibility probe in a meeting to a serverless migration; the pressure column is the failure or demand that forced the stage, the last two columns what it added to the product and to the apparatus.

These stages are retrospective labels, not a preplanned roadmap. The clean stages read like a plan. They were not one. They were reconstructed afterward from ordinary engineering events: a context window exhausted mid-task and forced a handoff protocol; subscription limits turned model access into a schedulable resource; unattended runs exposed the need for authoritative agent state; an overnight build generated unexpected cloud charges and forced cost controls; deployment failures exposed couplings that static checks could not see. Each event either revealed a missing property of the system or forced a mechanism that later became part of MAGE.

The two-line summary of the whole sequence: the product grew, and the support apparatus around it grew faster. Measured in source size, that apparatus — testing, modeling, orchestration, documentation, and governance, plus the tooling that runs them — ran about 3.0 times the size of the production code, and the test suite alone was roughly the size of the product. A ratio like that reads as gold-plating until you recall that at 200 commits a day no human reads the diffs. The apparatus carries the repeatable review burden no human could perform per diff (more in the road to MAGE); the architectural and semantic judgment it cannot perform stays with me. It is the price of the velocity.

5.2.3 The velocity curve

Figure 5.2-1 plots commits per week: a steep rise as the fleet gained capability, then a dip during the hardening interval, when the work record shifts toward mechanisms and validation.

The repository's own activity records the shape of the effort. Commits per week rose steeply through the MVP stages — feasibility, PowerPoint, formats, service — then fell during hardening. Read against the commit classification, the dip is not a slowdown but a change of character: from producing features to producing the mechanisms that let features be produced safely. The classification, not the bar height, tells you what changed. Read that way, the curve is the operator's own shift from coder to architect, drawn as a line — an interpretation of the coded record, not a measurement the bars make on their own. A fleet run this hard needs machinery that holds the line when its operator cannot, because the operator's attention is exactly what runs out first.

5.2.4 The controls accumulate

The apparatus shows up as more than lines. It shows up as controls — the lints and gates the environment accrues one class at a time as the fleet surfaces the failures that demand them, nearly all discovered ex-post, obligations no up-front specification would have named. Figure 5.2-2 counts its two hardest control surfaces — project-specific lint files and gate scripts — at the same four windows the build passed through.

The curve is the accumulation, not its cause. Those lint files carry 993 registered lint specs at the final window, each a policy the environment now enforces on every agent. What the count cannot separate is which controls a failure drove and which the domain suggested up front — the two modes interleave by design, and no commit trail cleanly splits them. But the discipline that converts a failure is documented and named: a paired fix-and-lint tag marks the commits that ship a code fix beside the check that forbids its recurrence, appearing in 208 commits, and 27 lints name a specific dated incident in their own text, spot-checked as genuine conversions. Read the curve as a documented, growing discipline, then — not a measured causal rate. For the data, see The Build →

5.2.5 The support ratio: build the environment first

The apparatus outgrowing the product is not a one-time fact; it is a curve. Measure the support apparatus against the production code at four dated commits and the revealed preference of the whole build falls out in one line. Figure 5.2-3 plots it.

Read the curve as a decision, not an accident. In the prototype the apparatus was smaller than the product — 0.85×, below parity — because there was barely a product to govern. By mechanization it had crossed production to 2.48×, and by hardening it peaked at 3.68×, roughly three and a half times the code it guarded. The governance was built first, then the features leaned on it. The final-window dip to 3.06× is not a retreat: support kept growing in absolute terms; production simply grew faster as feature work resumed on the finished environment. Lines of code are a coarse proxy for effort, and this is a revealed preference across one repository, not a controlled comparison — but the apparatus crossed from sub-parity to about three times the product, built out ahead of the features that leaned on it.

5.2.6 The shape of the churn

Under the ratio sits the raw motion of the code: lines added and deleted, week over week, on the two paths that carry the product — web/, the Python service and worker, and backend/, the C# tool and rule engine. The preface named churn as the wall a fleet hits; traced per path across the four windows it has a clear silhouette. Figure 5.2-4 draws it.

Mechanization — the wave where the fleet learned to build the environment — is where both paths move hardest; backend/ alone rewrote itself in that window. Two constructs stay apart: repository motion is added and deleted lines, what git counts; churn in the theoretical sense is effort spent undoing and reconciling rather than advancing. The line motion is a proxy for restructuring intensity, not a direct measurement of wasted effort. The book-level result is the shape: restructuring peaked during mechanization, then the deletions collapsed and both paths turned net-additive, window after window — consistent with a period of structural investment followed by stabilization, though the case cannot establish that governance caused the decline.[^churn-accounting]

[^churn-accounting]: These counts come from git's own line accounting, so generated bundles and vendored trees ride along with hand-authored source. Partitioned by path, backend/ carries zero generated lines; web/ carries 14.6% of added lines, almost all in the mechanization window (24.5% there) when the TypeScript-to-JavaScript build output and wire-contract codegen were still tracked, dropping to 1–2% once that output was git-ignored; across both paths combined it is 5.2%, because backend/ dominates and carries none of it. Read the bars as a churn signal, predominantly non-generated tracked source.

That churn had a direct price in tokens. At one point I was running four concurrent Claude Max subscriptions to keep the fleet fed; once the models carried the context, the same velocity ran on two — an observation from this one case, not a measured law. The mechanism is the one this section traces: a model lets each agent find what it needs instead of re-deriving it, so the work stays in-window, off the compaction cliff where the token bill jumps. That is the tokenomics of churn, and it is why keeping a task inside the window is not merely tidier but cheaper.

Tokenomics. How many tokens it takes to get a task done — and it is not linear. Overflow the context window and the cost jumps: the harness compacts (a lossy save), and the agent then burns tokens rebuilding the context it lost. So keeping a task inside the window is not merely tidier, it is far cheaper — which is exactly what a model buys, by shrinking what the agent must hold.

The raw counts behind these three curves — support-apparatus and production lines per window, the per-path churn, and the running control-growth totals — are collected as lookup tables in the evidence ledger: Appendix H.

For a stretch of the build, nearly every unwelcome surprise came from deployment, and they shared a shape. The unit tests stayed green; the lints passed. What broke was never a statement in a file but a behavior of the running system — a cold start a hundred times its warm latency, a gate budget sized for a regime that no longer held, two services contending for a core, a dependency graph that woke in the wrong order. None of these lived in the statics. They lived in the dynamics, invisible to any check that reads one layer alone, because each coupling ran across five layers at once: the code, the lints that guard it, the tests that exercise it, the infrastructure-as-code that places it, and the deploy pipeline that sequences it.

That is the case for testing the dynamics, not only the statics. A one-off fix to a cold start clears today's surprise and leaves the next to be found the same expensive way. The durable answer turns each discovered dynamic into a mechanism that watches it: a measured cold-start number fed to a model that recomputes the worst-case path, a budget derived rather than guessed, an ordering asserted rather than assumed. The coupling is not on the diff, so a finite review never had a chance against it — only a mechanism watching the whole running dynamic holds the line as the fleet's deploys speed up.

5.2.7 The kinds of work it required

Read the build as a catalog of the kinds of work agentic development turned out to need — more useful than any feature list. The code was cheap. Everything around it was where the judgment went, and the same handful of activities recurred at every stage: framing the problem (what "accessible" even meant for a math-dense graduate deck, where the file alone underdetermines the answer); discovering abstractions (recognizing PDF as a distinct problem, and building structured models so structure was compiler-checked instead of passed as anonymous primitives); deciding local versus structural (for each failure, judging whether it was a one-off bug or the symptom of a missing mechanism — the single most repeated act in the whole record); building the checks (standards-grounded validation, content-preservation checks that assert the input survives into the output, hundreds of project lints atop a maxed-out commodity floor, a tiered regression suite from smoke tests to fuzz campaigns); governing the fleet (isolated worktrees, a pre-commit gate whose passage is proven by a hash-keyed marker file, an agent registry that authoritatively answers "who is in flight"); and operating and deploying (staged deploy gates, quota and cost controls, observability channels, audit trails, and runbooks for the failures that recur).

Learn more about these governance mechanisms: standards-grounded check engine · content-preservation checks · pre-commit gate · agent registry.

Read as a list, those are the chapters of this book. Every one answers a failure this build produced, and the recurring move that produced them all is the same: velocity surfaces a failure; judgment classifies it as local or structural; the structural ones get converted into a durable mechanism that narrows the next agent's room to fail.

By the end of the study period the system could process Office and PDF files to pass or substantially improve their accessibility scores — a representative graduate deck in about a minute for about a dollar on a warm-start service, against the $20,000 a hand- remediated course would cost. Direct development cost was about sixty thousand dollars, most of it salary, with a few thousand each in model inference, cloud hosting, and subscriptions. That is the arc: as implementation accelerated, engineering effort migrated into the environment around it — into the apparatus that held the line where a reader no longer could. That migration is the subject of the chapters that follow.
