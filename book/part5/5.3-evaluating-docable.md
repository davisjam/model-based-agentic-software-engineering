## Baselines and Evaluation {#human-remediation-as-a-reference}

<!-- point: manual-remediation-provides-a-reference-not-an-oracle | The trained undergraduate corpus provides artifacts and billed labor for a reference workflow, not expert ground truth or matched labor savings. -->

**Human reference.** Across approximately six months, {{seip_uta_workers}} trained undergraduates remediated documents. The recovered corpus contains {{seip_uta_pairs}} identifiable before/after pairs from {{seip_uta_courses}} courses.

The student outputs document an institutional workflow rather than expert-verified accessibility. I developed DocAble against standards using independently obtained documents and did not examine the students’ remediated files until near the end of development. Eligibility screening yielded {{seip_eval_eligible}} pairs, randomly split by document and stratified by format into {{seip_eval_development}} development documents and {{seip_eval_attempts}} validation documents. One validation spreadsheet had prior development exposure.

<!-- point: agent-comparison-tests-economics-without-a-capability-boundary | The agent baseline uses separate configurations and a ten-document cohort; results compare automatic findings, runtime, and inference expenditure. -->

**General-purpose agents.** Could a general-purpose agent perform the remediation directly? A separate comparison used community document tools, unlike the engineered DocAble operations reused in [Substituting the Reasoning Engine](5.2-inside-docables-software-factory.html#exploring-alternative-reasoning-engines). Opus and Fable each used one configuration on ten validation documents: five PDFs, two Word documents, two presentations, and one spreadsheet.

<!-- point: human-comparison-separates-syntax-and-semantics | The human-reference evaluation separately measures checker findings and transcript-based semantic improvements relative to the original. -->

**Evaluation measures.** The human-reference evaluation compared the original, human-remediated, and DocAble-remediated versions of each document along two dimensions. **Syntactic accessibility** concerns selected mechanically testable properties, measured with veraPDF’s PDF/UA-1 checks and Microsoft Office’s October 2026 Accessibility Checker. For each document, the study calculated the signed reduction in findings relative to the original, then summarized those reductions by format. A reduction does not establish that every accessibility obligation holds.

**Semantic accessibility** asks what information the document conveys. A GPT-5.2 rubric assessment examined text transcripts, with the original identified and the two outputs presented under randomized labels concealing their provenance. The model judged individual criteria and supplied transcript evidence. A deterministic scorer credited improvements and penalized regressions relative to the original; an output-score difference within one point defined the parity band. The rubric was refined on the development partition before the validation run.

**Results against the human reference.** DocAble completed {{seip_eval_completed}} of {{seip_eval_attempts}} remediation attempts; one PDF failed. [ref:docable-human-remediation-results] summarizes the comparative accessibility findings.

<!-- point: human-comparison-results-vary-by-property | DocAble led on PDF and DOCX median checker reductions; humans led on PPTX. Of 62 rubric scores, 20 favored DocAble, 41 fell in parity, and one favored the human output. -->

DocAble achieved larger median checker reductions for PDF and DOCX; the human workflow achieved larger reductions for PPTX. Both workflows had the same frequency of increased finding counts. The spreadsheet had no original findings, so its percentage reduction was undefined. The failed PDF’s partial artifact was excluded from completed-output comparisons.

Across the {{seip_eval_completed}} scored outputs, the semantic rubric rated DocAble better on {{seip_eval_semantic_better}}, within the parity band on {{seip_eval_semantic_parity}}, and worse on {{seip_eval_semantic_worse}}. Gains in complex-image descriptions accounted for much of its advantage. These are weighted document scores; the gain/regression panel separately counts document–criterion judgments, not unique defects.

<!-- point: semantic-example-exposes-the-checker-limit | A title-only description passed a checker but omitted table contents; a transcription improved access to those contents, while other examples favored human repair. -->

One presentation makes the distinction concrete. It contained an image of a thermochemical table. The human-authored alternative text repeated the table’s title, which satisfied a checker’s requirement for alternative text but did not communicate the entries. DocAble supplied a description and a transcription next in the reading order. This author-inspected example explains a disagreement between the measures; it is not systematic expert adjudication.

<!-- point: human-comparison-limits-define-the-result | Fallible transcript assessment, inconclusive criteria, changed checkers and unmeasured visual and screen-reader properties limit the results. -->

The semantic assessment covered only part of the intended rubric. Of {{seip_eval_rubric_rows}} criterion-level rows, {{seip_eval_inconclusive}} were inconclusive for DocAble, including {{seip_eval_adapters_pending}} whose automatic adapters were pending. Inconclusive judgments contributed no points; they were not evidence of satisfactory accessibility. Two scoring passes agreed on {{seip_eval_repeat_agreement}} of {{seip_eval_completed}} document classifications, but repeatability does not establish agreement with accessibility experts.

The evaluation did not include vision-tier or screen-reader assessments, nor systematically measure complete content preservation or visual fidelity. The Office checker had also changed since the students’ summer work, although all versions were evaluated with the same current tools. The findings show improvements on measured properties and identify remaining weaknesses. They do not establish full WCAG conformance or equivalent accessibility for outputs within the parity band.

**Results against general-purpose agents.** Opus and DocAble completed all ten; Fable timed out on one PDF. Quality varied by format. On the four PDFs completed by every configuration, DocAble had the largest median reduction in checker findings. Both agents exceeded DocAble’s reductions on the two Word documents, while human remediation led on the two presentations. Median runtime was 8.22 minutes for Opus, 7.77 for Fable, and 1.31 for DocAble; median agent inference costs were $2.466 and $3.977, compared with less than $0.50 for DocAble. These costs concern this ten-document cohort, not the larger human-reference cohort.

The result concerns particular configurations, community tools, and automatic findings. It does not establish comparable semantic quality or a lasting capability boundary between a specialized service and a general-purpose agent. The engineering problem includes acquiring a useful rubric, providing suitable tools, and delivering a workflow users can operate. A specialized service packages that accumulated work and can reduce the cost of applying it repeatedly; its advantage depends on the quality of that work, operating economics, and usability.

## Production Economics

**Runtime and inference cost.** Median elapsed time across all {{seip_eval_attempts}} attempts, including the failed attempt, was {{seip_eval_runtime_median_minutes}} minutes. Inference expenditure per completed output had a median of ${{seip_eval_inference_median_usd}} across the {{seip_eval_completed}} outputs, with a measured range of approximately ${{seip_eval_inference_min_usd}}–${{seip_eval_inference_max_usd}}. Full cloud execution costs were not measured for this cohort.

The undergraduates billed {{seip_uta_hours}} hours, or {{seip_uta_hours_per_pair}} billed hours per pair. At an assumed typical wage of ${{seip_uta_hourly_wage}}, estimated direct labor is ${{seip_uta_cost_per_pair}} per pair. These are corpus-level quantities; individual document timings and subsequent review effort were not measured.

These quantities show that the specialized system can produce remediation outputs quickly and with little inference expenditure. The human reference averaged {{seip_uta_hours_per_pair}} billed hours per document pair; DocAble’s median unattended runtime was {{seip_eval_runtime_median_minutes}} minutes per attempt. The labor average and elapsed-time median describe different populations and different measures of production effort. Estimating labor savings would additionally require measuring the competent review and correction needed after automated remediation and human remediation. To the best of my knowledge, the faculty whose courses were manually remediated have not been surveyed regarding quality of the result and the need for subsequent changes. Faculty using DocAble have reported follow-up work ranging from a few minutes to tens of minutes per document.

<!-- point: the-accounting-prices-the-case | Operating the product, building the product and factory, and changing the product incur distinct costs. -->

<!-- point: measured-production-cost-leaves-review-and-carrying-cost-open | Measured inference expenditure, estimated development investment, and observed change capacity establish different things; per-change cost remains unmeasured. -->

**Product and factory costs.** A software factory incurs costs in building the machinery for production, using that machinery to change the software, and maintaining it as the software evolves. The resulting product also has operating costs. DocAble illustrates these distinctions:

- **Cost to operate the product.** The preceding measurements establish low inference expenditure and short processing times. Full cloud execution costs and the human effort needed to review or correct outputs remain additional costs.
- **Cost to build the product and factory.** I estimate that developing DocAble over six months cost approximately $110,000: $90,000 in engineering labor and $20,000 in cloud expenditure. This investment produced both the remediation service and its supporting engineering machinery. Some expenditure was avoidable: despite my software-development experience, I had limited cloud-operations expertise. For example, I omitted cleanup requirements when directing Claude to deploy DocAble, allowing approximately 2,000 obsolete deployments to accumulate and contributing to a cloud bill of about $1,000. These figures do not represent optimized development costs.
- **Cost to change the product.** Once the factory was established, coding agents could implement substantial changes while I directed engineering decisions and evaluated their consequences. The development activity in {{sec:inside-docables-software-factory}} illustrates this capacity, but does not isolate the cost of individual changes or establish a general productivity multiplier.

## Limits of the Case {#liability}

<!-- point: the-claim-is-bounded-one-engineer-one-ecosystem | The evidence has clear limits: one longitudinal case, one primary engineer, one model ecosystem, no controlled comparison — the case establishes mechanism and sequence, not prevalence. | terms: field-report -->

The case follows one primary engineer directing multiple coding agents within one contemporary model ecosystem, without a controlled comparison against another engineering process. It cannot establish that another organization will encounter the same failures, develop the same mechanisms, or achieve the same economics. What it does establish is mechanism and sequence: these pressures occurred, these responses followed, and selected structures persisted under continuing change.

<!-- point: turn-outward-to-other-factories | DocAble illustrates one response to increased implementation capacity; other organizations begin with different resources and constraints. -->

DocAble illustrates one response to increasing implementation capacity: an engineer constructed models, controls, and production processes while directing agents to build the product. How have other organizations organized their factories as agents take on more implementation work?
