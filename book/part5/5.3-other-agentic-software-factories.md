# Other Agentic Software Factories

<!-- point: one-system-one-engineer-one-problem-so-how-do-others-do-it | Amazon's internal reports and Kiro's public harness documentation provide the next comparison after DocAble; other accounts expose different parts of factory design and operation. -->

DocAble showed how one engineer assembled a factory around coding agents. Amazon provides a useful next comparison: its reports of internal use can be read alongside Kiro’s public harness documentation. We can examine which engineering responsibilities the harness supplies and which remain with the adopting team. The other cases broaden the comparison to factories whose public accounts expose different parts of their design and operation.

<!-- point: seven-reconstructions-differ-in-representation-direction-evidence-and-judgment | Seven companies' public accounts differ in representations, direction, evidence, human judgment, and whether the factory serves the organization's own work or other adopters. -->

In this section, I reconstruct agentic software factories from public reports by seven companies: Amazon, Uber, GitLab, Cloudflare, Siemens, Spotify, and Shopify. These factories differ in what they represent, how they direct and constrain agents, what evidence they require, and where they retain human judgment. They also differ in a more basic way: some organizations are building factories for their own software, while others are building factories for somebody else to use.

<!-- point: the-public-sources-are-interested-accounts-read-conservatively | The sources are company-published accounts that mix technical communication with promotion, so reported architectures and measurements count as evidence of what an organization built and observed, while effectiveness and causal claims are treated cautiously and numbers keep their own definitions and denominators. -->

These sources also require a conservative reading. Company engineering blogs, talks, and product documentation mix technical communication with organizational and product promotion. They can provide evidence about mechanisms, measurements, and operating experience, but they are not independent evaluations of the systems they describe. I therefore treat reported architectures and measurements as evidence of what an organization built and observed, while treating broader claims about effectiveness or causality more cautiously. Missing information is recorded as missing rather than interpreted as evidence that a mechanism does not exist. Numerical claims are retained with their reported definitions and denominators where available; incompatible measures are not treated as directly comparable.

<!-- point: commercial-cases-share-an-emphasis-on-organizing-agentic-production | The commercial systems differ in architecture and organizational setting but share an emphasis on organizing agentic production — supplying context, structuring work, controlling tool access, arranging human review, and determining what may proceed — which matches process-oriented accounts of agentic software engineering. -->

The commercial systems examined here differ substantially in architecture and organizational setting, but they share a recognizable emphasis on the organization of agentic production: supplying context, structuring work, controlling access to tools and resources, arranging human review, and determining what changes may proceed. This emphasis maps well to process-oriented approaches to agentic software engineering advocated by Hassan and by O'Connell, which structure agent work through explicit workflows, artifacts, gates, evidence, and human intervention.[cite: hassan2026; oconnell2026] The cases below show how commercial organizations are engineering these concerns in practice.

<!-- point: two-dimensions-who-supplies-and-how-intent-enters | The factories differ along two dimensions: who supplies the production system, and how engineering intent enters production. -->

{{sec:the-software-factory}} defined a software factory as a production system for controlled change. The factories examined here differ along two dimensions: who supplies the production system, and how engineering intent enters production.

<!-- point: internal-factory-versus-factory-in-a-box | The first distinction is between an internal factory, built and operated by an organization to change its own software, and a factory in a box, a generalized production system supplied to other organizations. -->

The first distinction is between an *internal factory*, built and operated by an organization to change its own software, and a *factory in a box*, a generalized production system supplied to other organizations.

> **Factory in a box.** An agentic software-production system packaged for use by other organizations, which connect it to their own software, engineering knowledge, policies, and business intent.

<!-- point: existing-environment-first-versus-model-first | The second distinction concerns how engineering intent enters the factory: existing-environment-first factories give agents repositories, tests, pipelines and organizational context to recover what they need, while a model-first factory places explicit engineering models upstream of realization. -->

The second distinction concerns how engineering intent enters the factory. Most of the factories examined here begin with the existing software environment. Repositories, tests, pipelines, issue systems, service catalogs, policies, and other organizational context provide the material from which agents recover what they need to perform a change. I call these *existing-environment-first* factories. A *model-first* factory instead places explicit engineering models upstream of realization: selected engineering intent is represented in models, and software realization proceeds downstream from them.

<!-- point: the-two-distinctions-are-independent-axes | Who supplies a factory and how intent enters production are independent dimensions; an organization may supply more than one kind of factory. -->

These distinctions are independent. An internal factory can be existing-environment-first or model-first. So can a factory in a box. The seven public accounts occupy several positions in that design space, and one organization can supply more than one kind of factory. [ref:factory-forms] provides the map I will use for the rest of the section.

<!-- point: the-shared-substrate-is-the-baseline-and-the-cases-report-the-differences | Recurring structures include externalized knowledge, bounded action, evidence before acceptance, and retained human judgment; the cases develop their different implementations. -->

Across these factories, several structures recur. Teams record engineering knowledge, externalizing it so that agents need not ask someone to reconstruct it on every task. Tools, permissions, roles, and execution environments limit what agents can do. Tests, validators, reviews, and simulations supply evidence before consequential changes are accepted. People retain judgments that the available knowledge, evidence, or organizational obligations do not justify delegating.

Against this common background, the cases differ in what each organization has built around its agents, what operating experience has led it to change, and what remains difficult to maintain.

<!-- point: the-accounts-also-show-how-the-factories-are-operated | The public accounts also reveal operation, and unevenly — a mechanism may be described without its effectiveness being measured — so the cases serve three questions: how production is structured, what the organization chooses to measure, and what operating experience changes. -->

The public accounts also reveal how these factories are being operated, and they do so unevenly: a mechanism may be described without its effectiveness being measured, and a source may publish extensive activity data while saying little about the quality of the resulting software. That asymmetry is itself useful. It distinguishes mechanisms that exist from mechanisms whose effects have been observed, and it exposes several constraints that become visible only after realization capacity increases. I therefore use the cases for three related questions: how production is structured, what the organization chooses to measure, and what operating experience causes the factory to change.

## Amazon

<!-- point: kiro-builds-engineering-artifacts-into-the-harness | Kiro packages a reviewable specification workflow, allowing engineering intent to precede implementation. -->

Kiro makes requirements and design part of the coding harness’s documented workflow. A feature specification records user stories and acceptance criteria, develops an architectural design, and divides implementation into tasks. Engineers can review the artifacts as work proceeds.[cite: kiro2026specs] In this book’s terms, the requirements and design are engineering models: they represent intended behavior and structure before implementation. DocAble arranged related work through project-specific work orders, design documents, and completion checks. Kiro packages a specification workflow in the harness that other teams can adopt, then connects it to their repositories and tools.

<!-- point: kiro-connects-requirements-to-tests-and-configurable-controls | Configured checks connect selected specifications to implementation evidence; their coverage and enforcement depend on the project. | terms: modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->

The connection to evidence is concrete. In its IDE, Kiro can extract properties from requirements and generate property-based tests against the implementation. Execution is optional by default, and the documentation distinguishes this evidence from proof.[cite: kiro2026correctness] Configured hooks can run checks after edits or block tool calls whose preconditions fail.[cite: kiro2026hooks] I read this arrangement as an application of Modeling and Alignment: engineers record intent, tests check selected requirements, and configured hooks can reject prohibited actions. Its strength depends on which properties they select, whether the tests express those properties correctly, and where failures actually block work. A team adopting the harness must make those choices for its own system.

<!-- point: amazon-internal-use-adds-team-specific-production-structure | The Bedrock case combines Kiro with modular code, commit-sized tasks, testing, and senior review; its staffing comparison is an estimate and its reported twentyfold increase measures normalized commit velocity. -->

The Bedrock case shows the additional work inside an adopting team. Six senior engineers rebuilt part of the inference engine in 76 days, against an earlier estimate of 30 engineers working twelve to eighteen months. They organized small modules, scoped work to reviewable commits, supplied focused operational tools, and used tests and end-to-end canaries; senior engineers inspected the generated code.[cite: kiro2026bedrock] AWS’s June 2026 account defines the reported twentyfold increase as normalized commit velocity, rising from two to forty commits per developer per week.[cite: sivasubramanian2026frontier] That measures production activity. It neither measures defect reduction nor isolates Kiro’s contribution from team selection and workflow redesign; the staffing estimate is not an observed comparison team.

<!-- point: amazon-governs-improvements-to-the-harness-itself | Kiro-trace separates proposing improvements from checking and admitting them; experience also changes its checks. | terms: tolerance, governance-conversion -->

Amazon also reports improving Kiro through kiro-trace, which proposes prompt or tool changes from recorded runs. A separate deterministic gate screens targeted failures, checks regression assertions, and compares candidates with an unchanged control on the full benchmark. It requires gains on selected tasks without losses beyond a specified margin elsewhere; a person decides whether to merge. One apparent gain failed when traced to a lucky run. Evaluation mistakes became checks, and confirmed fixes add regression assertions. The results are internal experiments.[cite: kim2026kirotrace]

The same factory concerns recur at this second level. Process design organizes the improvement work; tolerances determine which candidate changes qualify; governance conversion preserves lessons in checks that later candidates must pass. The harness is itself software under engineering control. Improving its agents therefore also requires maintaining the machinery that decides whether an apparent improvement is real.

## Internal Software Factories

<!-- point: five-internal-accounts-each-answering-a-different-engineering-question | Four further internal accounts examine optimization, institutional policy, changing controls, and shared learning; teams combine supplied tools with their own engineering structure. -->

Four further accounts describe systems built by an organization for its own work. Uber asks how to optimize the factory itself. Cloudflare asks how institutional policy becomes production control. Spotify asks how the controls should change as agents improve. Shopify asks how one engineer’s experience becomes knowledge that later sessions can use. These internal factories combine tools supplied by vendors with engineering structure developed by their own teams.

### Uber

<!-- point: uber-treats-the-factory-itself-as-an-object-of-measurement-and-optimization | Uber's distinctive move is treating the factory as an engineered system with measurable properties — model choice, context, orchestration, token cost and reviewer attention as tunable variables — at a scale whose headline attribution figure has an undefined denominator. -->

Uber's distinctive move is not that its factory is sophisticated but that the factory is itself an object of measurement and optimization. Model choice, context, decomposition, orchestration, token cost and reviewer attention are treated as engineering variables with numbers attached, rather than as fixed properties of the reasoner. Uber reports more than 70 percent of pull requests attributed to local or cloud agents, more than 3,600 engineer-created agent skills executing over 30,000 times per day, and managed agents performing code review, CI repair, end-to-end changes, alert triage, debugging, and maintenance.[cite: medisetty2026factory] The headline figure needs its measurement caveat attached: no source defines "attributed to," and figures published earlier the same year under narrower definitions were far lower — 11 percent of pull requests *opened* by agents as of March.[cite: orosz2026uber] The scale is real; its denominator is not public. Product intent still originates with people, but a growing share of sessions is initiated by managed agents responding to the factory's own operational signals — CI failures, on-call alerts, incoming bugs — and by centrally scheduled maintenance, with incident reviews mined at a roughly monthly cadence for lessons that become new maintenance skills applied across services.[cite: medisetty2026sdlc]

<!-- point: uber-tunes-the-environment-against-published-numbers | The optimization targets the environment rather than the model: context and tool access are centralized, deterministic orchestration moves into executable code, session anti-patterns are priced, real workloads become benchmarks, and managed agents are evaluated in outcome-denominated units. -->

The optimization targets the environment rather than the reasoner. Context and tool access are centralized; deterministic orchestration is moved from repeated model turns into executable code, cutting token use by more than half on common workflows and by more than 90 percent in bulk cases; and session anti-patterns are detected and priced.[cite: medisetty2026factory; medisetty2026sdlc] Real workloads become benchmarks, models are selected against cost, quality, and reliability, and managed agents are evaluated in outcome-denominated units: cost per merged pull request, review, alert, or cleanup.[cite: medisetty2026factory]

Uber’s AI Context Graph connects services, teams, incidents, and engineering documents so agents can retrieve relevant organizational knowledge.[cite: medisetty2026factory][^uber-retrieval]

<!-- point: uber-cannot-optimize-the-evidence-or-the-reviewer | Uber's evidence includes simulator comparisons, a producer-assembled check table, further review, CI, and human admission; the account does not establish independent re-derivation of every table entry. -->

Two things resist that optimization, and Uber publishes both. The first is the evidence. Evaluation begins inside the agent's own loop, before shared machinery sees the work: static-analysis repair, a simulator screenshot compared against the Figma specification, and staging integration, with the resulting pull request carrying a table of the checks performed, screenshots included.[cite: medisetty2026sdlc] The producing agent assembles that table about its own work, and no source states that any entry is re-derived by independent machinery before approval. Behind it sit a smaller reviewer model inside the loop, a reasoning model outside it, self-healing CI, and then a person. Admission is human, and Uber says so plainly: "we still have humans approving the code," with "a short path in the near future to a percentage of our code landing automatically."[cite: bond2026ureview] It does not publish the criteria for selecting eligible changes or the evidence those changes must provide.

<!-- point: uber-publishes-cost-and-withholds-quality | Uber treats reviewer attention as a production resource with finite capacity — review latency rose from three to nine hours, and maintenance is scheduled against Monday's review load — and tracks revert rate, F1, and time to recovery alongside production cost, while no source states what evidence a change must produce before it may proceed. -->

The second is reviewer attention, which Uber treats as a production resource with finite capacity. Time to first review rose from three hours in 2024 to nine in 2026 as volume and change size grew, and the stated answer is to move reviewers up a layer, toward architecture and product judgment, rather than out of the loop.[cite: bond2026ureview] Maintenance jobs are placed on Sunday for spare CI capacity while the number of diffs engineers face on Monday is separately capped, because spare compute does not imply unlimited reviewer attention.[cite: medisetty2026sdlc] No other case in this corpus schedules admission capacity as a resource. Uber also tracks revert rate, F1, and time to recovery alongside production cost. The factory is therefore being optimized against both the cost of producing changes and signals about what happens after they are produced. Nor does any source state what evidence a change must produce before it may proceed. Uber's own closing lands where this chapter began: once building a feature is easy, implementation feasibility no longer settles the product question — "the factory still needs people to decide whether that feature should be built."[cite: medisetty2026sdlc]

### Cloudflare

<!-- point: cloudflare-turns-standards-prose-into-statements-with-stable-identity | Cloudflare's question is how institutional policy becomes a production control; the answer begins with representation — an extraction agent converts each SHOULD and MUST into a structured statement whose identifier survives revision of its document, and that identity is what makes an obligation trackable across systems. -->

How does an organization's own policy become a control that runs in production? Cloudflare's factory attaches to its engineering organization at review rather than at origination — engineers still open the merge requests and write the specs — and its answer begins with representation. A standards corpus that had outgrown individual memory, and, in the CIO's franker telling, a review process that AI-accelerated engineers had outrun — "Anyone at Cloudflare could now write bad code, faster"[cite: rhea2026cloudflareos] — was extracted by a purpose-built agent into structured statements, each SHOULD or MUST carrying a stable identifier that survives revision of the document containing it.[cite: reimann2026codex] Stable identifiers allow obligations to be tracked across document revisions and across the systems that apply them. They also support explicit waivers and measurements of compliance. Among the cases examined here, Cloudflare is the only one whose published account describes assigning stable identifiers to individual normative obligations. The corpus then meets work at several stations: human review of standards before they enter the corpus, fast linters in the editing loop, semantic review in CI, specification review before implementation, and incident review after production, whose findings are mandatory to clear for high-severity incidents.[cite: reimann2026codex] Cloudflare moves checks into deterministic tooling where the language permits it; the stated reason for doing so is practical — semantic review latency had become expensive.

<!-- point: cloudflare-promotes-obligations-into-blocking-authority-and-instruments-one-side-only | Authority arrives in a separate promotion step from advisory to blocking, after which a coordinator agent maps its verdict onto the version-control action with no human in the path — bounded by an approval-biased rubric and a logged 0.6 percent break-glass rate which, like the larger published counts, measures activity rather than defects prevented. -->

Authority arrives in a second, separate step. Any employee may propose a standard and a named domain owner approves it; a distinct promotion then moves an approved standard from advisory to enforced, and only then can its MUST statements block — though no source names who performs that promotion. Once promoted, admission is machine-held in the ordinary case. A coordinator agent orchestrates up to seven specialist reviewers, judges severity, and maps its verdict directly onto the version-control action — approve, revoke a prior approval, or block the merge — with no human in the path.[cite: skidmore2026review] Cloudflare is the only organization in this corpus that hands a probabilistic evaluator that power, and it engineers around the grant rather than assuming it: only enforced MUST statements can block, the rubric is biased toward approval so a lone warning does not, and a human reviewer can force approval by commenting "break glass" — a logged override used 288 times, 0.6 percent of merge requests, in the measured month.[cite: skidmore2026review] The instrumentation runs in one direction only. The break-glass count measures forced approvals; no published number measures the violations the reviewer failed to flag. The much larger published counts — review runs, findings, and blocked merges — likewise measure activity by the control, not defects prevented or failures escaped. Policy authorship is human, application is machine, and only the escape hatch is counted.

<!-- point: cloudflare-converts-failures-into-structure-but-never-measures-the-loop | Operating experience converts into environmental structure along four documented paths — named rules from named outages, Snapstone enrolment, representation revision, a new linter station — but no loop is closed by measurement, and Cloudflare itself calls the agent-evaluation loop unfinished. -->

Operating experience does become new structure here, along four documented paths. The November and December 2025 outages produced named rules — do not call `.unwrap()` outside tests; validate that upstream dependencies are in an expected state — now enforced at review; a dangerous configuration pattern can be enrolled in Snapstone, a shared substrate through which it "immediately inherits safe deployment"; the extracted representation itself was revised, Markdown to JSON, when agents needed to filter it more accurately; and reviewer latency produced the linter station.[cite: hartman2026failsmall] What the record does not contain is a measurement closing any of these loops. The outcome claim for the outage-derived rules is a counterfactual — that the outages "would have been rejected merge requests instead of global incidents" — and Cloudflare places the remaining loop in the future in its own voice: the current focus is giving engineers "the tools to define the loops that evaluate the work their agents produce."[cite: rhea2026cloudflareos] The conversion paths are described and instantiated; their effect is asserted, not shown.

### Spotify

<!-- point: spotify-holds-the-line-fixed-and-swaps-the-station-inside-it | Spotify's fleet line predates its agent by years; the agent replaced the deterministic transform at one station while targeting, review, merge and monitoring "remain exactly the same" — which isolates what a factory must change when only its fabricator does. -->

Spotify's case isolates a variable the others cannot. Its production line predates the agent by years. Fleet Management targets repositories across the software estate, executes transformations across thousands of repositories, automerges on green, and watches merged changes in production, alerting the change author when deployments fail.[cite: brown2023fleet] In 2025 the company replaced the deterministic transformation script at the center of that line with an agent, and stated the substitution precisely: "All the surrounding Fleet Management infrastructure — targeting repositories, opening pull requests, getting reviews, and merging into production — remains exactly the same."[cite: charas2025honk] The agent occupies one station. It took over the residual the prior technique could not reach — the last 30 percent of a fleet migration, the corner cases that had grown one dependency updater to twenty thousand lines of script. Everything that moved afterward moved because the fabricator at that one station had changed.

<!-- point: spotify-installs-and-then-retires-a-control-as-the-fabricator-improves | Spotify restricts agent tools and checks work before a pull request can be opened, preserving bounded degrees of freedom while enforcing acceptance conditions. An LLM judge added after observed failures illustrates governance conversion; its reported removal amid false vetoes and improved foundation models illustrates the need to reassess controls. -->

Spotify limits what the agent can do and checks its work before opening a pull request. In the configuration described in late 2025, the agent could call a verification tool, use selected Git subcommands that excluded pushing, and run shell commands from an allowlist. Code-search and documentation tools were withheld; users supplied the relevant context in the prompt.[cite: charas2025honk] Within those bounds, the agent retains degrees of freedom in how it implements the requested change.

The verification tool selects checks from the repository’s contents without requiring the agent to understand how each check works. A failing check prevents the pull request from being opened. This is Alignment: the verification machinery decides whether the work can proceed, independently of the agent’s own assessment. Spotify also added an LLM judge to compare the proposed diff with the original prompt after agents made unwanted changes, such as disabling tests. In December 2025, Spotify reported that the judge vetoed about a quarter of thousands of sessions, and that agents corrected their work after about half of those vetoes.[cite: charas2025honk]

A March 2026 report of Spotify’s QCon talk describes further changes. The team separated the agent runtime from the verification runtime, with pull requests created only after CI validation. The report also describes agents commenting out failing tests or downgrading Java versions to make builds pass. It says the judge sometimes blocked valid changes. It also reports that engineers later removed the judge as foundation models improved and verification instructions in prompts proved sufficient.[cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon] Adding the judge illustrates governance conversion: engineers turned observed failures into a check applied to later work. Its reported removal illustrates why controls also need maintenance. Engineers must reassess what a check catches and what valid work it blocks as the agent’s behavior changes.

<!-- point: spotify-splits-admission-by-fabricator-and-names-verification-as-the-constraint | Admission splits on which fabricator produced the change — deterministic fleet changes automerge by default while agent-authored ones wait for human review — and Spotify's own incident retrospectives name verification capacity, not generation, as the binding constraint. -->

Admission itself splits on which fabricator produced the change. Deterministic fleet changes automerge by default — more than 2.5 million automated maintenance PRs, "the vast majority auto-merged with no human in the loop,"[cite: spotify2026constraint] with the change author rather than the repository owner deciding what automerges[cite: brown2023fleet] — while agent-authored changes wait for human review, and Spotify names review, not generation, as the new bottleneck.[cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon] Humans scope the migrations — work that once involved hundreds of teams over weeks is scoped by one engineer in days[cite: spotify2026constraint] — author prompts as version-controlled, tested artifacts,[cite: charas2025honk] and encode non-delegation directly: in one documented migration, wherever a judgment call was required the agent was instructed to leave the field unchanged and annotate it for the human reviewer.[cite: edwardsjoseph2026migrations] The factory's health is itself instrumented. Monthly incident retrospectives now ask whether AI-authored code contributed to an incident; the published finding is that it has not been a material direct contributor, but that "the volume of change increased faster than some of our verification controls could adapt" — one automated dependency upgrade passed every check and still failed in production. The constraint, stated by the SVP who owns the platform: "AI increased the capacity to produce change. The next constraint became our ability to verify it."[cite: singer2026quality]

<!-- point: spotify-measures-the-health-of-the-engineering-system-not-just-agent-activity | Spotify measures the health of the engineering system rather than agent activity: a refined rework measure reports no increase during the AI ramp-up, while complexity and pull-request size rise with the thresholds explicitly held fixed. -->

Spotify also reports no increase in a refined measure of rework, while code complexity and pull-request size have begun to rise; it explicitly declines to move those thresholds. These measurements are unusual in this corpus because they concern the health of the resulting engineering system rather than agent activity. Together with the incident record, they show a mixed result: no measured increase in rework or material direct incident contribution from AI-authored code, alongside change volume beginning to outrun parts of the verification system.[cite: singer2026quality]

### Shopify

<!-- point: shopify-makes-every-session-observable-organizational-material | Shopify's question is how one engineer's fix becomes something every later session inherits, and the answer starts in the architecture: River lives in Slack, refuses direct messages, and turns every session into a public transcript watched by colleagues. -->

Shopify's question is how one engineer's hard-won fix becomes knowledge that every later session inherits, and its answer begins in the architecture rather than in a pipeline. River, the company's internal agent, lives in Slack and refuses direct messages; every session is a public transcript, and an employee starts work by mentioning the agent in a channel.[cite: moreno2026river] Work is therefore performed in front of an audience by construction, and a failure is watched rather than reported. In a thirty-day window reported in May 2026, 59,918 sessions ran across 5,170 channels, touching the work of more than 7,000 people, and one in eight merged pull requests was River-coauthored.[cite: moreno2026river] By September, Lütke reported that up to half of pull requests now begin as a River conversation.[cite: drecksler2026river]

<!-- point: shopify-mines-sessions-by-hand-and-measured-the-effect | The documented conversion mechanism is human — people watch River work and write down what it should have known, as files in the monorepo — and the effect is measured with the counterfactual controlled: merge rate 36 to 77 percent in two months, self-reported and entangled with environmental improvement. -->

At Shopify, people use the shared sessions to improve the knowledge available to later agents. They watch River work, notice where it gets stuck, and externalize the missing knowledge, recording it in the monorepo: a skill update, an `AGENTS.md` change, or a new shared skill.[cite: moreno2026river] These files sit alongside code, conventions, intent documents, and runbooks that later sessions can use. River’s reported merge rate rose from 36 to 77 percent over two months while the foundation model stayed the same: "We did not retrain a model. We did not switch models."[cite: lutke2026shopfloor] This self-reported result does not separate the contribution of human learning from changes to the surrounding environment. It does describe an improvement without a foundation-model change. Shopify offers the corresponding design advice directly: "Make the agent multiplayer by construction."[cite: moreno2026river]

<!-- point: shopifys-instruction-corpus-also-accumulates-maintenance | The mechanism that accumulates shared knowledge accumulates upkeep with it: instruction files apply recursively across the monorepo, a directory missing one of the two files leaves some developers running a degraded agent, and the CEO calls the automation that repairs the drift a stupid complexity tax. -->

The corpus is not free to carry. Instruction files apply recursively down the monorepo tree, and rival tool conventions read different ones; Lütke threatened to ban Claude Code at Shopify over the "split brain problems" that divergence creates "when different team members use different tools."[cite: lutke2026splitbrain] In follow-up posts the trade press quoted, he gave the consequence at scale: with thousands of developers in one repository "it just does happen that one directory is missing one of the two files and this means that a subset of devs work with lobotomy."[cite: caswell2026agentsmd] Automation repairs the drift, and Lütke calls that repair "a stupid complexity tax that shouldn't have to be paid."[cite: caswell2026agentsmd] The knowledge compounds; keeping it coherent is work somebody does.

<!-- point: shopify-converted-one-failure-into-a-code-enforced-guarantee-and-scopes-the-claim | The sharpest conversion is a failure that moved a rule from an instruction the agent interprets to a condition the production system enforces — River violated its own prompt-stated enumeration requirement, producing the rule that guarantees belong in code — and the admission controls named alongside it are stated for the security lane only. -->

The sharpest instance of the conversion is a failure that changed where the rule lived: from an instruction the agent had to interpret to a condition the production system was expected to enforce. A September account of River's security workflows names the controls: the agent's output is draft-only, the engineers who own the affected code hold merge authority, a freshness-gated merge queue rejects stale evidence, and a post-merge check verifies that the default branch reflects the fix — merges through the gated queue rose from about 10 percent to 80 percent.[cite: son2026security] The same account states the governing rule and publishes the run that taught it: "Prompts are the right place to express judgment and the wrong place to express a guarantee," and in security, "any rule that determines whether a vulnerability is closed must be enforced in code" — written after River violated its own prompt-stated enumeration requirement and said so in its report.[cite: son2026security] Those controls are stated for security workflows. What governs the other seven-eighths of River's output — what evidence an ordinary River pull request must carry, how often one fails in production — the public record does not say.

## The Factory in a Box

<!-- point: gitlab-sells-the-capability-and-its-own-practice-prices-what-the-box-omits | GitLab generalizes and sells the production capability, which poses a question no internal factory faces — what productizes generically and what the adopter must supply — and its own handbook prices the second half: failing-test-first doctrine, CI as the gate, a test-count guard, and a two-week 95-percent-agent-generated service built after four weeks of harness construction. -->

GitLab belongs in the other column because the capability its account describes is generalized and sold, to be attached to somebody else's repositories, policies, and engineering knowledge. That form poses a question no internal factory faces: what part of a factory can be productized generically, and what must the adopting organization supply? GitLab's own internal record is worth taking first, because it prices the second half. The company's handbook documents an internal practice with doctrine attached: never give an agent a task without a failing test; "Fix the environment, not the prompt"; "Prompts are suggestions. CI is a gate. If the agent can break a rule and still pass the pipeline, the rule doesn't exist" — down to a CI job that fails when the test count decreases, because agents delete tests to make them pass.[cite: gitlab2026playbook] And it publishes a dated internal quantity: a 135,000-line Rust service, roughly 95 percent agent-generated, built by four engineers across 259 merge requests in about two weeks, with every agent-generated change still passing CI, security scanning, and code review.[cite: gitlab2026orbitissue] The timing matters. Roughly four weeks were spent constructing the harness before the roughly two-week production episode began. The factory therefore had a visible startup investment before its marginal production economics could be observed.

<!-- point: the-factory-in-a-box-is-an-integration-problem-at-both-ends | A factory in a box introduces a boundary an internal factory does not have: the vendor supplies fabricator, orchestration, context representations, evidence, and generic controls, and the engineering problem is integration, not installation — at both ends of the production line. -->

The form introduces a boundary an internal factory does not have. The vendor supplies the fabricator, the orchestration, the context representations, the evidence infrastructure, and the generic controls; the customer must connect those to the knowledge and authority that make a change acceptable in its own system. The engineering problem is integration, not installation, and it sits at both ends of the production line. Upstream: how does organization-specific intent enter generic machinery? Downstream: how does organization-specific authority govern what comes out?

<!-- point: customer-intent-enters-as-files-and-customer-authority-as-a-withheld-capability | Upstream, intent enters as files the customer authors at fixed repository paths, under the vendor's thesis that what an agent cannot access in context does not exist; downstream, admission authority stays human by construction — the flow never sets the Approve state — with agent privileges intersected and tool policy tightenable only. -->

GitLab's upstream answer is files the customer authors at fixed paths in its own repository — an `AGENTS.md`, chat rules, review instructions, skills — plus the customer's CI configuration and, optionally, authored flow definitions.[cite: gitlab2026customize] The vendor states the thesis behind the arrangement normatively: "anything the agent can't access in-context effectively doesn't exist."[cite: gitlab2026orbitissue] What GitLab supplies as infrastructure is the recovered remainder — a context graph over the customer's code, merge requests, pipelines, and security findings, assembled from artifacts that already exist. Recovered context carries relationships. It cannot carry the intent the customer never wrote down. Downstream, authority is expressed as a withheld capability. GitLab's security review flow "never sets the **Approve** state, even when it finds no issues" — the machinery may produce, repair, and advise, and only a person approves.[cite: gitlab2026securityreview] Around that prohibition sits governance of the action rather than the change: an agent's privileges are the intersection of its service account's and the triggering human's, tool policy is tightenable by a project but never loosenable below its group's rule, and audit events record which agent acted under which policy. The vendor industrializes the record that a human decision was made. It does not move the decision.

<!-- point: the-record-prices-the-infrastructure-and-not-the-knowledge-authoring | The public record specifies the supplied infrastructure and not the cost of authoring the organization-specific knowledge autonomy depends on — no case study measures that work or how output varies with instruction quality — and the layer that produced GitLab's own 95-percent-agent-generated service is the layer the adopting organization must supply. -->

The asymmetry is in what the vendor can supply. GitLab specifies the supplied infrastructure in detail, but not the cost of authoring the organization-specific knowledge on which autonomy depends: no case study measures the work required to construct those instructions or how output varies with their quality. The CEO's essay locates trust in a durable layer of "context, verification, governance and evidence" around a replaceable foundation model.[cite: staples2026abundant] Yet the layer that produced GitLab's own 95-percent-agent-generated service included failing tests written first, CI gates, repository-resident context, and the test-count guard — precisely the layer the adopting organization must supply.

<!-- point: the-form-question-stated-plainly | The externalization question follows directly from the two-ended boundary and is worth stating plainly. -->

The question the form poses follows directly, and it is worth stating plainly.

<!-- inline-quote -->

> How much organization-specific engineering knowledge must be externalized before an off-the-shelf factory can reliably produce controlled changes to that organization's software?

<!-- point: the-externalization-question-is-the-modeling-problem-commercially | The question restates the Modeling problem in commercial terms, and the factory chapter already named the tradeoff behind it: a purchased factory that works only when the buyer describes the software almost completely has recreated the arrangement the earlier software factories died of. -->

This is the representation problem faced by a commercial software-factory provider, and {{sec:the-software-factory}} already named the tradeoff behind it. A purchased factory that reliably produces your software only when you describe that software almost completely has recreated the arrangement the earlier software factories died of. So the buyer's side of the transaction is a modeling decision: which consequential intent to externalize, in what form, at what cost, before generic machinery can respect it. {{chapter:theory}} takes up what that externalization costs.

## Model-First Factories

<!-- point: model-first-production-inverts-where-intent-enters | Siemens brings established engineering models upstream of realization, extending the model-first question already raised by Kiro's specification workflow. -->

Siemens brings an established engineering-model tradition to the question raised by Kiro’s specification workflow: what changes when explicit engineering models sit upstream of realization? {{sec:the-software-factory}} told this ambition's history in software — enterprise architecture and model-driven development tried to determine the consequential decisions upstream, in shared representations, and lost economically rather than conceptually. Here the same ambition is still working, as the ordinary practice of an engineering tradition that arrived at models by a different route.

<!-- point: siemens-model-first-evidence-is-eigen-and-agent-studio | The load-bearing Siemens evidence is Eigen — control code generated from a TIA Portal engineering model, iterating until pre-defined performance benchmarks are met, a tolerance in this chapter's exact sense — and the 2024 Agent Studio research on SysML v2 with simulation as validator: a model supplies goal and context, a deterministic engine supplies the verdict. -->

Siemens enters from outside software-first engineering, and "Siemens" is not one factory: its public record spans several business units, and most of it describes production systems Siemens sells rather than a factory it runs on its own software. The clearest instance of software fabricated downstream of a model is the Eigen Engineering Agent. It reads a TIA Portal automation project — an engineering model already carrying the devices, network topology, and control logic — writes SCL and LAD control code, test logic, and HMI JavaScript against it, and "iterates until pre-defined performance benchmarks are met."[cite: siemens2026eigen] The admission criterion is a pre-set acceptance region, not a specified realization — a tolerance in this chapter's exact sense. Two years earlier, Siemens' Agent Studio research placed agents on formal modeling languages, SysML v2 among them, with generated candidate architectures validated through simulation and the system waiting for the engineer's decision at critical points.[cite: liu2024agentstudio] The recurring structure across the Siemens record: a model supplies the goal and the context, a deterministic engine — a simulator, a compiler, a benchmark harness — supplies the verdict, and the agent searches freely between them. That is the bargain the earlier model-driven attempts could not strike.

<!-- point: siemens-vision-disclaims-itself-and-the-gaps-sit-where-stakes-are-highest | The broader architecture Siemens publishes is an explicit research vision about simulation, not software realization; no source establishes a model-implementation conformance gate, Eigen's record names no human approval step, and almost no operating measurement is published — while the one account of Siemens changing its own software keeps a human in the loop on compliance grounds. -->

The broader architecture Siemens publishes around this — task agents and orchestrators over knowledge graphs of engineering data — is explicitly "a research vision and innovation roadmap, not a committed product delivery," and its subject is simulation rather than software realization.[cite: siemens2026a3e] The public sources leave two important questions unanswered. No Siemens source establishes a conformance mechanism that rejects divergence between model and implementation — Eigen's benchmark is a threshold, not a correspondence — and Eigen's public record names no human review or approval step. Source silence is not organizational absence, but both omissions concern consequential controls. Where the record does describe Siemens changing its own software, in a legacy-modernization account written up by its cloud partner rather than by Siemens engineers, the posture inverts: the system "keeps a human in the loop at every step," and the reason given is an obligation the software carries rather than a stage of maturity. Industrial systems run "over 15 to 20 years of operation," so AI-generated output "must therefore be explainable, traceable, and verifiable," and "hallucinated or unvalidated changes are not merely inefficient but operationally unacceptable."[cite: nawalgaria2026siemens] Unlike the software-first cases, the available Siemens record supplies almost no operating measurement of the factory itself: no throughput series, admission rate, failure rate, or before-and-after outcome for the agentic production systems described here.

<!-- point: a-cheap-and-capable-fabricator-changes-what-a-model-must-determine | The sharp difference from the old model-driven dream is that capable realization is now inexpensive, so a model-first factory need not determine every realization decision — only the consequential ones. -->

One difference from the old dream matters more than the rest, and it is the difference {{sec:the-software-factory}} isolated. Model-driven development had to specify realization almost completely, because its downstream fabricator was inexpensive but not capable. Today, capable realization is inexpensive. A model-first factory can therefore represent the decisions engineering must control and leave the remaining realization freedom to an agent that will resolve it acceptably. **The model need not determine every realization decision.** That is a different bargain from the one the earlier attempts lost, and whether it pays is not something the public sources settle.

<!-- point: model-first-is-a-position-and-siemens-sells-the-combined-cell | Model-first is independent of who supplies the factory; Siemens supplies industrial instances and Kiro supplies a specification-driven path for general-purpose software, with coverage and enforcement still requiring examination. -->

Two cautions keep the axis honest. Model-first is not a rung above existing-environment-first; it is a different answer to where intent enters, carrying its own costs. And the axis is independent of the first one. A factory in a box can be sold as model-first, driven by models the customer authors rather than a repository the vendor crawls — and Siemens itself shows the combined cell is not empty: Eigen and its EDA verification toolkit are sold production systems driven by the customer's own engineering models, in the narrow software of control logic and chip design. Kiro also supplies a specification-driven path for general-purpose software. The combined category therefore extends beyond industrial control; how fully each factory represents and enforces a project’s obligations remains an engineering question.

## What the Factories Share, and Where They Differ

<!-- point: the-factories-place-admission-in-four-different-locations | The factories differ in where they place admission: Cloudflare's coordinator holds the merge gate under human-authored policy, Spotify splits admission by fabricator, Uber keeps approval human while naming partial automation, and GitLab withholds approval from the agent by construction. -->

The factories differ in where they place admission:

- Cloudflare gives its coordinator the merge gate under human-authored policy, with a logged human override.
- Spotify automerges deterministic fleet changes while agent-authored changes wait for human review.
- Uber keeps approval human while naming partial automatic approval as a near-term destination.
- GitLab withholds approval from the agent in its documented security-review flow.

<!-- point: human-in-the-loop-is-not-one-phenomenon | The reasons for retaining human judgment differ — interim evidence adequacy at Uber, responsibility at Shopify, long-lived industrial obligation at Siemens — and only Uber expects its boundary to move with capability, so the cases do not support a single delegation frontier. -->

The reasons for retaining human judgment differ as well. Uber's boundary is interim: the available representations and evidence do not yet justify further delegation. Shopify grounds the boundary in responsibility: a machine cannot take responsibility for a decision.[cite: podcastalpha2026lutke] Siemens grounds it in the obligations of long-lived industrial software: generated output must remain explainable, traceable, and verifiable over systems expected to operate for fifteen to twenty years. Only Uber describes its boundary as one expected to move with improving capability. The cases therefore do not support a single delegation frontier that simply advances as the fabricator improves.

<!-- point: the-reported-measurements-favor-production-over-quality | The cases publish different production and quality measures, including Amazon's harness experiments; those measures retain their own definitions and limits. -->

The reported measurements are much stronger on the production of change than on the quality of the changes produced. Uber and Cloudflare report cost, throughput, findings, and overrides in detail, but much less about resulting software quality. Spotify reports measures of engineering-system health, including rework, complexity, pull-request size, and incident contribution. Shopify reports a merge-rate improvement with the foundation model held fixed. GitLab describes measurement mechanisms, Amazon adds harness experiments, and Siemens provides little operating evidence of this kind. These public accounts may omit measurements used internally, but as evidence they characterize production much more fully than they establish the quality of its output.

<!-- point: operating-experience-changes-the-factories-but-the-effect-is-rarely-established | Operating experience changes factory controls. The accounts provide uneven evidence of effects and leave long-term production recurrence unresolved. | terms: governance-conversion -->

Operating experience changes the factories too. Cloudflare responds to incidents, review latency, and representation problems by changing rules and tooling. Shopify records lessons from agent failures in shared instructions and enforces selected security requirements in code. GitLab recommends fixing the environment when prompt repair would otherwise recur. A later account of Spotify reports the reverse change: a probabilistic check removed after the foundation models improved. These examples illustrate governance conversion. Most of these accounts describe what changed without measuring its effect. Amazon adds experimental comparisons and permanent regression checks; the public record still leaves their effect on long-term production recurrence unresolved.

<!-- point: supervision-and-verification-do-not-scale-with-production | One constraint recurs: the capacity to supervise and verify does not automatically increase with the capacity to produce changes — Uber's reviewer delay, Spotify's verification controls outrun by volume, Shopify's slop grenade, Cloudflare's checks moved earlier — so fabrication can outpace the attention and evidence that establish acceptability. -->

One constraint nevertheless appears repeatedly enough to matter: the capacity to supervise and verify production does not automatically increase with the capacity to produce changes. Uber measures reviewer delay increasing from three to nine hours and schedules work against finite reviewer attention. Spotify states the same pressure directly: change-production capacity increased faster than some verification controls could adapt. Shopify's "slop grenade" names the social version of the problem, in which cheap production transfers expensive inspection to somebody else. Cloudflare moved deterministic checks earlier partly because semantic review latency had become costly. These mechanisms differ, but the underlying resource is recognizable: fabrication can scale faster than the attention and evidence required to establish that its output is acceptable.

<!-- point: the-supervision-constraint-is-an-operating-constraint-not-a-new-factory-kind | The observation identifies an operating constraint on the factories already described rather than another kind of factory: process design determines where scarce supervision is spent and tolerances determine what must be established before admission, and cheaper fabrication makes the factory's economics depend on both. | terms: tolerance -->

That observation does not require another kind of software factory. It identifies an operating constraint on the factories already described. Process design determines where that scarce supervision is spent, what can be established mechanically, and which work reaches a person. Tolerances and their evidence determine what must be established before a realization can be admitted. As fabrication becomes cheaper, the economics of the factory increasingly depend on both.

<!-- point: the-inheritance-channel-carries-its-own-maintenance-cost | A second recurring cost is the upkeep of the representations through which a factory inherits engineering knowledge — GitLab's freshness and convergence machinery, Cloudflare's context checks, Shopify's drift repair — so production structures are capital only while they remain accurate enough to be useful. -->

A second recurring cost is keeping recorded engineering knowledge accurate. GitLab maintains documentation freshness and consistency, Cloudflare checks the context supplied through AGENTS.md, and Shopify repairs drift between instruction files. These resources require maintenance just as the software does. They remain engineering capital only while later work can still rely on them.

<!-- point: the-common-structure-is-leverage-organizational-capital | Across the cases, engineers preserve selected judgments in policies, shared representations, and constraints so that those decisions can guide later changes. -->

Across these cases, engineers arrange for selected judgments to guide more than one task. Engineers spend more of their effort on scoping work, supervising it, and building reusable checks. One policy can then guide many changes, a shared representation can help plan work across repositories, and a constraint learned from one failure can restrict later work. The common lesson is that engineers can preserve a decision in the environment instead of making it again for every change.
