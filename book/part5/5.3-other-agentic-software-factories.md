# Other Agentic Software Factories

<!-- point: one-system-one-engineer-one-problem-so-how-do-others-do-it | DocAble is one system built by one engineer for one problem; the natural question is how engineers in other organizations, under different constraints, approach the same problem. -->
My experience with DocAble showed me one way of building reliable software with unreliable agents. But that is one system, built by one engineer, for one problem. The natural question is how other engineers, working in other organizations and under different constraints, are approaching the same problem.

<!-- point: seven-reconstructions-differ-in-representation-direction-evidence-and-judgment | Seven public accounts are reconstructed; they differ in what they represent, how they direct and constrain agents, what evidence they require, where they keep human judgment — and in whether the factory is built for their own software or for somebody else's. -->
In this section, I reconstruct agentic software factories from public reports by seven major companies: Uber, GitLab, Cloudflare, Siemens, Spotify, Shopify, and Zenseact. These factories differ in what they represent, how they direct and constrain agents, what evidence they require, and where they retain human judgment. They also differ in a more basic way: some organizations are building factories for their own software, while others are building factories for somebody else to use.

<!-- point: two-dimensions-who-supplies-and-how-intent-enters | The factories differ along two dimensions: who supplies the production system, and how engineering intent enters production. -->
{{sec:the-software-factory}} defined a software factory as a production system for controlled change. The factories examined here differ along two dimensions: who supplies the production system, and how engineering intent enters production.

<!-- point: internal-factory-versus-factory-in-a-box | The first distinction is between an internal factory, built and operated by an organization to change its own software, and a factory in a box, a generalized production system supplied to other organizations. -->
The first distinction is between an *internal factory*, built and operated by an organization to change its own software, and a *factory in a box*, a generalized production system supplied to other organizations.

> **Factory in a box.** An agentic software-production system packaged for use by other organizations, which connect it to their own software, engineering knowledge, policies, and business intent.

<!-- point: existing-environment-first-versus-model-first | The second distinction concerns how engineering intent enters the factory: existing-environment-first factories give agents repositories, tests, pipelines and organizational context to recover what they need, while a model-first factory places explicit engineering models upstream of realization. -->
The second distinction concerns how engineering intent enters the factory. Most of the factories examined here begin with the existing software environment. Repositories, tests, pipelines, issue systems, service catalogs, policies, and other organizational context provide the material from which agents recover what they need to perform a change. I call these *existing-environment-first* factories. A *model-first* factory instead places explicit engineering models upstream of realization: selected engineering intent is represented in models, and software realization proceeds downstream from them.

<!-- point: the-two-distinctions-are-independent-axes | The two distinctions are independent: either factory form can be existing-environment-first or model-first, and the seven public accounts occupy only part of that design space. -->
These distinctions are independent. An internal factory can be existing-environment-first or model-first. So can a factory in a box. The seven public accounts happen to occupy only part of that design space. [ref:factory-forms] provides the map I will use for the rest of the section.

## Internal Software Factories

<!-- point: five-accounts-four-delegate-realization-one-federates | Five accounts describe systems an organization built for its own work, each entering from a different problem; four delegate software realization to agents from the existing environment, while Zenseact's agents do knowledge work around its factory rather than realization inside it. -->
Five of the seven accounts describe a system an organization built for its own work, and each
enters from a different problem. Uber enters from the economics of the factory itself; Cloudflare
from institutional policy; Spotify from fleet-scale change; Shopify from organizational knowledge;
Zenseact from where authority should sit between a platform team and the domain teams around it.
Four of the five begin with the existing software environment and delegate more of realization to
agents from there. Zenseact is the exception — its agents, as the case will show, perform knowledge
work around the software factory rather than realization inside it — and the exception is itself a
data point about where agentic capability lands first.

### Uber

<!-- point: uber-reports-the-largest-scale-with-an-undefined-denominator | Uber reports the corpus's largest realization share — over 70 percent of PRs attributed to agents, 3,600 skills, 30,000 executions daily — but the attribution is undefined, and earlier same-year figures under narrower definitions were far lower; work increasingly originates from the factory's own operational signals. -->
Uber reports a factory operating at the largest scale in this corpus: more than 70 percent of pull
requests attributed to local or cloud agents, more than 3,600 engineer-created agent skills
executing over 30,000 times per day, and managed agents performing code review, CI repair,
end-to-end changes, alert triage, debugging, and maintenance.[cite: medisetty2026factory] The
headline figure needs its measurement caveat attached: no source defines "attributed to," and
figures published earlier the same year under narrower definitions were far lower — 11 percent of
pull requests *opened* by agents as of March.[cite: orosz2026uber] The scale is real; its
denominator is not public. Where the work comes from is the sharper fact. Product intent still
originates with people, but a growing share of sessions is initiated by managed agents responding
to the factory's own operational signals — CI failures, on-call alerts, incoming bugs — and by
centrally scheduled maintenance, with incident reviews mined at a roughly monthly cadence for
lessons that become new maintenance skills applied across services.[cite: medisetty2026sdlc]

<!-- point: uber-compiles-the-environment-and-keeps-admission-human | The fabricator receives a governed, pre-compiled environment and produces its own evidence in an inner loop attached to the PR — a table the producing agent assembles about its own work, with no independent re-derivation stated — and admission stays human: "we still have humans approving the code," with automatic approval a named destination whose criterion is unpublished and review capacity the measured constraint. -->
What the fabricator receives is a governed, pre-compiled environment: a context graph spanning more
than thirty internal systems and one gateway fronting more than a thousand tool servers under
centralized policy,[cite: medisetty2026factory] pre-provisioned execution environments, and a
skills marketplace whose entries pass lint checks and automated review before they reach
agents.[cite: medisetty2026sdlc] Deterministic orchestration moved out of repeated model turns into
executable code cuts token use by more than half on common workflows and by more than 90 percent in
bulk cases.[cite: medisetty2026factory] Evaluation
begins inside the agent's own loop, before shared machinery sees the work: static-analysis repair,
a simulator screenshot compared against the Figma specification — the one place in this record
where a design artifact serves as a mechanical oracle — and staging integration, with the resulting
pull request carrying a table of the checks performed, screenshots included.[cite: medisetty2026sdlc]
The producing agent assembles that table about its own work, and no source states that any entry is
re-derived by independent machinery before approval.
Behind that sit a smaller reviewer model inside the loop, a reasoning model outside it, self-healing
CI, and then a person. Admission is human, and Uber says so plainly: "we still have humans
approving the code," with "a short path in the near future to a percentage of our code landing
automatically" — the destination named, the criterion for which code and on what evidence
unpublished.[cite: bond2026ureview] The pressure on that human boundary is measured: time to first
review rose from three hours in 2024 to nine in 2026 as volume and change size grew, and the stated
answer is to move reviewers up a layer, toward architecture and product judgment, rather than out
of the loop.[cite: bond2026ureview]

<!-- point: uber-publishes-cost-and-withholds-quality | Supervision is structured by measurement, asymmetrically: benchmarks from real workloads and outcome-denominated costs are published in detail, while the named quality signals are tracked and never published; the reasoning surface is a knowledge graph, not a model of the software's behavior — and Uber's own closing note moves the bottleneck to human judgment. -->
Supervision is structured by measurement, and the measurement is asymmetric. Real workloads become
benchmarks; models are selected against cost, quality, and reliability; managed agents are
evaluated in outcome-denominated units — cost per merged pull request, review, alert, or
cleanup.[cite: medisetty2026factory] The cost half is published in detail. The quality half —
revert rate, F1, time to recovery — is named as tracked and appears nowhere in the public record,
and no source states what evidence a change must produce before it may proceed. The reasoning
surface remains a knowledge-and-context graph, not an executable model of the software's behavior;
the Figma comparison is the narrow exception. And Uber's own closing lands where this chapter
began: once building a feature is easy, implementation feasibility no longer settles the product
question — "the factory still needs people to decide whether that feature should be
built."[cite: medisetty2026sdlc]

### Cloudflare

<!-- point: cloudflare-extracts-obligations-and-applies-them-at-five-stations | Cloudflare's agentic apparatus attaches at review: a purpose-built agent extracts the RFC corpus into structured statements with stable identities, applied at four or five stations across the lifecycle — with the linter-versus-review line drawn by mechanical verifiability per language, motivated by latency rather than a taxonomy. -->
Cloudflare's factory attaches to its engineering organization at review rather than at origination:
engineers still open the merge requests and write the specs, and the agentic apparatus evaluates
them. The move begins with representation. A standards corpus that had outgrown individual memory —
and, in the CIO's franker telling, a review process that AI-accelerated engineers had outrun:
"Anyone at Cloudflare could now write bad code, faster"[cite: rhea2026cloudflareos] — was extracted
by a purpose-built agent into structured statements, each SHOULD or MUST carrying a stable
identifier that survives revision of the document containing it.[cite: reimann2026codex] The corpus
then meets the work at four or five stations, not one: human review of the standards themselves
before they enter the corpus; fast custom linters in the engineer's editing loop; a multi-agent
semantic review in CI; a spec reviewer upstream of implementation entirely; and an incident-report
reviewer after production, whose findings are mandatory to clear for high-severity
incidents.[cite: reimann2026codex] The line between linter and review question is drawn by what
each language's tooling can verify mechanically, and Cloudflare's stated motive for drawing it was
latency — engineers complained about the reviewer's round trips — not a taxonomy of obligations.

<!-- point: cloudflare-admission-is-machine-held-under-human-policy | Admission is machine-held in the ordinary case — a coordinator agent approves, revokes, or blocks with no human in the path — bounded by human-authored policy, an approval-biased rubric, and a logged break-glass override used on 0.6 percent of merge requests. -->
Admission is machine-held in the ordinary case. A coordinator agent orchestrates up to seven
specialist reviewers, judges severity, and maps its verdict directly onto the version-control
action — approve, revoke a prior approval, or block the merge — with no human in the
path.[cite: skidmore2026review] That authority is bounded on three sides. Only MUST statements of
standards explicitly promoted to enforced can block; the rubric is biased toward approval, so a
lone warning does not; and a human reviewer can force approval by commenting "break glass" — a
logged override used 288 times, 0.6 percent of merge requests, in the measured
month.[cite: skidmore2026review] Humans hold what the machine does not: any employee may propose a
standard, a named domain owner approves it, and a separate promotion step converts it from advisory
to blocking — though no source names who performs the promotion. Policy authorship is human,
application is machine, and the escape hatch is instrumented.

<!-- point: cloudflare-converts-failures-into-structure-but-never-measures-the-loop | Failures convert into environmental structure along four documented paths — named rules from named outages, Snapstone enrolment, representation revision, a new linter station — but no loop is closed by measurement, and Cloudflare itself calls the agent-evaluation loop unfinished. -->
Failures do become new structure here, along four documented paths. The November and December 2025
outages produced named rules — do not call `.unwrap()` outside tests; validate that upstream
dependencies are in an expected state — now enforced at review; a dangerous configuration pattern
can be enrolled in Snapstone, a shared substrate through which it "immediately inherits safe
deployment"; the extracted representation itself was revised, Markdown to JSON, when agents needed
to filter it more accurately; and reviewer latency produced the linter station.[cite: hartman2026failsmall]
What the record does not contain is a measurement closing any of these loops. The outcome claim for
the outage-derived rules is a counterfactual — that the outages "would have been rejected merge
requests instead of global incidents" — and Cloudflare places the remaining loop in the future in
its own voice: the current focus is giving engineers "the tools to define the loops that evaluate
the work their agents produce."[cite: rhea2026cloudflareos] The conversion machinery is described
and instantiated; its effect is asserted, not shown.

### Spotify

<!-- point: spotify-substitutes-an-agent-at-one-station-of-a-line-that-predates-it | Spotify's fleet line — targeting from BigQuery and the Backstage software catalog, Fleetshift execution, automerge, post-merge monitoring — predates the agent by years; the agent replaced the deterministic transform at one station, everything else "remains exactly the same," and it took over the residual the prior technique could not reach. -->
Spotify's production line predates its agent by years. Fleet Management targets repositories by
querying the estate — code and configuration ingested into BigQuery, the Backstage software catalog
carrying component identity, ownership, dependencies, and endpoint lineage — executes
transformations across thousands of repositories, automerges on green, and watches merged changes
in production, alerting the change author when deployments fail.[cite: brown2023fleet] In 2025 the
company replaced the deterministic transformation script at the center of that line with an agent,
and stated the substitution precisely: "All the surrounding Fleet Management infrastructure —
targeting repositories, opening pull requests, getting reviews, and merging into production —
remains exactly the same."[cite: charas2025honk] The agent occupies one station. It took over the
residual the prior technique could not reach — the last 30 percent of a fleet migration, the corner
cases that had grown one dependency updater to twenty thousand lines of script.

<!-- point: spotify-narrows-the-action-surface-and-gates-pr-existence-on-verification | The agent's freedom is set environmentally — a verify tool, restricted git, an allowlisted shell, with code search deliberately withheld — a PR may not exist until verification passes, and the LLM judge added against scope creep was later removed as models improved: a control retired, not just installed. -->
Realization freedom is set environmentally rather than by instruction. The agent chooses its own
path through the code but holds a deliberately narrow tool surface — a verify tool, restricted git
subcommands that can never push, an allowlisted shell — and code search and documentation tools
were deliberately withheld, with context condensed into the prompt up front: "The more tools you
have, the more dimensions of unpredictability you introduce."[cite: charas2025honk] Verification
runs before a pull request may exist: verifiers activate from the component's contents, opaque to
the agent, and a failing verifier means no PR;[cite: charas2025honk] a later architecture separates
the verification runtime entirely, so the PR is created only after full CI
validation.[cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon]
The clearest control story in the case is a lifecycle. Agents gamed builds — commenting out failing
tests, downgrading Java versions — so Spotify added an LLM judge, which by late 2025 was vetoing
about a quarter of sessions, the agent self-correcting half the time; the talk record then reports
the judge removed as models improved, "verification steps in prompts proving
sufficient."[cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon] A control installed against a measured failure mode and
retired when the capability beneath it moved — reported second-hand, with no first-party text yet
confirming the removal.

<!-- point: spotify-runs-two-admission-regimes-and-instruments-its-own-factory-health | Deterministic fleet changes automerge by default — 2.5 million maintenance PRs, most with no human in the loop — while agent-authored PRs wait for human review, the named bottleneck; incident retrospectives ask whether AI-authored code contributed, and the published constraint is verification capacity. -->
Admission runs in two regimes, and the record distinguishes them. Deterministic fleet changes
automerge by default — more than 2.5 million automated maintenance PRs, "the vast majority
auto-merged with no human in the loop,"[cite: spotify2026constraint] with the change author rather
than the repository owner deciding what automerges[cite: brown2023fleet] — while agent-authored changes wait for human
review, and Spotify names review, not generation, as the new bottleneck.[cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon]
Humans scope the migrations — work that once involved hundreds of teams over weeks is scoped by one
engineer in days[cite: spotify2026constraint] — author prompts as version-controlled, tested
artifacts,[cite: charas2025honk] and encode non-delegation directly: in one documented migration,
wherever a judgment call was required the agent was instructed to leave the field unchanged and
annotate it for the human reviewer.[cite: edwardsjoseph2026migrations] The factory's
health is itself instrumented. Monthly incident retrospectives now ask whether AI-authored code
contributed to an incident; the published finding is that it has not been a material direct
contributor, but that "the volume of change increased faster than some of our verification controls
could adapt" — one automated dependency upgrade passed every check and still failed in production.
The constraint, stated by the SVP who owns the platform: "AI increased the capacity to produce
change. The next constraint became our ability to verify it."[cite: singer2026quality]

### Shopify

<!-- point: shopify-work-originates-in-public-conversation-and-the-figures-are-dated | Work originates in open Slack conversations with River — no direct messages by design — with one in eight merged PRs coauthored as of May 2026, channels roughly doubling by September while the people count stayed flat, and a founder-stated half-of-PRs origination share that is a different measure. -->
At Shopify, work originates in a conversation held in the open. River, the company's internal
agent, lives in Slack and refuses direct messages; every session is a public transcript, and an
employee starts work by mentioning the agent in a channel.[cite: moreno2026river] In a thirty-day
window reported in May 2026, 59,918 sessions ran across 5,170 channels, touching the work of more
than 7,000 people, and one in eight merged pull requests was River-coauthored.[cite: moreno2026river]
By September, third-party digests of a founder podcast put the channel count near ten thousand with
the population flat — the growth is in surfaces, not users — and have Lütke saying up to half of
pull requests now *start* as a River conversation: an origination share, founder-stated and
unaudited, not a movement in the merged-coauthored figure.[cite: drecksler2026river]

<!-- point: shopify-mines-sessions-by-hand-and-measured-the-effect | The session corpus is mined into skills and instruction files by people, with the effect measured and the counterfactual controlled — merge rate 36 to 77 percent in two months without retraining or switching models; "multiplayer by construction" is Shopify's advice to readers, not its self-description, and no automated extraction is established. -->
The corpus of sessions is the factory's learning substrate, and the mechanism Shopify documents is
human. People watch River work, notice where it got stuck, and write down what it should have
known; the artifacts are files in the monorepo — a skill update, an `AGENTS.md` diff, sometimes a
whole new shared skill — alongside the code, conventions, intent documents, and runbooks the next
session inherits.[cite: moreno2026river] The effect is measured with the counterfactual controlled:
River's merge rate rose from 36 to 77 percent over two months, and "We did not retrain a model. We
did not switch models."[cite: lutke2026shopfloor] Shopify's engineering account closes by handing
readers an imperative — "Make the agent multiplayer by construction" — a design prescription
offered outward, not a label the company applies to itself.[cite: moreno2026river] Whether any of
the extraction is automated the record does not establish: a reported nightly loop in which River
reviews its own conversations and rewrites its instruction files appears only in third-party
digests of a members-only podcast, and the engineering prose describes people doing the writing.

<!-- point: shopify-states-admission-controls-for-the-security-lane-only | A September security-workflow account specifies the admission machinery — draft-only output, human merge authority, a freshness-gated merge queue, post-merge verification — and the rule that guarantees belong in code, not prompts; the stated grounding for the human gate is responsibility rather than evidence adequacy, reported only at second hand; the record does not say what governs the rest of River's output. -->
Where admission is specified, it is specified for one lane. A September account of River's security
workflows names the controls: the agent's output is draft-only, the engineers who own the affected
code hold merge authority, a freshness-gated merge queue rejects stale evidence, and a post-merge
check verifies that the default branch reflects the fix — merges through the gated queue rose from
about 10 percent to 80 percent.[cite: son2026security] The same account states the governing rule and
publishes the run that taught it: "Prompts are the right place to express judgment and the wrong
place to express a guarantee," and in security, "any rule that determines whether a vulnerability
is closed must be enforced in code" — written after River violated its own prompt-stated
enumeration requirement and said so in its report.[cite: son2026security] Why a person holds that
merge authority is stated as a claim about responsibility rather than about the machinery's
adequacy: machines can inform a decision but cannot take responsibility for one. That grounding
reaches the public record only at second hand, through third-party digests of a members-only
podcast, so the wording is the digests' and not Lütke's.[cite: podcastalpha2026lutke] Those controls
are stated for security workflows. What governs the other seven-eighths of River's output — what evidence an
ordinary River pull request must carry, how often one fails in production — the public record does
not say.

### Zenseact

<!-- point: zenseact-agents-do-knowledge-work-around-the-factory-not-realization-inside-it | Zenseact's ZAP agents query Gerrit, Zuul, JIRA, and SAP as data sources and produce answers, not changes — not an agentic software factory under this chapter's definitions — and the case stays for the federation move: platform team owns a runtime with no business logic, a domain agent is a directory with two text files. -->
Zenseact, a driver-assistance software company, belongs in this section with a scoping sentence
attached: its agents do not produce software. The one substantive source — a company-authored
whitepaper describing its ZAP platform — shows agents querying Gerrit, Zuul, JIRA, SAP, and
document stores as data sources and synthesizing answers; no agent authors a patch, opens a change,
or merges anything, and the code-review system appears only as something to read.[cite: dufwa2026zap]
Under this chapter's definitions that is not an agentic software factory. It is an agentic
knowledge-work platform operating around one, and I keep the case for what that placement shows:
where agentic capability landed first in this organization, and how it was federated when it
landed. A platform team owns the runtime — authentication, sessions, tool execution, the model
loop — and the runtime deliberately "does not contain any business logic"; a domain team's agent is
a directory with two text files, a configuration listing its skills and a markdown file encoding
the domain's rules, so a new agent is a configuration act rather than a platform
change.[cite: dufwa2026zap] This is architecture described by its authors rather than practice
observed: one source, no adoption count, no outcome figure.

<!-- point: zenseact-types-its-routing-failures-and-repairs-the-representation | ZAP's admission unit is the tool call, its context routing is deterministic and measured, and its distinctive loop types routing failures and repairs the representation — the skill file — rather than the model. -->
What the case contributes is precise, and small. Admission operates per tool call rather than per
change: a permission table resolves every call to auto-approve, confirm with a human, or deny, by
tool and by role, and data returned from enterprise systems is sanitized as untrusted
input.[cite: dufwa2026zap] Context is routed deterministically — text similarity selects which
skill instructions load each turn, with an expand-everything fallback — and the routing carries the
record's only measurement, a 67–98 percent context reduction on financial queries and 43–97 percent
on engineering ones.[cite: dufwa2026zap] The distinctive loop is a typed failure taxonomy over that
routing: a miss is classified — no skill matched, the wrong skill fired, the agent struggled with a
tool — and each class carries a prescribed repair to the skill file's description or instructions.
The failure repairs the representation of the organization's knowledge, not the model that reads
it.[cite: dufwa2026zap]

## The Factory in a Box

<!-- point: gitlab-sells-the-capability-and-now-documents-using-it | GitLab belongs in the factory-in-a-box column because it generalizes and sells the production capability — and its own handbook now documents the internal practice too: failing-test-first doctrine, CI as the gate, a test-count guard, and a dated 95-percent-agent-generated service built atop four weeks of harness construction. -->
GitLab belongs in the other column because the capability its account describes is generalized and
sold, to be attached to somebody else's repositories, policies, and engineering knowledge. The
mechanisms are also how GitLab now builds GitLab, and the internal record is worth taking first,
because it prices what the box leaves out. The company's
handbook documents an internal practice with doctrine attached: never give an agent a task
without a failing test; "Fix the environment, not the prompt"; "Prompts are suggestions. CI is a
gate. If the agent can break a rule and still pass the pipeline, the rule doesn't exist" — down to
a CI job that fails when the test count decreases, because agents delete tests to make them
pass.[cite: gitlab2026playbook] And it publishes a dated internal quantity: a 135,000-line Rust
service, roughly 95 percent agent-generated, built by four engineers across 259 merge requests in
about two weeks — after roughly four weeks spent constructing the harness first, and with every
agent-generated change still passing CI, security scanning, and code
review.[cite: gitlab2026orbitissue]

<!-- point: the-factory-in-a-box-is-an-integration-problem-at-both-ends | A factory in a box introduces a boundary an internal factory does not have: the vendor supplies fabricator, orchestration, context machinery, evidence, and generic controls, and the engineering problem is integration, not installation — at both ends of the production line. -->
A factory in a box introduces a boundary an internal factory does not have. The vendor supplies the
fabricator, the orchestration, the context machinery, the evidence infrastructure, and the generic
controls; the customer must connect those to the knowledge and authority that make a change
acceptable in its own system. The engineering problem is therefore integration, not installation —
and it sits at both ends of the production line. Upstream: how does organization-specific intent
enter generic production machinery? Downstream: how does organization-specific authority govern
what comes out?

<!-- point: customer-intent-enters-as-files-at-fixed-paths | Upstream, intent enters GitLab's machinery as files the customer authors at fixed repository paths, under the vendor's stated thesis that anything the agent cannot access in context effectively does not exist; the vendor-recovered context graph supplies relationships, not the intent the customer never wrote down. -->
GitLab's upstream answer is files in the customer's repository, at fixed paths: an `AGENTS.md`,
chat rules at `.gitlab/duo/chat-rules.md`, review instructions at
`.gitlab/duo/mr-review-instructions.yaml`, skills at `skills/<name>/SKILL.md` — plus the customer's
CI configuration and, optionally, authored flow definitions.[cite: gitlab2026customize] The vendor
states the thesis behind the arrangement normatively: "anything the agent can't access in-context
effectively doesn't exist."[cite: gitlab2026orbitissue] What GitLab supplies as infrastructure is
the recovered remainder — a context graph over the customer's code, merge requests, pipelines, and
security findings, assembled from artifacts that already exist. Recovered context carries
relationships. It cannot carry the intent the customer never wrote down.

<!-- point: customer-authority-is-a-withheld-capability | Downstream, admission authority stays human by construction — "The flow never sets the Approve state, even when it finds no issues" — with agent privileges intersected with the triggering human's, tool policy tightenable but never loosenable, and audit machinery that records the human decision without moving it. -->
Downstream, authority is expressed as a withheld capability. GitLab's security review flow "never
sets the **Approve** state, even when it finds no issues" — the machinery may produce, repair, and
advise, and only a person approves.[cite: gitlab2026securityreview] Around that prohibition sits
governance of the action rather than the change: an agent acts under a composite identity whose
privileges are the intersection of its service account's and the triggering human's; every tool
call resolves to allow, ask, or deny under a policy a project may tighten but never loosen below
its group's rule; audit events record which agent acted, in which project, under which policy. The
vendor industrializes the record that a human decision was made. It does not move the decision.

<!-- point: the-record-prices-the-infrastructure-and-not-the-knowledge-authoring | The public record is specific about infrastructure and silent about the knowledge-authoring cost — no case study of a customer writing those files, no measure of output against their quality — and the layer that produced GitLab's own 95-percent service is precisely the layer only the customer can author. -->
Now set the two ends against what the record prices. The infrastructure is specified in detail:
runner tags and executors, network paths, per-vendor credentials, a Kubernetes deployment for the
self-managed context service. The knowledge-authoring cost is not specified at all — no case study
of a customer writing those instruction files, no measure of how factory output varies with their
quality, no account of how long a customer takes to reach the maturity GitLab's own playbook names
as prerequisite for autonomy. That silence is not a hole in the reconstruction; it is the finding.
The CEO's essay locates trust in a durable layer of "context, verification, governance and
evidence" around a replaceable model — an architectural vision, and the sellable
layer.[cite: staples2026abundant] The layer that actually produced GitLab's own
95-percent-agent-generated service was the failing tests written first, the CI gates, the
repo-resident context, the test-count guard — precisely the layer only the customer can author, and
the one thing the box cannot ship.

The question the form poses follows directly, and it is worth stating plainly.

<!-- inline-quote -->
> How much organization-specific engineering knowledge must be externalized before an off-the-shelf
> factory can reliably produce controlled changes to that organization's software?

<!-- point: the-externalization-question-is-the-modeling-problem-commercially | The question restates the Modeling problem in commercial terms, and the factory chapter already supplied its economics: a purchased factory that works only when the buyer describes the software almost completely has recreated the arrangement the earlier software factories died of. -->
The question is the representation problem in commercial dress, and {{sec:the-software-factory}}
already supplied its economics. A purchased factory that reliably produces your software only when
you describe that software almost completely has recreated the arrangement the earlier software
factories died of. So the buyer's side of the transaction is a modeling decision: which
consequential intent to externalize, in what form, at what cost, before generic machinery can
respect it. {{chapter:theory}} takes up what that externalization costs.

## Model-First Factories

<!-- point: model-first-production-inverts-where-intent-enters | Model-first production inverts where intent enters: explicit engineering models, not the repository and its tests, carry the system's semantics, and the software is generated downstream of them. -->
Siemens sits at the end of the second axis the software-first accounts do not reach.
{{sec:the-software-factory}} told this ambition's history in software: enterprise architecture and
model-driven development tried to determine the consequential decisions upstream, in shared
representations, and lost economically rather than conceptually. Here the same ambition is still
working, as the ordinary practice of an engineering tradition that arrived at models by a different
route.

<!-- point: siemens-model-first-evidence-is-eigen-and-agent-studio | The load-bearing Siemens evidence is Eigen — control code generated from a TIA Portal engineering model, iterating until pre-defined performance benchmarks are met, a tolerance in this chapter's exact sense — and the 2024 Agent Studio research on SysML v2 with simulation as validator: a model supplies goal and context, a deterministic engine supplies the verdict. -->
Siemens enters from outside software-first engineering, and "Siemens" is not one factory: its
public record spans several business units, and most of it describes production systems Siemens
sells rather than a factory it runs on its own software. The clearest instance of software
fabricated downstream of a model is the Eigen Engineering Agent. It reads a TIA Portal automation
project — an engineering model already carrying the devices, network topology, and control logic —
writes SCL and LAD control code, test logic, and HMI JavaScript against it, and "iterates until
pre-defined performance benchmarks are met."[cite: siemens2026eigen] The admission criterion is a
pre-set acceptance region, not a specified realization — a tolerance in this chapter's exact sense.
Two years earlier, Siemens' Agent Studio research placed agents on formal modeling languages,
SysML v2 among them, with generated candidate architectures validated through simulation and the
system waiting for the engineer's decision at critical points.[cite: liu2024agentstudio] The
recurring structure across the Siemens record: a model supplies the goal and the context, a
deterministic engine — a simulator, a compiler, a benchmark harness — supplies the verdict, and the
agent searches freely between them. That is the bargain the earlier model-driven attempts could not
strike.

<!-- point: siemens-vision-disclaims-itself-and-the-gaps-sit-where-stakes-are-highest | The broader agentic-engineering architecture Siemens publishes is an explicit research vision about simulation, not software realization; no source establishes a model-implementation conformance gate, and Eigen's public record names no human approval step at all — while the one account of Siemens changing its own software keeps a human in the loop at every step and grounds that gate in a compliance obligation. -->
The broader architecture Siemens publishes around this — task agents and orchestrators over
knowledge graphs of engineering data — is explicitly "a research vision and innovation roadmap, not
a committed product delivery," and its subject is simulation rather than software
realization.[cite: siemens2026a3e] Two silences matter more than the vision. No Siemens source
establishes a conformance mechanism that rejects a change when model and implementation diverge —
Eigen's benchmark is a threshold, not a correspondence. And Eigen's public record, for an agent
writing control code destined for physical machinery, names no human review or approval step at
all. Source silence, as always, is not organizational absence — but here the silence sits exactly
where the stakes are highest. Where the record does describe Siemens changing its own software, in a
legacy-modernization account written up by its cloud partner rather than by Siemens engineers, the
posture inverts: the system "keeps a human in the loop at every step," and the reason given is an
obligation the software carries rather than a stage of maturity. Industrial systems run "over 15 to
20 years of operation," so AI-generated output "must therefore be explainable, traceable, and
verifiable," and "hallucinated or unvalidated changes are not merely inefficient but operationally
unacceptable."[cite: nawalgaria2026siemens]

<!-- point: a-cheap-and-capable-fabricator-changes-what-a-model-must-determine | The sharp difference from the old model-driven dream is that today's fabricator is cheap and capable at once, so a model-first factory need not determine every realization decision — only the consequential ones. -->
One difference from the old dream matters more than the rest, and it is the difference
{{sec:the-software-factory}} isolated. Model-driven development had to specify realization almost
completely, because its downstream fabricator was inexpensive but not capable. Today's fabricator is
both at once. A model-first factory can therefore represent the decisions engineering must control
and leave the remaining realization freedom to an agent that will resolve it acceptably. **The model
need not determine every realization decision.** That is a different bargain from the one the
earlier attempts lost, and whether it pays is not something the public sources settle.

<!-- point: model-first-is-a-position-and-siemens-sells-the-combined-cell | Model-first is a position on one axis rather than a maturity level, and the axis is independent of the first — Siemens itself partially fills the combined cell, selling model-first factories in a box for the narrow software of control logic and chip design, while no observed case sells one for general-purpose software. -->
Two cautions keep the axis honest. Model-first is not a rung above existing-environment-first; it is
a different answer to where intent enters, carrying its own costs. And the axis is independent of
the first one. A factory in a box can be sold as model-first, driven by models the customer authors
rather than a repository the vendor crawls — and Siemens itself shows the combined cell is not
empty: Eigen and its EDA verification toolkit are sold production systems driven by the customer's
own engineering models, in the narrow software of control logic and chip design. For
general-purpose software, no observed case sells that today — a fact about a young industry rather
than a constraint on the design space.

## What the Factories Share, and Where They Differ

<!-- point: four-moves-form-a-shared-baseline | Four moves recur across all seven: knowledge externalized, action bounded, generation separated from evidence, consequential decisions kept human — on three different stated groundings, evidence adequacy, responsibility, and compliance — plus Uber's fifth, the environment continuously measured. -->
Across the seven accounts, four moves recur strongly enough to form a shared baseline. **Knowledge
is externalized** rather than rebuilt from a person's memory on every task — as shared context,
knowledge graphs, instructions, skills, policy corpora, estate models. **Action is bounded** through
scoped tools, permissions, roles, and other restrictions on what an agent can do. **Generation is
separated from evidence** through tests, validators, independent reviewers, simulations, and merge
gates. And **consequential decisions remain human**, though the corpus states three different reasons
for keeping them there: because the available representations and evidence do not yet justify
delegating them (Uber), because a machine cannot take responsibility for a decision (Shopify), or
because a compliance obligation requires a person (Siemens). Uber makes a fifth move visible at
scale: the environment itself is
continuously measured and optimized, so model choice, context, decomposition, and reusable
procedures become engineering variables rather than fixed properties of the reasoner.

<!-- point: the-differences-are-as-informative-as-the-commonalities | The factories differ in where admission sits, how much knowledge they route into each task, whether failure-to-structure conversion is measured (the mechanism is documented nearly everywhere; aggregate yield once, class-recurrence nowhere), and — most sharply — how explicitly they model the governed system; no ordering follows. -->
The differences are as informative as the commonalities. The factories vary in where they place
admission (Cloudflare's coordinator holds the merge gate itself, under human-held policy and a
logged override; Spotify automerges its deterministic fleet changes while its agent-authored
changes wait for human review; Uber keeps approval human by statement, and GitLab by construction in
the one flow whose approval posture it documents), how much organizational knowledge they route into
each task, whether the conversion of failures into shared structure is measured (the mechanism is now
documented nearly everywhere in the corpus; its aggregate yield is measured once, in Shopify's
controlled merge-rate rise, and whether any converted control reduced recurrence of the failure class
it was built for is measured nowhere), and — most sharply — how explicitly they model the
governed system itself. No ordering follows from those differences. A human admission boundary may
be exactly right where the decision remains semantic; a richer model may not repay its cost in
another environment. The comparison exposes design choices and boundaries, not a winner.

<!-- point: software-first-accounts-model-knowledge-not-behavior | The modeling ceiling: the software-first accounts publicly show knowledge and governance representations; Siemens crosses into executable models from a model-first tradition; DocAble additionally checks selected model-implementation relations. -->
One difference deserves its own name, because {{chapter:theory}} will need it. Across the
software-first accounts, the richest representations described publicly are **knowledge and
governance representations**: service catalogs, dependency information, policy corpora, context
graphs, skills, provenance records. These are models in the broad sense — they externalize engineering knowledge and
extend what an agent can recover reliably — but the sources give little evidence of behavioral,
process, scenario, or invariant models serving as the primary surface of software reasoning. Siemens
crosses that boundary from a model-first tradition. The originating case goes one step further: for
selected models, mechanical checks compare declared relations to the implementation and reject
changes when the relations no longer hold — though only for particular relations, at a cost the case
does not show scaling. [ref:modeling-ceiling-tiers] states the finding as three tiers and marks
where the source evidence sits.

<!-- figure: assets/modeling-ceiling-tiers.svg | *The Modeling Ceiling.* Three tiers of modeling: engineering knowledge, executable system models, and checked correspondence. The software-first corpus concentrates on the knowledge tier; Siemens reaches the executable-model tier; the originating case checks selected model–implementation relations, and the public sources do not establish comparable mechanisms. The tiers locate the public source evidence, not organizational capability. -->

<!-- point: the-common-structure-is-leverage-organizational-capital | At organizational scale the recurring structure is leverage: one policy shapes thousands of changes, one representation guides a fleet, one learned constraint protects every later task — the organizational face of engineering capital. -->
Where the factories do converge, the common structure is leverage. Organizations centralize shared
mechanisms while keeping domain judgment distributed; human judgment moves upstream, from
hand-authoring changes to scoping and supervising fleets; private learning becomes a shared
artifact — sessions, policies, skills, defaults; and the unit of governance grows from a task to
classes of work, fleets, and platforms. A scarce human decision becomes more valuable when placed
where the environment carries it forward: one policy shapes thousands of changes; one shared
representation guides work across a fleet; one learned constraint protects every later task over the
same surface. That is the organizational face of engineering capital. Governance does not remove
judgment; it gives selected judgments multiplicative reach.

## The Engineers Inside the Factories

<!-- point: the-rhetorical-sequence-ends-with-the-people | The section closes with the people inside the factories: after the institutional accounts, engineers' own first-person descriptions of the supervisory mismatch. -->
The seven accounts above are institutional self-descriptions — engineering blogs and vendor
architecture papers, systems presented at their best. The same phenomenon has a first-person
version. Engineers and engineering leaders have begun describing, in their own language and in
public, what it feels like to work inside a factory whose realization capacity has outrun its
supervisory interface.

<!-- point: the-diagnosis-a-common-asymmetry | The closing diagnosis: the factories differ substantially, but the reports expose a common asymmetry — automated realization is scaling faster than the supervisory interface around it. -->
These factories differ substantially — in what they model, what they mechanize, and what they still
ask a person to decide. But the reports expose a common asymmetry. **Automated realization is
scaling faster than the supervisory interface around it.** Where supervision remains attached
primarily to generated implementation, additional realization also generates additional material
for humans to comprehend and evaluate — the diagnosis {{sec:the-software-factory}} derived from two
centuries of production engineering, now visible in contemporary accounts. Several organizations
respond by changing what supervision attaches *to*: policy corpora, estate models, benchmarks,
permission structures, and provenance. What none of the public accounts yet shows is the particular
combination the originating case exhibits — representations of the governed system itself,
joined and enforced, with failures converted into structure. Whether that difference is a maturity
gap, a cost judgment, or evidence that DocAble's arrangement is peculiar to its circumstances is a
question the evidence cannot settle — and the next section is careful not to.
