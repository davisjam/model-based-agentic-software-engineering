# Other Agentic Software Factories

<!-- point: chapter-asks-whether-docable-structures-recur-elsewhere | The section reads seven public accounts as reconstructions to ask whether independently developed factories exhibit the same pressures. -->
DocAble supplies depth, and depth from one system proves little on its own. The useful next question
is whether independently developed agentic software factories exhibit the same pressures. This
section reads seven public industrial accounts — Uber, GitLab, Cloudflare, Siemens, Spotify,
Shopify, and Zenseact — as **reconstructions** rather than case studies: public sources expose
selected mechanisms, not the full histories that produced them, and silence in a source does not
establish that an organization lacks a capability. [appendix: appendix-field-guide] presents the
evidence behind each reading.

<!-- point: software-factory-is-our-analytical-frame-not-their-claim | None of these organizations is claimed as a MAGE adopter; "software factory" is this book's analytical representation of their reported engineering systems, and the cases are expected to vary. -->
One framing rule governs everything below, stated once. **None of these organizations is claimed as
a MAGE adopter.** "Software factory" is this book's analytical representation of their reported
engineering systems — the lens of {{sec:the-software-factory}} applied to somebody else's machinery —
and the cases are valuable precisely where they do *not* resemble DocAble. Some factories have
strong task packaging but little explicit system modeling; some have sophisticated validation while
supervision stays centered on human review; some deliberately keep admission human. The variation is
the evidence.

<!-- point: one-consistent-lens-six-questions | Each factory is read through one consistent lens: what increased realization, what work inherits, what structures supervision, what stays attached to code inspection, what evidence exists outside the producer, what remains human. -->
Each factory is read through the same lens, six questions long: What increased realization? What
does new work inherit? What structures supervision? What remains attached to direct code inspection?
What evidence and controls exist outside the producer? What still requires human judgment?
[ref:six-entry-points] locates where each organization starts and where it invests.

## Organizing the Emerging Factory Forms

<!-- point: the-cases-are-all-production-systems-for-controlled-change | All seven are production systems for controlled software change in the factory chapter's sense; they differ in where production capability resides, how an organization obtains it, and how consequential engineering intent reaches the agents performing realization. -->
{{sec:the-software-factory}} defined a software factory as a production system for controlled
change. Every organization below runs one. What separates them is not whether they qualify but
three related things: where the production capability resides, how an organization obtains it, and
how consequential engineering intent reaches the agents performing realization. Two distinctions
carry that, and the reader should have them before the cases rather than after.

<!-- point: internal-factory-versus-factory-in-a-box | The first distinction asks who supplies the production system and whose software it produces: an internal factory is built by an organization to change its own software, while a factory in a box is agentic production capability packaged and sold to other organizations. -->
The first distinction asks **who supplies the production system, and whose software it produces**.
An *internal factory* is built by an organization to produce and change its own software. Uber,
Cloudflare, Spotify, Shopify, and Zenseact each describe one. GitLab describes something else, and
that alternative is new enough to need a name.

> **Factory in a box.** Agentic software-production capability packaged and sold to other
> organizations, which connect it to their own repositories, engineering knowledge, policies, and
> business intent.

<!-- point: existing-environment-versus-model-first | The second distinction asks how engineering intent enters production: most factories begin with the existing environment and delegate realization from there, while a model-first factory directs production largely through explicit engineering models. -->
The second distinction asks **how engineering intent enters production**. Most current factories
begin with the existing environment. Repositories, tests, pipelines, issue systems, and
organizational context are what an agent is pointed at, and realization is delegated from there.
A *model-first* factory works the other way: explicit engineering models sit much closer to the
primary interface through which production is directed, and realization happens downstream of them.
Siemens is the contrasting case.

<!-- point: the-two-distinctions-are-independent-axes | The two distinctions are independent axes rather than three factory types or three maturity levels: each answers one question and leaves the other open, so a factory in a box could also be model-first. -->
Read these as two axes, not as three kinds of factory and not as a ladder. Each distinction answers
one question and leaves the other open. Nothing forbids a purchased factory driven by models the
customer authors, and nothing makes model-first a rung above existing-environment-first. The forms
that happen to be occupied today are an observation about a young industry, not a structure in the
design space. [ref:factory-forms] states the two distinctions side by side.

## Internal Software Factories

<!-- point: five-internal-factories-enter-from-five-different-problems | Five accounts describe factories an organization built for its own software, each entering from a different problem — factory economics, institutional policy, fleet-scale change, organizational knowledge, and where platform authority sits — and all beginning from the existing environment. -->
Five of the seven accounts describe a factory an organization built to change its own software, and
each enters from a different problem. Uber enters from the economics of the factory itself;
Cloudflare from institutional policy; Spotify from fleet-scale change; Shopify from organizational
knowledge; Zenseact from where authority should sit between a platform and its domain teams. All
five begin with the existing environment and delegate more of realization to agents from there. The
common form is what makes the variation legible.

### Uber: the environment as the engineered object

<!-- point: uber-treats-the-surrounding-environment-as-an-object-of-engineering | Uber's realization increase is organization-wide, and the environment around the agents is itself the engineered object: benchmarks, model selection, compiled context, reusable skills, cost-per-outcome. -->
Uber gives the fullest account of realization increasing at organizational scale: more than 70
percent of pull requests attributed to local or cloud agents, more than 3,600 engineer-created agent
skills executing over 30,000 times per day, managed agents performing code review, CI repair,
end-to-end changes, alert triage, debugging, and maintenance.[cite: medisetty2026factory] What new
work inherits is the interesting structure: real workloads become benchmarks; models are selected
against cost, quality, and reliability; context and tool access are compiled down to reduce
unnecessary reasoning; recurring workflows harden into reusable skills — in one example,
deterministic tool orchestration moves out of repeated model turns into executable code, cutting
token use by more than half on common workflows and by more than 90 percent in bulk
cases.[cite: medisetty2026factory] Supervision is structured by measurement: managed agents are
evaluated in units such as cost per merged pull request, review, or alert. What remains human is the
judgment those measurements inform — and the reasoning surface is a knowledge-and-context graph, not
an executable model of the software's behavior. Uber's factory optimizes the economics of reasoning;
it does not, on the public record, represent the system the reasoning is about.

### Cloudflare: policy as a machine-readable corpus

<!-- point: cloudflare-policy-first-models-obligations-not-the-software | Cloudflare externalizes a standards corpus into machine-readable requirements — mechanically checkable obligations become custom checks, ambiguous ones remain review questions — modeling obligations, not the governed software. -->
Cloudflare gives the cleanest policy-first entry. A standards corpus that had outgrown individual
memory was externalized into structured, machine-readable requirements; obligations that can be
checked mechanically become custom checks, while obligations requiring semantic judgment remain
review questions.[cite: reimann2026codex] The move separates the authority to establish policy from
the machinery that applies it repeatedly — humans retain policy authority while agents extend the
reach of decisions made elsewhere. The limitation is as revealing as the mechanism: the source richly
models **obligations** and their enforcement, and shows much less of an executable model of the
governed software itself, or of failures being converted into new environmental structure over time.

### Spotify: fleet-scale change over an estate model

<!-- point: spotify-fleet-scale-migration-moves-judgment-upstream | Spotify's Honk moves fleet-scale migration from distributed manual work to centrally scoped agent execution over an estate model — one engineer scopes, a fleet implements, supervision handles exceptions. -->
Spotify's Honk system moves large-scale code migration from distributed manual implementation toward
centrally scoped, fleet-executed work: a migration that could previously involve hundreds of teams
over weeks can be scoped by one engineer over a few days, with tooling identifying and scheduling
targets, agents executing changes concurrently, and the engineer supervising the fleet and handling
exceptions.[cite: spotify2026honk] The inheritance is the estate representation — a system model and
service catalog carrying identities, ownership, dependencies, and lineage — through which migrations
are targeted rather than rediscovered per repository. Human judgment moves upstream to what to
change and where; reported activity increases measure implementation volume, not durable
throughput, and the admission machinery pushes further toward automatic fleet-scale acceptance than
most of the corpus.

### Shopify: shared sessions as organizational context

<!-- point: shopify-turns-shared-interaction-into-organizational-context | Shopify makes agent work multiplayer by construction — tens of thousands of shared sessions become searchable artifacts from which skills and defaults are extracted — a soft externalization with supervision still centered on people. -->
Shopify's account describes agent work occurring in shared, observable channels rather than private
sessions — one month's snapshot counts 59,918 sessions across 5,170 channels involving more than
7,000 people, "multiplayer by construction."[cite: moreno2026river] Shared sessions become
searchable organizational artifacts; recurring knowledge is extracted into skills, conventions, and
defaults later agents retrieve. Through the lens: realization increases broadly, and what work
inherits is *retrievable prose and skills* — a soft externalization, not a structured executable
model — while supervision remains centered on the people in the channels. The record establishes
session-to-reusable-knowledge; whether that becomes durable engineering capital is an
interpretation, not an observation.

### Zenseact: shared substrate, distributed domain judgment

<!-- point: zenseact-splits-platform-substrate-from-domain-judgment | Zenseact centralizes shared substrate — authentication, execution, safety envelope — in a platform team while domain teams keep their own agents and expertise, with routing machinery selecting task-relevant context. -->
Zenseact, building driver-assistance software, describes an organizational split: a platform team
owns common substrate — authentication, sessions, execution, and the safety envelope — while domain
teams retain their own agents, tools, instructions, and expertise.[cite: dufwa2026zap] Routing
machinery selects task-relevant expertise instead of loading the whole organizational corpus into
every reasoning window. The factory reading is about where knowledge and authority sit: centralize
the mechanisms that should be shared, leave domain judgment with the people who hold it. The sources
show the division of the platform, not the resulting productivity or safety — and supervision, on
the public record, remains attached to the domain teams' own review practices.

## The Factory in a Box

<!-- point: gitlab-sells-the-production-capability-rather-than-only-using-it | GitLab is the factory-in-a-box case because it generalizes and sells the production capability for other organizations' software, so what the vendor supplies and what the buying organization must still bring come apart. -->
GitLab belongs in the other column, and the reason is not the size of its agent deployment. The
mechanisms its account describes are not primarily how GitLab builds GitLab. They are a production
capability GitLab generalizes and sells, to be attached to somebody else's repositories, policies,
and engineering knowledge. That separation is what makes the form worth naming: generic machinery
travels between organizations, and consequential engineering intent does not.

<!-- point: gitlab-builds-a-durable-environment-around-a-replaceable-reasoner | GitLab starts from trust in abundant code and wraps the model in a durable layer — context, verification, governance, identity, provenance — kept independent of any one reasoner. -->
GitLab starts from the other end: as code becomes abundant, the scarce problem shifts to trusting
it. Its answer wraps the model in a durable layer of context, verification, governance, identity,
and provenance, kept deliberately independent of any one model so the organization's controls
persist as the reasoner changes.[cite: staples2026abundant] Its context graph makes relationships
among requirements, code, pipelines, deployments, production signals, policy, and evidence available
to agents and governance machinery. And its account describes the same conversion DocAble's history
kept exhibiting: a production failure can become a regression test; a security incident can become a
policy; a compliance obligation can become continuous validation — knowledge gained in one episode
converted into structure later work inherits.[cite: staples2026abundant] What stays attached to
inspection is honest to note: the account is a vendor architectural vision rather than a measured
deployment, and its representations preserve engineering context and control relationships, not an
executable model of behavior.

<!-- point: the-factory-in-a-box-poses-the-externalization-question | The factory-in-a-box form poses the question the section hands forward: how much organization-specific engineering knowledge must be externalized before a purchased factory can reliably produce controlled changes to that organization's software? -->
Notice what the vendor cannot ship. A context graph, a verification layer, and a provenance record
are machinery; the relationships they carry are the customer's. That is the form's interesting
question rather than its commercial one, and it is worth stating plainly.

<!-- inline-quote -->
> How much organization-specific engineering knowledge must be externalized before an off-the-shelf
> factory can reliably produce controlled changes to that organization's software?

<!-- point: the-externalization-question-is-the-modeling-problem-commercially | The question restates the Modeling problem in commercial terms, and the factory chapter already supplied its economics: a purchased factory that works only when the buyer describes the software almost completely has recreated the arrangement the earlier software factories died of. -->
The question is the representation problem in commercial dress, and {{sec:the-software-factory}}
already supplied its economics. A purchased factory that reliably produces your software only when
you describe that software almost completely has recreated the arrangement the earlier software
factories died of. So the buyer's side of the transaction is a modeling decision: which
consequential intent to externalize, in what form, at what cost, before generic machinery can
respect it. {{chapter:theory}} takes up what that externalization costs.

## Model-First Factories

<!-- point: model-first-production-inverts-where-intent-enters | Model-first production inverts where intent enters: explicit engineering models, not the repository and its tests, carry the system's semantics, and the software is generated downstream of them. -->
Siemens sits at the end of the second axis the software-first accounts do not reach.
{{sec:the-software-factory}} told this ambition's history in software: enterprise architecture and
model-driven development tried to determine the consequential decisions upstream, in shared
representations, and lost economically rather than conceptually. Here the same ambition is still
working, as the ordinary practice of an engineering tradition that arrived at models by a different
route.

<!-- point: siemens-model-first-tradition-crosses-the-boundary | Siemens, from a model-first engineering tradition, keeps behavioral structure, requirements, simulation, and traceability in persistent representations with software generation downstream — proof the strong end of Modeling is ordinary practice elsewhere. -->
Siemens enters from outside software-first engineering, and that is exactly its value. In its
model-based tradition, persistent engineering representations — requirements, behavioral structure,
simulation, traceability, virtual hardware — carry the system's semantics, and software generation
occurs downstream of those representations.[cite: siemens2026a3e] Supervision operates through the
models because the models came first; the fabricated artifact is one projection of them. The case
establishes that the strong end of the Modeling Principle is not a novelty this book invented — it
is ordinary practice in engineering domains that never enjoyed software's luxury of fused
engineer-fabricators. What the sources do not establish is an admission mechanism that rejects
changes when model and implementation diverge; source silence, as always, is not organizational
absence.

<!-- point: a-cheap-and-capable-fabricator-changes-what-a-model-must-determine | The sharp difference from the old model-driven dream is that today's fabricator is cheap and capable at once, so a model-first factory need not determine every realization decision — only the consequential ones. -->
One difference from the old dream matters more than the rest, and it is the difference
{{sec:the-software-factory}} isolated. Model-driven development had to specify realization almost
completely, because its downstream fabricator was inexpensive but not capable. Today's fabricator is
both at once. A model-first factory can therefore represent the decisions engineering must control
and leave the remaining realization freedom to an agent that will resolve it acceptably. **The model
need not determine every realization decision.** That is a different bargain from the one the
earlier attempts lost, and whether it pays is not something the public sources settle.

<!-- point: model-first-is-a-position-on-an-axis-not-a-destination | Model-first is a position on one axis rather than a maturity level, and it is independent of the first distinction: a factory in a box could be sold as model-first, driven by models the customer authors, and none of the observed cases is. -->
Two cautions keep the axis honest. Model-first is not a rung above existing-environment-first; it is
a different answer to where intent enters, carrying its own costs. And the axis is independent of
the first one. A factory in a box could be sold as model-first, driven by models the customer
authors rather than a repository the vendor crawls. No observed case does that today, which is a
fact about a young industry rather than a constraint on the design space.

## What the Factories Share, and Where They Differ

<!-- point: four-moves-form-a-shared-baseline | Four moves recur across all seven: knowledge externalized, action bounded, generation separated from evidence, consequential decisions kept human — plus Uber's fifth, the environment continuously measured. -->
Across the seven accounts, four moves recur strongly enough to form a shared baseline. **Knowledge
is externalized** rather than rebuilt from a person's memory on every task — as shared context,
knowledge graphs, instructions, skills, policy corpora, estate models. **Action is bounded** through
scoped tools, permissions, roles, and other restrictions on what an agent can do. **Generation is
separated from evidence** through tests, validators, independent reviewers, simulations, and merge
gates. And **consequential decisions remain human** where the available representations and evidence
do not justify delegating them. Uber makes a fifth move visible at scale: the environment itself is
continuously measured and optimized, so model choice, context, decomposition, and reusable
procedures become engineering variables rather than fixed properties of the reasoner.

<!-- point: the-differences-are-as-informative-as-the-commonalities | The factories differ in admission automation, knowledge routing, whether failures change the shared environment, and — most sharply — how explicitly they model the governed system; no ordering follows. -->
The differences are as informative as the commonalities. The factories vary in how aggressively they
automate admission (Spotify pushes toward fleet-scale acceptance, while Zenseact's supervision stays
with the domain teams' own review and Cloudflare routes every semantically judged obligation back to
a reviewer), how much organizational knowledge they route into each task, whether recurring failures
change the shared environment (explicit in GitLab's account, absent from several others), and — most
sharply — how explicitly they model the governed system itself. No ordering follows from those
differences. A human admission boundary may be exactly right where the decision remains semantic; a
richer model may not repay its cost in another environment. The comparison exposes design choices
and boundaries, not a winner.

<!-- point: software-first-accounts-model-knowledge-not-behavior | The modeling ceiling: the software-first accounts publicly show knowledge and governance representations; Siemens crosses into executable models from a model-first tradition; DocAble additionally checks selected model-implementation relations. -->
One difference deserves its own name, because {{chapter:theory}} will need it. In the six software-first
accounts, the richest representations described publicly are **knowledge and governance
representations**: service catalogs, dependency information, policy corpora, context graphs, skills,
provenance records. These are models in the broad sense — they externalize engineering knowledge and
extend what an agent can recover reliably — but the sources give little evidence of behavioral,
process, scenario, or invariant models serving as the primary surface of software reasoning. Siemens
crosses that boundary from a model-first tradition. The originating case goes one step further: for
selected models, mechanical checks compare declared relations to the implementation and reject
changes when the relations no longer hold — though only for particular relations, at a cost the case
does not show scaling. [ref:modeling-ceiling-tiers] states the finding as three tiers and marks
where the source evidence sits.

The software-first corpus concentrates on the knowledge tier; Siemens reaches the executable-model tier; the originating case checks selected model–implementation relations, and the public sources do not establish comparable mechanisms. The tiers locate the public source evidence, not organizational capability. -->

<!-- point: the-common-structure-is-leverage-organizational-capital | At organizational scale the recurring structure is leverage: one policy shapes thousands of changes, one representation guides a fleet, one learned constraint protects every later task — the organizational face of engineering capital. -->
Where the factories do converge, the common structure is leverage. Organizations centralize shared
mechanisms while keeping domain judgment distributed; human judgment moves upstream, from
hand-authoring changes to scoping and supervising fleets; private learning becomes a shared
artifact — sessions, policies, skills, defaults; and the unit of governance grows from a task to
classes of work, fleets, and platforms. A scarce human decision becomes more valuable when placed
where the environment carries it forward: one policy shapes thousands of changes; one shared
representation guides work across a fleet; one learned constraint protects every later task over the
same surface. That is the organizational face of engineering capital. Governance does not remove
judgment; it gives selected judgments multiplicative reach.

## The Engineers Inside the Factories

<!-- point: the-rhetorical-sequence-ends-with-the-people | The section closes with the people inside the factories: after the institutional accounts, engineers' own first-person descriptions of the supervisory mismatch. -->
The seven accounts above are institutional self-descriptions — engineering blogs and vendor
architecture papers, systems presented at their best. The same phenomenon has a first-person
version. Engineers and engineering leaders have begun describing, in their own language and in
public, what it feels like to work inside a factory whose realization capacity has outrun its
supervisory interface.

<!-- point: the-diagnosis-a-common-asymmetry | The closing diagnosis: the factories differ substantially, but the reports expose a common asymmetry — automated realization is scaling faster than the supervisory interface around it. -->
These factories differ substantially — in what they model, what they mechanize, and what they still
ask a person to decide. But the reports expose a common asymmetry. **Automated realization is
scaling faster than the supervisory interface around it.** Where supervision remains attached
primarily to generated implementation, additional realization also generates additional material
for humans to comprehend and evaluate — the diagnosis {{sec:the-software-factory}} derived from two
centuries of production engineering, now visible in contemporary accounts. The organizations that
have moved furthest are the ones that changed what supervision attaches *to*: policy corpora, estate
models, benchmarks, permission structures, provenance. What none of the public accounts yet shows is
the full progression the originating case exhibits — representations of the governed system itself,
joined and enforced, with failures converted into structure. Whether that difference is a maturity
gap, a cost judgment, or evidence that DocAble's arrangement is peculiar to its circumstances is a
question the evidence cannot settle — and the next section is careful not to.
