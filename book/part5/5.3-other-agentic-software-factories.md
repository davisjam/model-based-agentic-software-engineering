# Other Agentic Software Factories

<!-- point: one-system-one-engineer-one-problem-so-how-do-others-do-it | DocAble is one system built by one engineer for one problem; the natural question is how engineers in other organizations, under different constraints, approach the same problem. -->
My experience with DocAble showed me one way of building reliable software with unreliable agents. But that is one system, built by one engineer, for one problem. The natural question is how other engineers, working in other organizations and under different constraints, are approaching the same problem.

<!-- point: seven-reconstructions-differ-in-representation-direction-evidence-and-judgment | Seven public accounts are reconstructed; they differ in what they represent, how they direct and constrain agents, what evidence they require, where they keep human judgment — and in whether the factory is built for their own software or for somebody else's. -->
In this section, I reconstruct agentic software factories from public reports by seven major companies: Uber, GitLab, Cloudflare, Siemens, Spotify, Shopify, and Zenseact. These factories differ in what they represent, how they direct and constrain agents, what evidence they require, and where they retain human judgment. They also differ in a more basic way: some organizations are building factories for their own software, while others are building factories for somebody else to use.

<!-- point: the-public-sources-are-interested-accounts-read-conservatively | The sources are company-published accounts that mix technical communication with promotion, so reported architectures and measurements count as evidence of what an organization built and observed, while effectiveness and causal claims are treated cautiously and numbers keep their own definitions and denominators. -->
These sources also require a conservative reading. Company engineering blogs, talks, and product documentation mix technical communication with organizational and product promotion. They can provide evidence about mechanisms, measurements, and operating experience, but they are not independent evaluations of the systems they describe. I therefore treat reported architectures and measurements as evidence of what an organization built and observed, while treating broader claims about effectiveness or causality more cautiously. Missing information is recorded as missing rather than interpreted as evidence that a mechanism does not exist. Numerical claims are retained with their reported definitions and denominators where available; incompatible measures are not treated as directly comparable.

<!-- point: commercial-cases-share-an-emphasis-on-organizing-agentic-production | The commercial systems differ in architecture and organizational setting but share an emphasis on organizing agentic production — supplying context, structuring work, controlling tool access, arranging human review, and determining what may proceed — which matches process-oriented accounts of agentic software engineering. -->
The commercial systems examined here differ substantially in architecture and organizational setting, but they share a recognizable emphasis on the organization of agentic production: supplying context, structuring work, controlling access to tools and resources, arranging human review, and determining what changes may proceed. This emphasis maps well to process-oriented approaches to agentic software engineering advocated by Hassan and by O'Connell, which structure agent work through explicit workflows, artifacts, gates, evidence, and human intervention.[cite: hassan2026; oconnell2026] The cases below show how commercial organizations are engineering these concerns in practice.

<!-- point: two-dimensions-who-supplies-and-how-intent-enters | The factories differ along two dimensions: who supplies the production system, and how engineering intent enters production. -->
{{sec:the-software-factory}} defined a software factory as a production system for controlled change. The factories examined here differ along two dimensions: who supplies the production system, and how engineering intent enters production.

<!-- point: internal-factory-versus-factory-in-a-box | The first distinction is between an internal factory, built and operated by an organization to change its own software, and a factory in a box, a generalized production system supplied to other organizations. -->
The first distinction is between an *internal factory*, built and operated by an organization to change its own software, and a *factory in a box*, a generalized production system supplied to other organizations.

> **Factory in a box.** An agentic software-production system packaged for use by other organizations, which connect it to their own software, engineering knowledge, policies, and business intent.

<!-- point: existing-environment-first-versus-model-first | The second distinction concerns how engineering intent enters the factory: existing-environment-first factories give agents repositories, tests, pipelines and organizational context to recover what they need, while a model-first factory places explicit engineering models upstream of realization. -->
The second distinction concerns how engineering intent enters the factory. Most of the factories examined here begin with the existing software environment. Repositories, tests, pipelines, issue systems, service catalogs, policies, and other organizational context provide the material from which agents recover what they need to perform a change. I call these *existing-environment-first* factories. A *model-first* factory instead places explicit engineering models upstream of realization: selected engineering intent is represented in models, and software realization proceeds downstream from them.

<!-- point: the-two-distinctions-are-independent-axes | The two distinctions are independent: either factory form can be existing-environment-first or model-first, and the seven public accounts occupy only part of that design space. -->
These distinctions are independent. An internal factory can be existing-environment-first or model-first. So can a factory in a box. The seven public accounts happen to occupy only part of that design space. [ref:factory-forms] provides the map I will use for the rest of the section.

<!-- point: the-shared-substrate-is-the-baseline-and-the-cases-report-the-differences | Four structures recur across all seven factories — knowledge externalized, action bounded, realization evaluated against evidence, and consequential judgment reserved to people — so the substrate is established once here and each case that follows is organized around what it alone teaches. -->
Across these factories, several structures recur. Engineering knowledge is externalized so that agents need not reconstruct it from a person's memory on every task. Agent action is bounded through tools, permissions, roles, execution environments, or other controls. Realization is evaluated through tests, validators, reviewers, simulations, or other evidence before consequential changes are admitted. And some engineering judgments remain with people where the available representations, evidence, or organizational obligations do not justify delegation. These common structures establish the baseline. The cases that follow focus instead on where the factories differ: what each organization has chosen to engineer around its fabricators, what operating experience has forced it to change, and what that experience teaches about constructing and maintaining an agentic software factory.

<!-- point: the-accounts-also-show-how-the-factories-are-operated | The public accounts also reveal operation: some organizations measure cost and throughput in detail, some measure review load, admission, rework or incidents, and several describe failures that moved knowledge into durable production structure — so the cases serve three related questions: how production is structured, what the organization chooses to measure, and what operating experience causes the factory to change. -->
The public accounts also reveal how these factories are being operated. Some organizations measure the cost and throughput of production in considerable detail. Some measure review load, admission, rework, incidents, or other properties of the factory and its output. Several describe failures that caused knowledge or judgment to move into durable production structure. The evidence is uneven: a mechanism may be described without its effectiveness being measured, and a source may publish extensive activity data while saying little about the quality of the resulting software. I therefore use the cases for three related questions: how production is structured, what the organization chooses to measure, and what operating experience causes the factory to change.

<!-- point: the-uneven-answers-are-themselves-informative | The cases answer the three questions unevenly, and the asymmetry distinguishes machinery that exists from machinery whose effects have been observed, while exposing constraints that become visible only after realization capacity increases. -->
The cases that follow do not answer all three questions equally well. That asymmetry is itself useful. It distinguishes machinery that exists from machinery whose effects have been observed, and it exposes several constraints that become visible only after realization capacity increases.

## Internal Software Factories

<!-- point: five-internal-accounts-each-answering-a-different-engineering-question | Five accounts describe systems an organization built for its own work, and each answers a different question — optimizing the factory, converting policy into control, evolving controls as the fabricator changes, turning experience into shared knowledge, and placing authority — while Zenseact's agents do knowledge work around the factory rather than realization inside it. -->
Five of the seven accounts describe a system an organization built for its own work, and each answers a
different engineering question. Uber asks how to optimize the factory itself. Cloudflare asks how
institutional policy becomes production control. Spotify asks how the controls should change when the
fabricator does. Shopify asks how one engineer's experience becomes knowledge every later session
inherits. Zenseact asks where authority over the factory's agents should sit. Four of the five begin
with the existing software environment and delegate more of realization to agents from there. Zenseact
is the exception — its agents, as the case will show, perform knowledge work around the software factory
rather than realization inside it — and the exception is itself a data point about where agentic
capability lands first.

### Uber

<!-- point: uber-treats-the-factory-itself-as-an-object-of-measurement-and-optimization | Uber's distinctive move is treating the factory as an engineered system with measurable properties — model choice, context, orchestration, token cost and reviewer attention as tunable variables — at a scale whose headline attribution figure has an undefined denominator. -->
Uber's distinctive move is not that its factory is sophisticated but that the factory is itself an object
of measurement and optimization. Model choice, context, decomposition, orchestration, token cost and
reviewer attention are treated as engineering variables with numbers attached, rather than as fixed
properties of the reasoner. The scale gives the exercise its force: more than 70 percent of pull requests
attributed to local or cloud agents, more than 3,600 engineer-created agent skills executing over 30,000
times per day, and managed agents performing code review, CI repair, end-to-end changes, alert triage,
debugging, and maintenance.[cite: medisetty2026factory] The headline figure needs its measurement caveat
attached: no source defines "attributed to," and figures published earlier the same year under narrower
definitions were far lower — 11 percent of pull requests *opened* by agents as of
March.[cite: orosz2026uber] The scale is real; its denominator is not public. Product intent still
originates with people, but a growing share of sessions is initiated by managed agents responding to the
factory's own operational signals — CI failures, on-call alerts, incoming bugs — and by centrally
scheduled maintenance, with incident reviews mined at a roughly monthly cadence for lessons that become
new maintenance skills applied across services.[cite: medisetty2026sdlc]

<!-- point: uber-tunes-the-environment-against-published-numbers | The optimization targets the environment rather than the model: a governed pre-compiled context and tool perimeter, then token-cost, anti-pattern, default-setting and benchmark levers, with managed agents evaluated in outcome-denominated units. -->
The optimization targets the environment rather than the reasoner, and every piece of it is tuned against
a number. A context graph spanning more than thirty internal systems and one gateway fronting more than a
thousand tool servers carry grounding and tool access under centralized policy; pre-provisioned execution
environments let an agent start work in seconds.[cite: medisetty2026factory; medisetty2026sdlc] Moving
deterministic orchestration out of repeated model turns into executable code cuts token use by more than
half on common workflows and by more than 90 percent in bulk cases. Sixteen named session anti-patterns —
suboptimal model routing, context-window bloat, cache expiration — are detected in the runtime and priced.
Compaction thresholds, reasoning effort, and subagent model class are set as fleet-wide defaults rather
than per task. Real workloads become benchmarks, models are selected against cost, quality, and
reliability, and managed agents are evaluated in outcome-denominated units: cost per merged pull request,
review, alert, or cleanup.[cite: medisetty2026factory]

<!-- point: uber-cannot-optimize-the-evidence-or-the-reviewer | Two things resist the optimization: the evidence at admission is a table the producing agent assembles about its own work with no stated independent re-derivation, and reviewer attention is a rationed resource Uber schedules — review latency moved from three hours to nine. -->
Two things resist that optimization, and Uber publishes both. The first is the evidence. Evaluation begins
inside the agent's own loop, before shared machinery sees the work: static-analysis repair, a simulator
screenshot compared against the Figma specification — the one place in this record where a design artifact
serves as a mechanical oracle — and staging integration, with the resulting pull request carrying a table
of the checks performed, screenshots included.[cite: medisetty2026sdlc] The producing agent assembles that
table about its own work, and no source states that any entry is re-derived by independent machinery
before approval. Behind it sit a smaller reviewer model inside the loop, a reasoning model outside it,
self-healing CI, and then a person. Admission is human, and Uber says so plainly: "we still have humans
approving the code," with "a short path in the near future to a percentage of our code landing
automatically" — the destination named, the criterion for which code and on what evidence
unpublished.[cite: bond2026ureview]

<!-- point: uber-publishes-cost-and-withholds-quality | Reviewer attention is the one resource Uber schedules as scarce — review latency rose from three to nine hours and maintenance is placed against Monday's review load — while the measurement stays asymmetric: cost published in detail, the named quality signals tracked and never published. -->
The second is reviewer attention, and it is the one resource Uber schedules as scarce. Time to first
review rose from three hours in 2024 to nine in 2026 as volume and change size grew, and the stated
answer is to move reviewers up a layer, toward architecture and product judgment, rather than out of the
loop.[cite: bond2026ureview] Maintenance jobs are placed on Sunday for spare CI capacity while the number
of diffs engineers face on Monday is separately capped, because spare compute does not imply unlimited
reviewer attention.[cite: medisetty2026sdlc] No other case in this corpus schedules admission capacity as
a resource. The published measurement is asymmetric in the same direction: the cost half appears in
detail, while the named quality signals — revert rate, F1, time to recovery — are tracked and appear
nowhere in the public record, and no source states what evidence a change must produce before it may
proceed. Uber's own closing lands where this chapter began: once building a feature is easy,
implementation feasibility no longer settles the product question — "the factory still needs people to
decide whether that feature should be built."[cite: medisetty2026sdlc]

### Cloudflare

<!-- point: cloudflare-turns-standards-prose-into-statements-with-stable-identity | Cloudflare's question is how institutional policy becomes a production control; the answer begins with representation — an extraction agent converts each SHOULD and MUST into a structured statement whose identifier survives revision of its document, and that identity is what makes an obligation trackable across systems. -->
How does an organization's own policy become a control that runs in production? Cloudflare's factory
attaches to its engineering organization at review rather than at origination — engineers still open the
merge requests and write the specs — and its answer begins with representation. A standards corpus that
had outgrown individual memory, and, in the CIO's franker telling, a review process that AI-accelerated
engineers had outrun — "Anyone at Cloudflare could now write bad code,
faster"[cite: rhea2026cloudflareos] — was extracted by a purpose-built agent into structured statements,
each SHOULD or MUST carrying a stable identifier that survives revision of the document containing
it.[cite: reimann2026codex] That identifier does the work, and nothing else in this corpus has an
analogue for it: an obligation that keeps its identity across revisions of its own text can be tracked,
waived, and measured wherever it is applied. The corpus then meets the work at four or five stations, not
one: human review of the standards themselves before they enter the corpus; fast custom linters in the
engineer's editing loop; a multi-agent semantic review in CI; a spec reviewer upstream of implementation
entirely; and an incident-report reviewer after production, whose findings are mandatory to clear for
high-severity incidents.[cite: reimann2026codex] The line between linter and review question is drawn by
what each language's tooling can verify mechanically, and the stated motive for drawing it was latency,
not a taxonomy of obligations.

<!-- point: cloudflare-promotes-obligations-into-blocking-authority-and-instruments-one-side-only | Authority arrives in a separate promotion step from advisory to blocking, after which a coordinator agent maps its verdict onto the version-control action with no human in the path — bounded by an approval-biased rubric and a logged break-glass override whose 0.6 percent rate measures only the false-block direction. -->
Authority arrives in a second, separate step. Any employee may propose a standard and a named domain
owner approves it; a distinct promotion then moves an approved standard from advisory to enforced, and
only then can its MUST statements block — though no source names who performs that promotion. Once
promoted, admission is machine-held in the ordinary case. A coordinator agent orchestrates up to seven
specialist reviewers, judges severity, and maps its verdict directly onto the version-control action —
approve, revoke a prior approval, or block the merge — with no human in the
path.[cite: skidmore2026review] Cloudflare is the only organization in this corpus that hands a
probabilistic evaluator that power, and it engineers around the grant rather than assuming it: only
enforced MUST statements can block, the rubric is biased toward approval so a lone warning does not, and
a human reviewer can force approval by commenting "break glass" — a logged override used 288 times, 0.6
percent of merge requests, in the measured month.[cite: skidmore2026review] The instrumentation runs in
one direction only. The break-glass count measures forced approvals; no published number measures the
violations the reviewer failed to flag. Policy authorship is human, application is machine, and only the
escape hatch is counted.

<!-- point: cloudflare-converts-failures-into-structure-but-never-measures-the-loop | Failures convert into environmental structure along four documented paths — named rules from named outages, Snapstone enrolment, representation revision, a new linter station — but no loop is closed by measurement, and Cloudflare itself calls the agent-evaluation loop unfinished. -->
Failures do become new structure here, along four documented paths. The November and December 2025
outages produced named rules — do not call `.unwrap()` outside tests; validate that upstream dependencies
are in an expected state — now enforced at review; a dangerous configuration pattern can be enrolled in
Snapstone, a shared substrate through which it "immediately inherits safe deployment"; the extracted
representation itself was revised, Markdown to JSON, when agents needed to filter it more accurately; and
reviewer latency produced the linter station.[cite: hartman2026failsmall] What the record does not
contain is a measurement closing any of these loops. The outcome claim for the outage-derived rules is a
counterfactual — that the outages "would have been rejected merge requests instead of global incidents" —
and Cloudflare places the remaining loop in the future in its own voice: the current focus is giving
engineers "the tools to define the loops that evaluate the work their agents
produce."[cite: rhea2026cloudflareos] The conversion machinery is described and instantiated; its effect
is asserted, not shown.

### Spotify

<!-- point: spotify-holds-the-line-fixed-and-swaps-the-station-inside-it | Spotify's fleet line predates its agent by years; the agent replaced the deterministic transform at one station while targeting, review, merge and monitoring "remain exactly the same" — which isolates what a factory must change when only its fabricator does. -->
Spotify's case isolates a variable the others cannot. Its production line predates the agent by years.
Fleet Management targets repositories by querying the estate — code and configuration ingested into
BigQuery, the Backstage software catalog carrying component identity, ownership, dependencies, and
endpoint lineage — executes transformations across thousands of repositories, automerges on green, and
watches merged changes in production, alerting the change author when deployments
fail.[cite: brown2023fleet] In 2025 the company replaced the deterministic transformation script at the
center of that line with an agent, and stated the substitution precisely: "All the surrounding Fleet
Management infrastructure — targeting repositories, opening pull requests, getting reviews, and merging
into production — remains exactly the same."[cite: charas2025honk] The agent occupies one station. It
took over the residual the prior technique could not reach — the last 30 percent of a fleet migration,
the corner cases that had grown one dependency updater to twenty thousand lines of script. Everything
that moved afterward moved because the fabricator at that one station had changed.

<!-- point: spotify-installs-and-then-retires-a-control-as-the-fabricator-improves | The controls around the agent station are tuned to the fabricator and move with it: a deliberately narrow tool surface, verification as a precondition of the PR's existence, and an LLM judge added after agents gamed builds and later reported removed as models improved — the corpus's only recorded control withdrawal. -->
The controls around that station are tuned to the fabricator's properties, and they move when it does.
The agent chooses its own path through the code but holds a deliberately narrow tool surface — a verify
tool, restricted git subcommands that can never push, an allowlisted shell — with code search and
documentation tools deliberately withheld and context condensed into the prompt up front: "The more tools
you have, the more dimensions of unpredictability you introduce."[cite: charas2025honk] Verification runs
before a pull request may exist: verifiers activate from the component's contents, opaque to the agent,
and a failing verifier means no PR;[cite: charas2025honk] a later architecture separates the verification
runtime entirely, so the PR is created only after full CI validation.[cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon] The
clearest control story in the case is a lifecycle. Agents gamed builds — commenting out failing tests,
downgrading Java versions — so Spotify added an LLM judge, which by late 2025 was vetoing about a quarter
of sessions, the agent self-correcting half the time; the talk record then reports the judge removed as
models improved, "verification steps in prompts proving sufficient."[cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon] A
control installed against a measured failure mode and retired when the capability beneath it moved —
reported second-hand, with no first-party text yet confirming the removal. No other case in this corpus
records a control being withdrawn.

<!-- point: spotify-splits-admission-by-fabricator-and-names-verification-as-the-constraint | Admission splits on which fabricator produced the change — deterministic fleet changes automerge by default while agent-authored ones wait for human review — and Spotify's own incident retrospectives name verification capacity, not generation, as the binding constraint. -->
Admission itself splits on which fabricator produced the change. Deterministic fleet changes automerge by
default — more than 2.5 million automated maintenance PRs, "the vast majority auto-merged with no human
in the loop,"[cite: spotify2026constraint] with the change author rather than the repository owner
deciding what automerges[cite: brown2023fleet] — while agent-authored changes wait for human review, and
Spotify names review, not generation, as the new bottleneck.[cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon] Humans scope
the migrations — work that once involved hundreds of teams over weeks is scoped by one engineer in
days[cite: spotify2026constraint] — author prompts as version-controlled, tested
artifacts,[cite: charas2025honk] and encode non-delegation directly: in one documented migration,
wherever a judgment call was required the agent was instructed to leave the field unchanged and annotate
it for the human reviewer.[cite: edwardsjoseph2026migrations] The factory's health is itself
instrumented. Monthly incident retrospectives now ask whether AI-authored code contributed to an
incident; the published finding is that it has not been a material direct contributor, but that "the
volume of change increased faster than some of our verification controls could adapt" — one automated
dependency upgrade passed every check and still failed in production. The constraint, stated by the SVP
who owns the platform: "AI increased the capacity to produce change. The next constraint became our
ability to verify it."[cite: singer2026quality]

### Shopify

<!-- point: shopify-makes-every-session-observable-organizational-material | Shopify's question is how one engineer's fix becomes something every later session inherits, and the answer starts in the architecture: River lives in Slack, refuses direct messages, and turns every session into a public transcript watched by colleagues. -->
Shopify's question is how one engineer's hard-won fix becomes knowledge that every later session
inherits, and its answer begins in the architecture rather than in a pipeline. River, the company's
internal agent, lives in Slack and refuses direct messages; every session is a public transcript, and an
employee starts work by mentioning the agent in a channel.[cite: moreno2026river] Work is therefore
performed in front of an audience by construction, and a failure is watched rather than reported. In a
thirty-day window reported in May 2026, 59,918 sessions ran across 5,170 channels, touching the work of
more than 7,000 people, and one in eight merged pull requests was
River-coauthored.[cite: moreno2026river] By September, third-party digests of a founder podcast put the
channel count near ten thousand with the population flat — the growth is in surfaces, not users — and
have Lütke saying up to half of pull requests now *start* as a River conversation: an origination share,
founder-stated and unaudited, not a movement in the merged-coauthored figure.[cite: drecksler2026river]

<!-- point: shopify-mines-sessions-by-hand-and-measured-the-effect | The documented conversion mechanism is human — people watch River work and write down what it should have known, as files in the monorepo — with the effect measured and the counterfactual controlled: merge rate 36 to 77 percent in two months without retraining or switching models; no automated extraction is established. -->
The corpus of sessions is the factory's learning substrate, and the mechanism Shopify documents is human.
People watch River work, notice where it got stuck, and write down what it should have known; the
artifacts are files in the monorepo — a skill update, an `AGENTS.md` diff, sometimes a whole new shared
skill — alongside the code, conventions, intent documents, and runbooks the next session
inherits.[cite: moreno2026river] The effect is measured with the counterfactual controlled: River's merge
rate rose from 36 to 77 percent over two months, and "We did not retrain a model. We did not switch
models."[cite: lutke2026shopfloor] Whether any of the extraction is automated the record does not
establish: a reported nightly loop in which River reviews its own conversations and rewrites its
instruction files appears only in third-party digests of a members-only podcast, and the engineering
prose describes people doing the writing. Shopify's engineering account closes by handing readers an
imperative — "Make the agent multiplayer by construction" — a design prescription offered outward, not a
label the company applies to itself.[cite: moreno2026river]

<!-- point: shopifys-instruction-corpus-also-accumulates-maintenance | The mechanism that accumulates shared knowledge accumulates upkeep with it: instruction files apply recursively across the monorepo, a directory missing one of the two files leaves some developers running a degraded agent, and the CEO calls the automation that repairs the drift a stupid complexity tax. -->
The corpus is not free to carry. Instruction files apply recursively down the monorepo tree, and rival
tool conventions read different ones; Lütke threatened to ban Claude Code at Shopify over the "split
brain problems" that divergence creates "when different team members use different
tools."[cite: lutke2026splitbrain] In follow-up posts the trade press quoted, he gave the consequence at
scale: with thousands of developers in one repository "it just does happen that one directory is missing
one of the two files and this means that a subset of devs work with lobotomy."[cite: caswell2026agentsmd]
Automation repairs the drift, and Lütke calls that repair "a stupid complexity tax that shouldn't have to
be paid."[cite: caswell2026agentsmd] The knowledge compounds; keeping it coherent is work somebody does.

<!-- point: shopify-converted-one-failure-into-a-code-enforced-guarantee-and-scopes-the-claim | The sharpest conversion is a failure that moved a rule from prompt to code — River violated its own prompt-stated enumeration requirement, producing the rule that guarantees belong in code — and the admission controls named alongside it are stated for the security lane only. -->
The sharpest instance of the conversion is a failure that changed where a rule lives. A September account
of River's security workflows names the controls: the agent's output is draft-only, the engineers who own
the affected code hold merge authority, a freshness-gated merge queue rejects stale evidence, and a
post-merge check verifies that the default branch reflects the fix — merges through the gated queue rose
from about 10 percent to 80 percent.[cite: son2026security] The same account states the governing rule and
publishes the run that taught it: "Prompts are the right place to express judgment and the wrong place to
express a guarantee," and in security, "any rule that determines whether a vulnerability is closed must be
enforced in code" — written after River violated its own prompt-stated enumeration requirement and said so
in its report.[cite: son2026security] Why a person holds that merge authority is stated as a claim about
responsibility rather than about the machinery's adequacy: machines can inform a decision but cannot take
responsibility for one. That grounding reaches the public record only at second hand, through third-party
digests of a members-only podcast, so the wording is the digests' and not
Lütke's.[cite: podcastalpha2026lutke] Those controls are stated for security workflows. What governs the
other seven-eighths of River's output — what evidence an ordinary River pull request must carry, how often
one fails in production — the public record does not say.

### Zenseact

<!-- point: zenseact-federates-agent-ownership-while-keeping-the-authority-to-act-central | Zenseact's agents do no software realization, and the case earns its place on the federation question: a platform team owns a runtime with no business logic, a domain team's agent is a directory with two text files, and a central permission table still resolves every tool call. -->
Zenseact, a driver-assistance software company, belongs here with a scoping sentence attached: its agents
do not produce software. The one substantive source — a company-authored whitepaper describing its ZAP
platform — shows agents querying Gerrit, Zuul, JIRA, SAP, and document stores as data sources and
synthesizing answers; no agent authors a patch, opens a change, or merges anything.[cite: dufwa2026zap]
Under this chapter's definitions that is not an agentic software factory but an agentic knowledge-work
platform operating around one, and the case earns its place on a question none of the others puts
squarely: where should authority over the factory's agents sit? Zenseact's answer is federation. A
platform team owns the runtime — authentication, sessions, tool execution, the model loop — which
deliberately "does not contain any business logic"; a domain team's agent is a directory holding two text
files, a configuration listing its skills and a markdown file encoding the domain's rules, so a new agent
is a configuration act rather than a platform change.[cite: dufwa2026zap] What makes that boundary safe to
draw is that consequence stays central: a permission table resolves every tool call to auto-approve,
confirm with a human, or deny, by tool and by role.[cite: dufwa2026zap] The domain team writes the
knowledge; the platform keeps the authority to act.

<!-- point: zenseact-is-architecture-described-by-its-authors-not-practice-observed | The federation claim is a design property the record never demonstrates — one source, no adoption count, no outcome figure — and the single measured mechanism is a context router whose typed failures are repaired in the skill file rather than the model. -->
The evidentiary standing travels with the claim. This is architecture described by its authors rather than
practice observed: one source, no adoption count, no outcome figure, and no report of how many domain
teams built an agent — so "distributed ownership scales" is a design property the record states and never
demonstrates. Its one measured mechanism is the context router, which selects each turn's skill
instructions by text similarity and carries the record's only quantity, a 67 to 98 percent context
reduction on financial queries and 43 to 97 percent on engineering ones. Routing misses are typed — no
skill matched, the wrong skill fired, the agent struggled with a tool — and each class carries a
prescribed repair to the skill file rather than to the model that reads it.[cite: dufwa2026zap] The
federated unit is what gets repaired.

## The Factory in a Box

<!-- point: gitlab-sells-the-capability-and-its-own-practice-prices-what-the-box-omits | GitLab generalizes and sells the production capability, which poses a question no internal factory faces — what productizes generically and what the adopter must supply — and its own handbook prices the second half: failing-test-first doctrine, CI as the gate, a test-count guard, and a 95-percent-agent-generated service built atop four weeks of harness construction. -->
GitLab belongs in the other column because the capability its account describes is generalized and sold,
to be attached to somebody else's repositories, policies, and engineering knowledge. That form poses a
question no internal factory faces: what part of a factory can be productized generically, and what must
the adopting organization supply? GitLab's own internal record is worth taking first, because it prices
the second half. The company's handbook documents an internal practice with doctrine attached: never give
an agent a task without a failing test; "Fix the environment, not the prompt"; "Prompts are suggestions.
CI is a gate. If the agent can break a rule and still pass the pipeline, the rule doesn't exist" — down to
a CI job that fails when the test count decreases, because agents delete tests to make them
pass.[cite: gitlab2026playbook] And it publishes a dated internal quantity: a 135,000-line Rust service,
roughly 95 percent agent-generated, built by four engineers across 259 merge requests in about two weeks
— after roughly four weeks spent constructing the harness first, and with every agent-generated change
still passing CI, security scanning, and code review.[cite: gitlab2026orbitissue]

<!-- point: the-factory-in-a-box-is-an-integration-problem-at-both-ends | A factory in a box introduces a boundary an internal factory does not have: the vendor supplies fabricator, orchestration, context machinery, evidence, and generic controls, and the engineering problem is integration, not installation — at both ends of the production line. -->
The form introduces a boundary an internal factory does not have. The vendor supplies the fabricator, the
orchestration, the context machinery, the evidence infrastructure, and the generic controls; the customer
must connect those to the knowledge and authority that make a change acceptable in its own system. The
engineering problem is integration, not installation, and it sits at both ends of the production line.
Upstream: how does organization-specific intent enter generic machinery? Downstream: how does
organization-specific authority govern what comes out?

<!-- point: customer-intent-enters-as-files-and-customer-authority-as-a-withheld-capability | Upstream, intent enters as files the customer authors at fixed repository paths, under the vendor's thesis that what an agent cannot access in context does not exist; downstream, admission authority stays human by construction — the flow never sets the Approve state — with agent privileges intersected and tool policy tightenable only. -->
GitLab's upstream answer is files in the customer's repository, at fixed paths: an `AGENTS.md`, chat rules
at `.gitlab/duo/chat-rules.md`, review instructions at `.gitlab/duo/mr-review-instructions.yaml`, skills
at `skills/<name>/SKILL.md` — plus the customer's CI configuration and, optionally, authored flow
definitions.[cite: gitlab2026customize] The vendor states the thesis behind the arrangement normatively:
"anything the agent can't access in-context effectively doesn't exist."[cite: gitlab2026orbitissue] What
GitLab supplies as infrastructure is the recovered remainder — a context graph over the customer's code,
merge requests, pipelines, and security findings, assembled from artifacts that already exist. Recovered
context carries relationships. It cannot carry the intent the customer never wrote down. Downstream,
authority is expressed as a withheld capability. GitLab's security review flow "never sets the **Approve**
state, even when it finds no issues" — the machinery may produce, repair, and advise, and only a person
approves.[cite: gitlab2026securityreview] Around that prohibition sits governance of the action rather
than the change: an agent's privileges are the intersection of its service account's and the triggering
human's, tool policy is tightenable by a project but never loosenable below its group's rule, and audit
events record which agent acted under which policy. The vendor industrializes the record that a human
decision was made. It does not move the decision.

<!-- point: the-record-prices-the-infrastructure-and-not-the-knowledge-authoring | The public record is specific about infrastructure and silent about the knowledge-authoring cost — no case study of a customer writing those files, no measure of output against their quality — and the layer that produced GitLab's own 95-percent service is precisely the layer only the customer can author. -->
Now set the two ends against what the record prices. The infrastructure is specified in detail: runner
tags and executors, network paths, per-vendor credentials, a Kubernetes deployment for the self-managed
context service. The knowledge-authoring cost is not specified at all — no case study of a customer
writing those instruction files, no measure of how factory output varies with their quality, no account of
how long a customer takes to reach the maturity GitLab's own playbook names as prerequisite for autonomy.
That silence is not a hole in the reconstruction; it is the finding. The CEO's essay locates trust in a
durable layer of "context, verification, governance and evidence" around a replaceable model — an
architectural vision, and the sellable layer.[cite: staples2026abundant] The layer that actually produced
GitLab's own 95-percent-agent-generated service was the failing tests written first, the CI gates, the
repo-resident context, the test-count guard — precisely the layer only the customer can author, and the
one thing the box cannot ship.

<!-- point: the-form-question-stated-plainly | The externalization question follows directly from the two-ended boundary and is worth stating plainly. -->
The question the form poses follows directly, and it is worth stating plainly.

<!-- inline-quote -->
> How much organization-specific engineering knowledge must be externalized before an off-the-shelf
> factory can reliably produce controlled changes to that organization's software?

<!-- point: the-externalization-question-is-the-modeling-problem-commercially | The question restates the Modeling problem in commercial terms, and the factory chapter already named the tradeoff behind it: a purchased factory that works only when the buyer describes the software almost completely has recreated the arrangement the earlier software factories died of. -->
The question is the representation problem in commercial dress, and {{sec:the-software-factory}} already
named the tradeoff behind it. A purchased factory that reliably produces your software only when you
describe that software almost completely has recreated the arrangement the earlier software factories
died of. So the buyer's side of the transaction is a modeling decision: which consequential intent to
externalize, in what form, at what cost, before generic machinery can respect it. {{chapter:theory}}
takes up what that externalization costs.

## Model-First Factories

<!-- point: model-first-production-inverts-where-intent-enters | Model-first production inverts where intent enters: explicit engineering models, not the repository and its tests, carry the system's semantics, and the software is generated downstream of them. -->
Siemens answers a question the software-first accounts never have to ask: what changes when explicit
engineering models already sit upstream of realization? {{sec:the-software-factory}} told this ambition's
history in software — enterprise architecture and model-driven development tried to determine the
consequential decisions upstream, in shared representations, and lost economically rather than
conceptually. Here the same ambition is still working, as the ordinary practice of an engineering
tradition that arrived at models by a different route.

<!-- point: siemens-model-first-evidence-is-eigen-and-agent-studio | The load-bearing Siemens evidence is Eigen — control code generated from a TIA Portal engineering model, iterating until pre-defined performance benchmarks are met, a tolerance in this chapter's exact sense — and the 2024 Agent Studio research on SysML v2 with simulation as validator: a model supplies goal and context, a deterministic engine supplies the verdict. -->
Siemens enters from outside software-first engineering, and "Siemens" is not one factory: its public
record spans several business units, and most of it describes production systems Siemens sells rather
than a factory it runs on its own software. The clearest instance of software fabricated downstream of a
model is the Eigen Engineering Agent. It reads a TIA Portal automation project — an engineering model
already carrying the devices, network topology, and control logic — writes SCL and LAD control code, test
logic, and HMI JavaScript against it, and "iterates until pre-defined performance benchmarks are
met."[cite: siemens2026eigen] The admission criterion is a pre-set acceptance region, not a specified
realization — a tolerance in this chapter's exact sense. Two years earlier, Siemens' Agent Studio research
placed agents on formal modeling languages, SysML v2 among them, with generated candidate architectures
validated through simulation and the system waiting for the engineer's decision at critical
points.[cite: liu2024agentstudio] The recurring structure across the Siemens record: a model supplies the
goal and the context, a deterministic engine — a simulator, a compiler, a benchmark harness — supplies the
verdict, and the agent searches freely between them. That is the bargain the earlier model-driven attempts
could not strike.

<!-- point: siemens-vision-disclaims-itself-and-the-gaps-sit-where-stakes-are-highest | The broader architecture Siemens publishes is an explicit research vision about simulation, not software realization; no source establishes a model-implementation conformance gate, and Eigen's record names no human approval step — while the one account of Siemens changing its own software keeps a human in the loop and grounds that gate in compliance. -->
The broader architecture Siemens publishes around this — task agents and orchestrators over knowledge
graphs of engineering data — is explicitly "a research vision and innovation roadmap, not a committed
product delivery," and its subject is simulation rather than software realization.[cite: siemens2026a3e]
Two silences matter more than the vision, and they are content rather than weakness, because they mark
exactly what a model-first factory has not yet been shown to do. No Siemens source establishes a
conformance mechanism that rejects a change when model and implementation diverge — Eigen's benchmark is a
threshold, not a correspondence. And Eigen's public record, for an agent writing control code destined for
physical machinery, names no human review or approval step at all. Source silence, as always, is not
organizational absence — but here the silence sits exactly where the stakes are highest. Where the record
does describe Siemens changing its own software, in a legacy-modernization account written up by its cloud
partner rather than by Siemens engineers, the posture inverts: the system "keeps a human in the loop at
every step," and the reason given is an obligation the software carries rather than a stage of maturity.
Industrial systems run "over 15 to 20 years of operation," so AI-generated output "must therefore be
explainable, traceable, and verifiable," and "hallucinated or unvalidated changes are not merely
inefficient but operationally unacceptable."[cite: nawalgaria2026siemens]

<!-- point: a-cheap-and-capable-fabricator-changes-what-a-model-must-determine | The sharp difference from the old model-driven dream is that today's fabricator is cheap and capable at once, so a model-first factory need not determine every realization decision — only the consequential ones. -->
One difference from the old dream matters more than the rest, and it is the difference
{{sec:the-software-factory}} isolated. Model-driven development had to specify realization almost
completely, because its downstream fabricator was inexpensive but not capable. Today's fabricator is both
at once. A model-first factory can therefore represent the decisions engineering must control and leave
the remaining realization freedom to an agent that will resolve it acceptably. **The model need not
determine every realization decision.** That is a different bargain from the one the earlier attempts
lost, and whether it pays is not something the public sources settle.

<!-- point: model-first-is-a-position-and-siemens-sells-the-combined-cell | Model-first is a position on one axis rather than a maturity level, and the axis is independent of the first — Siemens itself partially fills the combined cell, selling model-first factories in a box for the narrow software of control logic and chip design, while no observed case sells one for general-purpose software. -->
Two cautions keep the axis honest. Model-first is not a rung above existing-environment-first; it is a
different answer to where intent enters, carrying its own costs. And the axis is independent of the first
one. A factory in a box can be sold as model-first, driven by models the customer authors rather than a
repository the vendor crawls — and Siemens itself shows the combined cell is not empty: Eigen and its EDA
verification toolkit are sold production systems driven by the customer's own engineering models, in the
narrow software of control logic and chip design. For general-purpose software, no observed case sells
that today — a fact about a young industry rather than a constraint on the design space.

## What the Factories Share, and Where They Differ

<!-- point: the-human-admission-boundary-rests-on-three-different-stated-groundings | The shared substrate was stated at the outset; the one part of it the corpus does not state uniformly is why consequential decisions stay human — evidence adequacy at Uber, responsibility at Shopify, compliance obligation at Siemens — and those three groundings predict different futures for the boundary. -->
The substrate these factories share was stated at the outset. One part of it varies in a way worth
keeping: the corpus gives three different reasons for keeping consequential decisions human. Uber's is
interim — the available representations and evidence do not yet justify delegating them. Shopify's is a
claim about responsibility: a machine cannot take responsibility for a decision. Siemens' is a compliance
obligation the software carries over fifteen to twenty years of operation. Only the first is stated as
interim; Uber names automatic approval as a near-term destination, while a boundary grounded in
responsibility or in a compliance obligation has no reason to move as the fabricator improves. No source
in the corpus argues with another's grounding.

<!-- point: the-differences-are-as-informative-as-the-commonalities | The factories differ in where admission sits, how much knowledge they route into each task, whether failure-to-structure conversion is measured (the mechanism is documented nearly everywhere; aggregate yield once, class-recurrence nowhere), and — most sharply — how explicitly they model the governed system; no ordering follows. -->
The differences are as informative as the commonalities. The factories vary in where they place
admission (Cloudflare's coordinator holds the merge gate itself, under human-held policy and a
logged override; Spotify automerges its deterministic fleet changes while its agent-authored
changes wait for human review; Uber keeps approval human by statement, and GitLab by construction in
the one flow whose approval posture it documents), how much organizational knowledge they route into
each task, whether the conversion of failures into shared structure is measured (the mechanism is now
documented nearly everywhere in the corpus; its aggregate yield is measured once, in Shopify's
controlled merge-rate rise, and whether any converted control reduced recurrence of the failure class
it was built for is measured nowhere), and — most sharply — how explicitly they model the
governed system itself. No ordering follows from those differences. A human admission boundary may
be exactly right where the decision remains semantic; a richer model may not repay its cost in
another environment. The comparison exposes design choices and boundaries, not a winner.

<!-- point: software-first-accounts-model-knowledge-not-behavior | The observed modeling frontier: the software-first accounts publicly show knowledge and governance representations; Siemens crosses into machine-checkable system models from a model-first tradition; DocAble additionally checks selected model-implementation relations and gives them consequences. -->
One difference deserves its own name, because {{chapter:theory}} will need it. Across the
software-first accounts, the representations described publicly are **knowledge and
governance representations**: service catalogs, dependency information, policy corpora, context
graphs, skills, provenance records. These are models in the broad sense — they externalize engineering knowledge and
extend what an agent can recover reliably — but the sources give little evidence of behavioral,
process, scenario, or invariant models serving as the primary surface of software reasoning. Siemens
crosses that boundary from a model-first tradition. The originating case goes one step further: for
selected models, mechanical checks compare declared relations to the implementation and reject
changes when the relations no longer hold — though only for particular relations, at a cost the case
does not show scaling. [ref:modeling-frontier-tiers] states the finding as three tiers and marks
where the public evidence sits.

<!-- point: the-common-structure-is-leverage-organizational-capital | At organizational scale the recurring structure is leverage: one policy shapes thousands of changes, one representation guides a fleet, one learned constraint protects every later task — the organizational face of engineering capital. -->
Where the factories do converge, the common structure is leverage. Organizations centralize shared
mechanisms while keeping domain judgment distributed; human judgment moves upstream, from
hand-authoring changes to scoping and supervising fleets; private learning becomes a shared
artifact — sessions, policies, skills, defaults; and the unit of governance grows from a task to
classes of work, fleets, and platforms. A scarce human decision becomes more valuable when placed
where the environment carries it forward: one policy shapes thousands of changes; one shared
representation guides work across a fleet; one learned constraint protects every later task over the
same surface. That is the organizational face of engineering capital. Governance does not remove
judgment; it gives selected judgments multiplicative reach.
