# Other Agentic Engineering Factories

<!-- point: one-system-one-engineer-one-problem-so-how-do-others-do-it | The longitudinal DocAble case is compared with organizations possessing different teams, processes, institutional knowledge and obligations; the four factory questions organize the comparison. -->

DocAble provides a longitudinal account of one engineer developing and operating an agentic software factory. Other organizations begin with established engineering teams, production processes, and accumulated institutional knowledge. How have they adapted their factories to accommodate agentic implementation?

<!-- point: the-public-sources-are-interested-accounts-read-conservatively | Public reports, talks, and documentation provide seven independently developed arrangements interpreted through the four factory questions. -->

The four questions from {{sec:the-software-factory}} provide a common basis for comparison: what the factory produces, what people do, how production is organized, and what tolerances the resulting software must satisfy. I use public engineering reports, talks, and product documentation to examine seven independently developed factories through these questions. Each illustrates a different aspect of factory engineering ([ref:agentic-engineering-factories]).[^1]

## Cloudflare

Cloudflare operates a global network that provides security, performance, and connectivity services for Internet applications. Its engineers develop the software and configuration that operate this network, including services whose failures can affect customers worldwide. The factory spans many teams and repositories, with shared engineering standards governing changes across them. As coding agents take on more work, Cloudflare has begun translating those standards into representations and checks that can govern both human- and agent-authored changes [cite: reimann2026codex]. Its case shows how organizational engineering knowledge can become enforceable control over a distributed production process.

<!-- point: cloudflare-turns-standards-prose-into-statements-with-stable-identity | Cloudflare represents institutional obligations as individually identified statements that can be tracked through revision and applied at several stages of engineering work. -->

Cloudflare’s standards corpus had grown beyond what individual engineers could readily remember. An extraction agent turns each SHOULD or MUST into a structured statement with a stable identifier, allowing the obligation to be tracked as its containing document changes [cite: reimann2026codex]. That identity supports compliance records and explicit waivers. The standards inform work at several stages: specification review before implementation, linters during editing, semantic review in CI, and incident review after production. The structured representation makes the standards available to these mechanisms without requiring each engineer or agent to reconstruct the relevant obligations from policy documents.

<!-- point: cloudflare-promotes-obligations-into-blocking-authority-and-instruments-one-side-only | Standards approval and enforcement promotion are separate; enforced MUST statements can drive merge decisions, with a recorded human override. -->

Cloudflare distinguishes the adoption of a standard from its enforcement. Any employee may propose a standard, and a named domain owner approves it. A separate promotion moves it from advisory to enforced. For enforced standards, a coordinator agent orchestrates up to seven specialist reviewers, evaluates severity, and can approve a merge request, revoke an earlier approval, or block the merge. Only enforced MUST statements can block a merge, and the review rubric favors approval so that a lone warning does not suffice to block it [cite: skidmore2026review].

Human reviewers retain an exception path: commenting “break glass” forces approval and records the override. Cloudflare reports 288 such overrides, representing 0.6 percent of merge requests, from March 10 to April 9, 2026 [cite: skidmore2026review]. The override records make the exercise of human authority observable. Engineers can examine when and why people overrode the automated review.

<!-- point: cloudflare-converts-failures-into-structure-but-never-measures-the-loop | Operating experience changed the representation of extracted standards and moved suitable checks into deterministic linters; engineering knowledge becomes more usable and cheaper to enforce. -->

Cloudflare has also improved the machinery through which its standards govern production. The extracted statements moved from Markdown to structured JSON so agents could identify and filter obligations more reliably. Semantic-review latency encouraged moving suitable checks into deterministic linters earlier in the workflow [cite: reimann2026codex]. These changes illustrate two ways to improve a factory: make engineering knowledge easier for agents and tools to consume, and move recurring judgments into cheaper, more predictable enforcement mechanisms. Cloudflare’s experience parallels DocAble’s governance conversion, although its engineering knowledge originates across many teams rather than with one engineer directing agents.

## Spotify

<!-- point: spotify-holds-the-line-fixed-and-swaps-the-station-inside-it | Spotify introduced agents into a pre-existing fleet-change process, reusing its production and verification machinery. -->

Spotify operates a music and audio streaming service supported by many independently developed software components. Its engineers maintain services, libraries, and data infrastructure that must evolve together as shared interfaces and dependencies change. To coordinate changes across repositories, Spotify developed a Fleet Management system that selects targets, applies transformations, creates pull requests, and monitors deployments [cite: brown2023fleet]. Coding agents were subsequently introduced into this existing production process, taking on changes that were difficult to automate deterministically [cite: charas2025honk]. In one fleet migration, an agent addressed the remaining 30 percent that scripted transformations had not covered [cite: charas2025honk]. Fleet Management continued to monitor deployments and alert the change author when production failed [cite: brown2023fleet]. Spotify’s case shows how an established software factory can incorporate a new fabricator without replacing the surrounding machinery.

<!-- point: spotify-installs-and-then-retires-a-control-as-the-fabricator-improves | Bounded tools and mandatory checks govern proposed changes; an added judge and its reported later removal show controls changing with observed failures and false vetoes. -->

Spotify bounded both the agent’s actions and its route to a pull request. Its late-2025 configuration exposed selected Git commands that excluded pushing, shell commands from an allowlist, and a verification tool. Users supplied relevant context in prompts; code-search and documentation tools were withheld [cite: charas2025honk]. The verification tool selected checks from repository contents, and failure prevented a pull request from being opened. The agent could choose how to implement a change within those bounds, but could not waive the required checks. After unwanted changes such as disabled tests, Spotify added an LLM judge that compared the diff with the original request. In December 2025, the company reported vetoes in about a quarter of thousands of sessions, with agents correcting their work after about half of those vetoes [cite: charas2025feedback].

Spotify subsequently separated the agent runtime from the verification runtime, creating pull requests only after CI checks [cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon]. The LLM judge sometimes rejected valid changes and was later removed as foundation models improved and verification instructions in prompts proved sufficient for its role [cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon]. This is an interesting example of the changing economics of governance. A control introduced to address the behavior of one generation of agents became less useful with the next. Maintaining the factory includes recognizing when a control has outlived its purpose.

<!-- point: spotify-splits-admission-by-fabricator-and-names-verification-as-the-constraint | People set migration scope, maintain prompts, reserve judgment calls, and review agent-authored changes; delegation varies within the production process. -->

Engineers retain responsibility for defining migration scope and maintaining prompts as tested, version-controlled artifacts. They can also reserve particular decisions. In one migration, the agent was instructed to leave fields unchanged and annotate them whenever a judgment call was needed [cite: charas2025honk;edwardsjoseph2026migrations]. Human review of agent-authored changes remained part of the production process in the reported configuration [cite: curtis2026spotifyqcon]. The engineers could delegate different portions of a migration according to the nature of the decisions involved.

<!-- point: spotify-measures-the-health-of-the-engineering-system-not-just-agent-activity | Rework, complexity, pull-request size, and incident observations provide a mixed account of system health and verification capacity rather than an activity-only success claim. -->

Increasing implementation capacity also placed new demands on Spotify’s verification machinery. Its refined rework measure did not rise during the AI ramp-up, while code complexity and pull-request size did; the company kept its thresholds unchanged. Incident retrospectives found no material direct contribution from AI-authored code, yet change volume had grown faster than some verification controls could adapt. One automated dependency upgrade passed its checks and still failed in production [cite: singer2026quality]. Producing more changes had exposed a capacity problem as well as a check-coverage problem.

## Shopify

Shopify develops a commerce platform through which merchants operate online stores, manage transactions, and run their businesses. Much of its software development occurs in a shared codebase, where changes must coexist with established conventions, services, and security requirements. Shopify has introduced coding agents into this environment, including an internal agent called River that employees invoke through shared Slack channels [cite: moreno2026river]. Shopify’s case shows how a factory can accumulate engineering knowledge through the observation of agentic work.

<!-- point: shopify-makes-every-session-observable-organizational-material | Public River sessions let colleagues inspect work and failures and record useful knowledge for subsequent sessions. -->

River refuses direct messages. An employee starts work by mentioning it in a channel, leaving a public transcript that colleagues can inspect [cite: moreno2026river]. This design makes the agent’s work and its difficulties visible to people other than the employee who initiated it.

<!-- point: shopify-mines-sessions-by-hand-and-measured-the-effect | People record missing knowledge in shared files; the reported merge-rate improvement occurred with the foundation model held fixed. -->

People use that visibility to improve the engineering environment. They watch River work, identify what it should have known, and add a skill, revise an `AGENTS.md` file, or record another reusable instruction in the monorepo [cite: moreno2026river]. These artifacts sit alongside code, conventions, intent documents, and runbooks. During this period of improvement, River’s reported merge rate rose from 36 to 77 percent over two months without a change in its foundation model [cite: lutke2026shopfloor]. Unlike DocAble, where I generally identified the lessons that warranted preservation, Shopify has organized this activity across an engineering community.

<!-- point: shopifys-instruction-corpus-also-accumulates-maintenance | Shared instructions require upkeep when different agent tools consume different files; repairing that divergence is part of maintaining the engineering environment. -->

The shared knowledge requires maintenance. Instruction files apply through the monorepo’s directory hierarchy, while different agent tools read different files. Shopify encountered divergence between those conventions: a directory could contain instructions for one tool and omit the corresponding file for another, so engineers working on the same code supplied different context to their agents [cite: lutke2026splitbrain;caswell2026agentsmd]. Automation repairs that drift. Recording engineering knowledge in reusable instructions creates a correspondence problem of its own.

<!-- point: shopify-converted-one-failure-into-a-code-enforced-guarantee-and-scopes-the-claim | A failed enumeration requirement motivates code enforcement of closure rules; the security workflow retains human merge authority, fresh evidence, and post-merge verification. -->

Some obligations warrant stronger machinery than instructions. In Shopify’s security-remediation workflow, River violated a prompt-stated enumeration requirement and acknowledged the omission in its report. Shopify draws the lesson that rules governing vulnerability closure must be enforced in code. Agents produce draft changes, the engineers responsible for the affected code retain merge authority, a merge queue requires fresh evidence, and River checks after dependency merges that the default branch no longer uses the vulnerable dependency. Shopify reports that use of the merge queue in this workflow rose from about 10 to 80 percent [cite: son2026security].

These two aspects of Shopify’s factory illustrate different responses to agentic failures. An engineer who notices missing knowledge can make it available to subsequent agents. An engineer who identifies an obligation that must hold can establish a mechanism to enforce it. The organization benefits from making both kinds of engineering work part of its ordinary production process.

## Uber

<!-- point: uber-treats-the-factory-itself-as-an-object-of-measurement-and-optimization | Uber treats foundation-model choice, context, orchestration, workflow cost, and reviewer attention as measurable engineering variables. -->

Uber operates a platform for transportation and delivery, supported by software services that coordinate users, providers, transactions, and real-time operations. Its engineering organization must continually maintain and change this large production system. Uber has introduced managed agents to perform development and operational tasks, including CI repair, code review, alert triage, and maintenance. These agents can receive work from people, schedules, or operational signals [cite: medisetty2026sdlc]. Uber’s case illustrates the engineering economics of operating an agentic software factory at scale.

<!-- point: uber-cannot-optimize-the-evidence-or-the-reviewer | Uber describes producer-assembled evidence followed by additional review, CI, and human approval, while exploring partial automatic approval. -->

Evaluation begins inside the producing agent’s loop, with static-analysis repair, simulator screenshots compared with design specifications, and staging integration. The resulting pull request carries a table of checks and evidence assembled by that agent [cite: medisetty2026sdlc]. Additional model-based review, CI, and human approval follow. Uber is also exploring partial automatic approval [cite: bond2026ureview].

<!-- point: uber-publishes-cost-and-withholds-quality | Review latency exposes finite reviewer capacity; rising fabrication capacity increases demands on supervision and the surrounding production process. -->

Reviewer attention has become a production constraint. Time to first review rose from three hours in 2024 to nine in 2026 as volume and change size grew [cite: bond2026ureview]. Like DocAble, Uber must organize supervision around its implementation capacity. DocAble responded by moving recurring checks into machinery; Uber also manages the flow of work into human review. Increasing fabrication capacity changes the demands on the surrounding production process.

<!-- point: uber-tunes-the-environment-against-published-numbers | Context delivery, orchestration, foundation-model selection, and outcome-denominated costs make the agent's surrounding production system an object of optimization. -->

Uber evaluates its factory in units of completed work, such as cost per merged pull request, review, alert, or cleanup. These measures connect resource expenditure to production outcomes rather than individual agent operations. Uber adjusts context delivery, tool access, and coordination against those production costs. Real workloads become benchmarks, and foundation models are selected against cost and reliability. Moving deterministic orchestration from repeated agent turns into executable code reportedly cut token use by more than half for common workflows and by more than 90 percent for bulk cases [cite: medisetty2026factory;medisetty2026sdlc]. Some work is cheaper when executed directly than when repeatedly reconstructed through model interactions. The organization tracks revert rate, F1, and time to recovery alongside production cost.

Uber also schedules maintenance jobs on Sunday to use spare CI capacity, while separately limiting the diffs engineers must review on Monday [cite: medisetty2026sdlc]. This is factory engineering in a particularly recognizable form: scheduling production to make economical use of machinery while accounting for the capacity of the people who evaluate its output.

## Amazon

Amazon develops software for commerce and cloud computing, including Amazon Web Services (AWS), whose products depend on large, continuously evolving software systems. Its engineering organizations maintain their own development processes, while AWS also offers Kiro, an agentic development environment that packages specification, implementation, and verification workflows for reuse [cite: kiro2026specs]. Three aspects of Amazon’s experience are of interest here: the production workflow supplied by Kiro, its use in rebuilding part of an AWS inference system, and the machinery Kiro’s developers use to improve the tool itself.

<!-- point: kiro-builds-engineering-artifacts-into-the-harness | Kiro supplies a reviewable specification workflow that adopting teams connect to their own repositories, tools, and engineering decisions. -->

Kiro supplies a reusable workflow for making intent explicit before implementation. A feature specification records user stories and acceptance criteria, develops an architectural design, and divides implementation into tasks that engineers can review [cite: kiro2026specs]. In MAGE’s vocabulary, these requirements and design artifacts are engineering models. DocAble arranged related work through project-specific work orders and design documents; Kiro makes a specification workflow part of the harness.

<!-- point: kiro-connects-requirements-to-tests-and-configurable-controls | Property-based tests provide evidence and configured hooks can enforce selected conditions; coverage and blocking decisions remain project responsibilities. | terms: modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->

The workflow can connect specifications to evidence and enforcement. Kiro’s IDE can extract properties from requirements and generate property-based tests, with execution optional by default [cite: kiro2026correctness]. Engineers can configure hooks to run checks after edits or block tool calls when preconditions fail [cite: kiro2026hooks]. Kiro supplies machinery through which engineers can establish these connections for their own systems.

<!-- point: amazon-internal-use-adds-team-specific-production-structure | Bedrock's reported use combined Kiro with scoped work, tests, operational tools, and senior review; the twentyfold figure measures normalized commit velocity rather than quality or isolated causal effect. -->

Consider how one AWS team organized its work. Six senior engineers rebuilt part of the Bedrock inference engine in 76 days, using small modules, reviewable commit-sized tasks, focused operational tools, tests, end-to-end canaries, and senior inspection of generated code [cite: kiro2026bedrock]. AWS reports that normalized commit velocity rose twentyfold, from two to forty commits per developer per week [cite: sivasubramanian2026frontier]. The team organized its architecture, work decomposition, and evaluation around agentic implementation. These are engineering decisions about the production process itself.

<!-- point: amazon-governs-improvements-to-the-harness-itself | Kiro-trace tests proposed harness changes against an unchanged control and regression requirements, with human merge decisions and new checks derived from evaluation failures. | terms: tolerance, governance-conversion -->

Kiro’s developers also evaluate changes to the agentic machinery. Kiro-trace proposes prompt or tool changes from recorded runs. A separate deterministic gate screens targeted failures, checks regression assertions, and compares candidates with an unchanged control on the full benchmark. It requires gains on selected tasks without losses beyond a specified margin elsewhere; a person decides whether to merge. One apparent improvement was rejected after it proved to be a lucky run. Evaluation mistakes became checks, and confirmed fixes add regression assertions [cite: kim2026kirotrace].

The same engineering principles apply to the factory’s own machinery as to the software it produces. Its developers establish tolerances, measure the consequences of proposed changes, and preserve what they learn in mechanisms that govern subsequent work.

## GitLab

GitLab develops a software-development platform that brings source control, code review, continuous integration, and deployment workflows into a common environment. Its product is itself part of the software factory used by other organizations: it provides the machinery through which changes are proposed, checked, approved, and delivered—a “factory in a box.” GitLab has extended this environment with agents that can perform development and review tasks while operating under existing permissions and workflow controls [cite: gitlab2026customize;gitlab2026identity]. Its case illustrates how agentic work can inherit established engineering controls.

<!-- point: customer-intent-enters-as-files-and-customer-authority-as-a-withheld-capability | GitLab integrates organizational context with CI and permission boundaries, including a security-review flow that cannot approve changes. -->

Repository instructions, chat rules, review skills, CI configuration, and workflow definitions supply different kinds of context [cite: gitlab2026customize]. For human-triggered flows running on runners, composite identity limits access to the intersection of the service account’s and triggering user’s privileges [cite: gitlab2026identity]. Tool-governance rules can also constrain actions; project rules may tighten but not loosen a group rule [cite: gitlab2026toolgovernance]. The documented security-review flow can report findings but cannot approve a change [cite: gitlab2026securityreview]. These mechanisms serve distinct engineering purposes: instructions inform the work, permissions restrict actions, and the approval boundary reserves a consequential decision.

GitLab’s internal playbook also makes acceptance depend on the surrounding pipeline. Alongside its failing-test-first practice, it recommends a CI job that fails when the test count decreases unless an explicit bypass label is present, addressing agents that remove tests to obtain a passing result [cite: gitlab2026playbook]. In this arrangement, a decrease in test count requires an explicit bypass rather than passing unnoticed through the pipeline.

GitLab illustrates the advantage of introducing agents into an environment that already governs software development. The organization can extend its existing machinery for permissions, review, and admission to encompass a new class of participants in the production process.

## Siemens

Siemens develops industrial technologies and engineering software used to design, simulate, manufacture, and maintain physical products. Its engineering software connects representations of product structure, system behavior, geometry, and manufacturing information across the product lifecycle. Unlike the preceding software-centered cases, the factory examined here concerns the production and evolution of engineered systems whose realizations may include physical artifacts. Siemens is exploring agents that operate through established systems-engineering models, simulation tools, and product-lifecycle management systems [cite: liu2024agentstudio;siemens2026a3e;haines2026bomagent]. Its case illustrates a different starting point for agentic engineering: an established model-based engineering environment.

Agent Studio research integrates AI agents with SysML v2 models and engineering simulation tools, including Simcenter Amesim. In a reported hybrid-vehicle design example, agents explore candidate architectures, simulate their behavior, and check them against requirements, with an engineer deciding how to proceed at critical points [cite: liu2024agentstudio]. Related Siemens work connects geometry, physical simulation, and engineering data through a digital thread [cite: siemens2026a3e]. In Teamcenter, an agent analyzes product structures and proposes bill-of-materials changes; a human confirms updates within existing governance and change processes [cite: haines2026bomagent].

Models of the engineered system, rather than source-code repositories, provide the common basis for design exploration, analysis, and subsequent realization. An organization with an established model-based engineering environment may already possess much of the machinery needed to direct and evaluate agentic work. Its models have defined semantics, and its tools can analyze, simulate, and transform them.

Consider the advantage of that starting point. Many kinds of engineering machinery I constructed while developing DocAble already exist in such an organization. Its agents can work through engineering abstractions that have been developed for the domain, using established tools to investigate the consequences of their proposed changes. Agents can also help extend that machinery where existing modeling languages and tools do not address a particular engineering problem.

## What the Factories Share, and Where They Differ

<!-- point: the-factories-place-admission-in-four-different-locations | The cases reveal distinct allocations of production and acceptance authority and distinct arrangements for supervisory capacity and engineering knowledge. -->

The factories differ in the products they support, the work they delegate, and the engineering machinery through which they govern production. Several comparisons are particularly instructive.

**Who may accept the work?** Cloudflare distinguishes standards that advise reviewers from those whose violations can block a merge, while retaining a recorded human override [cite: skidmore2026review]. Spotify inserted agents into an existing production process with required verification and human review [cite: charas2025honk;curtis2026spotifyqcon]. Shopify’s security workflow reserves merge authority to the engineers responsible for the affected code [cite: son2026security]; GitLab’s security-review flow can report findings but cannot approve a change [cite: gitlab2026securityreview]. In each arrangement, the authority to produce a change is distinct from the authority to accept it. Process design determines who or what may make the admission decision; tolerance and its evidence determine what that decision must establish.

<!-- point: supervision-and-verification-do-not-scale-with-production | Production and supervisory capacity can grow at different rates; review delays, workload scheduling, and verification experience make this a process-design concern. -->

**How is production capacity balanced against supervisory capacity?** Uber’s Sunday maintenance schedule would be incomplete without its separate limit on Monday’s review load. Spotify’s ability to generate more changes exposed limits in verification even when its measured rework did not rise. A factory must organize evaluation and admission alongside realization. Increasing the capacity of one stage can shift delay or risk to another. The engineering objective is to increase the capacity of the production system as a whole.

<!-- point: factories-acquire-engineering-knowledge-through-different-mechanisms | Standards, observed agent work, supplied specification workflows, measured experiments, and construction failures provide different routes for preserving engineering knowledge beyond an individual task. -->

**How does the factory acquire engineering knowledge?** Cloudflare turns organizational standards and incident lessons into structured obligations and checks. Shopify makes agent work visible to colleagues, who preserve useful discoveries in instructions and skills. Amazon’s Kiro supplies a specification workflow that engineers populate with project-specific decisions, while its internal experiments improve the agentic machinery through measured comparisons. DocAble developed many of its models and controls in response to failures encountered during construction. Preserving an obligation does not itself make it enforceable: Cloudflare distinguishes advisory standards from blocking controls, Shopify identifies security requirements that need enforcement in code, and Kiro leaves teams to decide which generated checks should govern subsequent work. These arrangements differ in how engineers identify, represent, and preserve knowledge for subsequent work.

**What engineering machinery already exists?** Spotify introduced agents into an established production and verification process; Siemens brings existing engineering models, analyses, and simulation tools to agentic work. Other organizations may have extensive source-level checks but few explicit system models. DocAble developed much of this machinery while developing the product itself. Recall the alignment kernel from [ref:4.1-the-dynamics-of-mage:heading:establish-a-kernel-before-expanding]: an organization introducing agents may already possess many of the models and controls needed to protect the properties that subsequent engineering must preserve. Identifying these assets and their gaps is a useful starting point for adoption ([ref:chapter:appendix-b-brownfield-migration]).

<!-- point: the-inheritance-channel-carries-its-own-maintenance-cost | Shared knowledge and checks require maintenance; the documented revisions show how reusable engineering structure exchanges repeated judgment for upkeep. -->

**How does the factory maintain and improve its machinery?** Shopify repairs divergent instructions; Cloudflare revises policy representations and moves suitable checks earlier; Kiro’s improvement experiments acquire new regression assertions. Each preserved lesson can reduce future reconstruction or repeated judgment, but the resulting artifact must remain connected to the work it governs. This is the carrying-cost question from {{sec:the-software-factory}} in operational form. Improvements in foundation models can change that calculation, as Spotify’s retired judge illustrates. Conversely, Shopify’s rising merge rate with an unchanged foundation model illustrates the opportunity to improve the factory independently of its fabricator.

These organizations have made different investments in the two problems of factory engineering introduced in {{sec:the-software-factory}}: process design and tolerance. They have organized how agents perform work, how engineering knowledge reaches them, and how the resulting changes are evaluated and admitted. The differences reflect the engineering problems each organization faces and the machinery it already possesses.
