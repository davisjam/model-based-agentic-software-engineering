# Failures That Became Infrastructure

> ### This chapter illustrates {#illustrates}
>
> ✓ Governance Conversion · ✓ Soft aims, hard holds · ✓ Tests for agent failure modes

<!-- point: every-mechanism-began-as-a-recurring-problem | Every mechanism began as an ugly recurring problem. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The method's third beat — convert recurring failures into engineering capital — is a claim, and
this chapter is where you watch it run at the source. Every clean mechanism in the built system
began as an ugly, recurring problem. DocAble is where the loop was first turned, so its failures
are where a recurring failure first became a wall. Each one below is retold once: the failure, the
mechanism it forced into being, and the capital that mechanism banked. Read them as the raw
material of governance conversion. The aphorisms they paid for are gathered at the chapter's end,
once the cases have earned them.

## The instruction satisfied to the letter

<!-- point: an-instruction-satisfied-to-the-letter-and-voided-in-spirit | An under-specified instruction is satisfied to the letter and voided in spirit. | terms: stringly-typed, explicitness-is-essential -->
**Problem.** By April the system was 300,000 lines that worked but were janky. It had started as an
MVP built only to prove the thing was possible, so I had imposed real architecture on the parts I
knew were sensitive and hoped the agent would get the rest right. The web layer and the front end
turned crufty.

**Initial attempt.** So I told it to use typed Python. It did, in a sense: it used the type
`string` for everything. Stringly-typed — the word "type" satisfied to the letter and voided in
spirit, with none of the safety the compiler could give. Told to retrofit real types, it turned on
the checker and made everything strings and ints, without building the abstractions I wanted. The
same thing happened porting JavaScript to TypeScript that week. Each port took the cheapest path,
because the agent had no model and did not know the names of things.

**Why it failed.** The failure is not laziness. It is the shape of an under-specified instruction
met by a system that optimizes for the next green checkmark. "Use types" names a checkbox, not the
abstractions, and the agent found the reading that turned the check green with the least work.

<!-- point: the-fix-was-a-typed-model-per-dense-region-and-lints-to-blocking | The fix: a structured model per dense region, every commodity lint driven to blocking. | terms: audits-into-lints, make-lint-blocking, dense-primitive-region -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The fix was the method's recipe run on my own codebase. I turned on every
commodity lint from Ruff, Pylint, and ESLint and drove each to zero, making it blocking as it hit
zero so the smell could never return. Then I worked region by region: find a dense primitive region
— a stretch doing many low-level operations by hand, raw calls and untyped strings passed around
with no abstraction that owns them — and induce a structured model that owns it, routing the region
through it. Each lint driven to zero named one primitive smell and gave it a home; the coarse
model-view-controller split was the same move at the coarse grain.

<!-- point: structure-is-compression-and-the-models-now-shape-every-task | Structure is compression; the models now shape every later task. | terms: governance-conversion, brownfield-migration -->
**General lesson.** A month later I had 200,000 lines that were not janky. The system got smaller as
it got better, because structure is compression and the primitive sprawl had been the bloat. The
project's churn corroborates the shape: add-and-delete peaked exactly during this work, then
deletions collapsed and later windows went net-additive — the signature of a one-time replacement,
not steady thrashing. [data: churn] That month was governance conversion at brownfield scale. The
capital was not the 200,000 lines. It was the enforcement that holds the models, the architecture,
and the code together every time the code changes.

## The architecture was the constraint

<!-- point: optimizing-within-an-architecture-that-was-itself-the-constraint | I optimized within an architecture that was itself the constraint. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
<!-- index-def: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
**Problem.** The service cost thirty to forty dollars a day to run idle on a Kubernetes cluster.

**Initial attempt.** So I set the agents on a scale-to-zero push: keep the cluster, keep the custom
autoscaler, squeeze the idle pods down to nothing. For weeks they made it better and better.

**Why it failed.** It never paid off, because scaling a cold pod back up took sixty to three hundred
seconds, and a five-minute cold start is a worse problem than the bill. I was optimizing *within*
the architecture when the architecture itself was the constraint. The real move was to change it: go
serverless, and let the platform own the scaling I had been hand-building against its own ceiling.

<!-- point: a-recurring-wall-is-the-signal-to-price-a-paradigm-change | A fix that keeps hitting the same wall is the signal to price a paradigm change. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
**Mechanism introduced.** Coding agents accelerate within the frame you give them even when the
frame is wrong, so a wrong frame gets very good very fast. This one had a tell, visible only in
hindsight: the optimization kept colliding with the *same* wall. When an in-paradigm fix keeps
hitting the paradigm's own limit, that recurring wall is the mechanism the failure hands you. Not a
lint, but a decision rule: price out the paradigm change instead of buying another round of in-frame
optimization.

<!-- point: some-conversions-produce-judgment-not-machinery | Some conversions bank judgment, not machinery. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**General lesson.** The capital here is not a wall the environment enforces; some conversions produce
judgment, not machinery. What those weeks bought was a cheap detector for a wrong frame: a fix that
recurs against the same ceiling is evidence about the ceiling, not the fix. It is the same signal,
one level up, as the second occurrence of a failure earning its lint. The recurrence is the
information.

## The decision left open

<!-- point: an-unmade-decision-is-where-the-agent-guesses-worst | The unmade decision is where the agent guesses worst. | terms: optionality-is-poison -->
<!-- index-def: optionality-is-poison -->
**Problem.** My merge-train cron kept stalling when two agents edited the same file.

**Initial attempt.** It proposed a one-flag fix: on any conflict, take one side and drop the other. I
signed off without asking what "drop the other side" meant.

**Why it failed.** For four days it silently deleted real work from the main branch every time two
changes overlapped. The same commit heading landed again and again, each time a little smaller,
while I chased the symptoms one missing function at a time. The failure was not the flag. It was the
decision I left open. The agent filled it with the cheapest thing that made the error go away,
because an open choice at the point of execution becomes a choice someone has to make now, and the
agent makes it to unblock itself, with the consequences nowhere in its objective.

<!-- point: decide-conflict-handling-up-front-name-the-resolver | Decide conflict handling up front; name the resolver. | terms: optionality-is-poison, explicitness-is-essential -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The right answer was a merge resolver that understood the files it was
merging, and I could have decided that up front. The mechanism the failure earned is the discipline
of naming the consequential dimensions before execution — the architecture, the types, and here,
precisely, what happens on a conflict — and delegating only the rest. The narrower the agent's
search, the more of its speed lands on the target instead of scattering.

<!-- point: the-capital-was-a-conflict-policy-the-environment-now-carries | The capital was a conflict policy the environment now carries. | terms: governance-conversion, judgment-into-infrastructure -->
**General lesson.** What the four days bought was a standing rule: no destructive default on a
conflict, and a resolver that reads the files instead of picking a side. The decision, made once and
encoded, is one no later agent has to re-guess at midnight. That is the move the whole method turns
on: convert the judgment into infrastructure so the call is made once and held.

## "Done" was a claim

<!-- point: two-green-reports-that-were-not-true | Two green reports, neither of them true. | terms: done-is-a-claim -->
<!-- index-def: done-is-a-claim -->
**Problem.** Two incidents, close together, made "done is a claim" non-negotiable.

**Initial attempt.** I trusted the reports. Once, a lint gate hit a stray error, caught it in a
catch-all that returned success, and let a run of commits land completely unlinted while the log said
fine. Another time a chain of pin tests passed green.

**Why it failed.** A botched merge had landed those pin tests onto a tree missing the very field they
were supposed to check, so they confirmed a field that existed and never touched the one that
mattered. Both reported done. Neither was. These are agent-shaped failures: a report of "done"
describes the agent's intent, not the state of the repository, and the gap between the two is where a
confident green hides.

<!-- point: the-mechanism-was-a-gate-the-agent-cannot-talk-past | The mechanism: a gate the agent cannot talk its way past. | terms: done-is-a-claim, audits-into-lints -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The mechanism both failures earned is verification the agent cannot author:
re-run the tests yourself, re-run the lints the work was supposed to satisfy, compare the counts
against the claim. The catch-all taught a sharper rule. A gate that swallows its own error is worse
than no gate, because it reports success it did not earn, so a control must fail loud and never
silent-catch. The pin tests taught that a check is only as trustworthy as the tree it ran against.

<!-- point: the-capital-was-a-control-system-around-the-work | The capital was a control system around the work, not more code. | terms: governance-conversion, judgment-into-infrastructure -->
**General lesson.** Out of these, my job changed shape. It shifted from writing implementation to
running the control system around it: decomposing work, cross-checking invariants, hardening tests,
re-running the gates against the real tree, deciding whether the architecture was genuinely cleaner
or merely different. The capital is that control system, the standing habit and machinery that treat
every "done" as the start of verification, not the end.

## A content-free detector, measured on the wrong world {#fieldnote-content-free-detector}

<!-- point: a-detector-that-measured-the-right-quantity-on-the-wrong-world | A detector measured the right quantity on the wrong world. | terms: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
<!-- index-def: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
**Problem.** Late in the same stretch, an agent wrote me a content-free detector: if an image's
pixels barely vary, skip the vision model and mark it decorative. Clean, typed, careful.

**Initial attempt.** Its own comment promised a false positive was "not possible" — real photos and
charts vary twenty times more than the cutoff, so nothing real could slip under. The margin was true.

**Why it failed.** It was measured on the wrong world. Hand it a Rothko, or Malevich's black square,
and the pixels barely vary; the detector marks the painting decorative and drops it, and a blind
student reading an art book gets silence where the subject should be. No threshold saves you, because
a blank spacer and a Malevich are identical in the one quantity the code looked at. "Content-free"
was never a fact about pixels. It is a fact about an image in a context of use, and the use here is
teaching, and teaching includes the one tradition built to prove a picture need not vary. That
confident "not possible" was the tell: it marks the spot where the model's picture of its own inputs
was too small.

<!-- point: when-the-cheap-proxy-is-unsure-describe-anyway | When the cheap proxy is unsure, describe anyway. | terms: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The mechanism is a rule about proxies: when the cheap proxy is unsure,
describe the image anyway. A content-free detector that has never seen a Rothko will tell you a
Rothko is nothing, so the detector does not get the last word — it defers to the fuller model at the
margin. And the test that guards it is built for the agent's failure mode, not a human's: pin the
class, the picture that varies little but carries everything, rather than the example.

**General lesson.** Build tests for the failure modes the agent actually has, not the ones a person
would. The next confident, coherent, subtly-wrong subsystem should meet a sensor instead of a
checkbox.

## The retry that became a storm

The failures so far were about operating the fleet. The last four are about the product it built — the models inside DocAble — and each one, like the mechanisms above, began in a failure.

<!-- point: automatic-retry-amplified-the-load-it-was-meant-to-relieve | Automatic retry amplified the load exactly when the system was already hurting. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**Problem.** The dominant production failure was a slow generation call, and the lifecycle around it was implicit. Status got written from scattered places, and one terminal state masked many distinct internal failures.

**Initial attempt.** The reflex to any failed job was to requeue it. Retry was the default recovery for every timeout and every crash.

**Why it failed.** Under a slow dependency that reflex became a storm — requeue, still slow, same timeout, requeue again — and the recovery amplified the load precisely when the system was already hurting. Cost hid the damage. Every attempt wrote its full cost with nothing marking it a retry, so a job tried three times billed roughly three times over and the dashboards showed a silent multiplication no one could explain.

<!-- point: the-replacement-was-a-machine-an-owner-and-a-cut | The replacement named the states, gave each chunk one atomic owner, and cut the retry. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**Mechanism introduced.** Three moves replaced the reflex. An explicit state machine named the states and the transitions and put one guarded function in front of them that refuses any illegal move. Ownership by compare-and-set let a chunk be claimed only while it was still queued, so a redelivered claim loses the race and does nothing a second time — the exactly-once effect rests on that atomic claim, not on a lease that can lapse. And the retry was cut: a failure now surfaces as failed and never requeues, the one sanctioned exception being planned preemption, a recycle mid-flight rather than a failure, with a check that keeps the exception from widening back into general retry.

<!-- point: the-sweep-learned-to-tell-dead-from-slow | A later failure taught the model a distinction it did not have on day one: dead versus merely slow. | terms: governance-conversion, judgment-into-infrastructure -->
**General lesson.** A later failure taught the model a distinction it lacked on day one. A stale sweep hunting dead owners began reaping jobs that were slow but still advancing; a long-running job looked dead and got failed. The fix taught the sweep to tell a dead job from a merely slow one and to spare anything still making progress. Automatic recovery is not free. A reflex that fires into a wounded system can be the thing that finishes it, and the cure was to name the lifecycle, give each unit one atomic owner, and make recovery a decision instead of a reflex.

## Provenance became a lookup

<!-- point: the-simplest-forensic-question-was-expensive | The system could not cheaply answer which step wrote a bad element. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**Problem.** A corrupted document once took four cross-file hops to trace back to the pass that wrote it. Each hop was cheap; the chain was expensive. The system could not cheaply answer the plainest question a remediation tool owes its user: which step changed this?

**Initial attempt.** Stamps were written by hand, site by site, with nothing checking that a given mutation was stamped at all. A low-level write path recorded its changes into the document's own structure and never into the registry the changelog reads.

<!-- point: bypasses-produced-empty-changelog-rows | Eleven sites bypassed the stamp helper and shipped empty changelog rows for a whole class of jobs. | terms: governance-conversion, done-is-a-claim -->
**Why it failed.** Verification found eleven production sites writing through that low-level path, skipping the stamp helper, shipping empty changelog rows for a whole class of jobs. The customer's account of what changed came out blank while the code had changed plenty. An empty changelog row is "done" as a claim again — a report the work cannot back.

<!-- point: the-changelog-became-a-projection-of-stamps | Stamps became mandatory and the changelog a loss-free projection of them, landed warn-then-block. | terms: governance-conversion, soft-aims-hard-holds -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The changelog became a projection. Every mutation writes its stamp — the verb, the pass, the visibility — and the customer-facing changelog is derived from those stamps rather than authored by hand, so diagnosis stopped being inference and became a lookup. A wiring check now fails the build for any mutating verb that returns without recording its stamp, and it landed the honest way: as a warning first, while dozens of verbs still lacked wiring; a fix wave drained the gap; only then did it turn blocking. Warning first made the obligation visible. Migration made it feasible. Blocking made it authoritative.

<!-- point: governance-conversion-in-literal-form | A forensic cost became a derived guarantee, then a gate, by a landing discipline anyone can reuse. | terms: governance-conversion, soft-aims-hard-holds -->
**General lesson.** This is governance conversion in almost embarrassingly literal form: a forensic cost turned into a derived guarantee, then into a gate. And the landing is the reusable move. You do not switch on a blocking gate the day you write it while the tree is already full of violations, or the likely response is to bury the code in exceptions. The warning that precedes the drain is what makes the gate survivable.

## Measurement: the story that stops before the gate

<!-- point: a-timeout-spoke-only-after-the-budget-was-spent | A silent hang failed after eight minutes with an opaque message and a progress bar frozen at thirty-eight percent. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**Problem.** A document's repair stalled in silence. The subprocess was killed by the job timeout after roughly eight minutes, and the job failed with an opaque message while the customer's progress bar sat frozen at thirty-eight percent. The timeout existed. It told you almost nothing until the budget was already spent.

**Initial attempt.** There was no model of per-chunk cost or time. The timeout was a bare kill — nothing predicted whether a chunk's worst case would breach it, and there was no honest baseline for the happy path.

<!-- point: the-incident-created-the-model-and-its-calibration | The incident forced a per-chunk budget into existence, seeded with a provisional measurement kept as external data. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The incident created the model: a per-chunk worst-case budget measured against the job ceiling, seeded from the very incident that motivated it. The seed measurement is externalized, timestamped, and marked provisional — kept as a datapoint in a file rather than frozen as a constant in the code, precisely because over-fitting to one fixture is the model's own top risk. [ref:measurement-no-gate] draws where the chain deliberately stops.

<!-- point: no-later-failure-forced-this-model-stronger | The production gate was consciously left for later; the model reports a breach but does not yet refuse or split the work. | terms: soft-aims-hard-holds, governance-conversion -->
**General lesson.** Here the history has a clean, honest ending, and I do not dress it up. No later failure forced this model stronger. Production enforcement is a deliberate, ratified deferral: the model reports a would-be breach, but it does not yet refuse the work or split it, and the only hard cap lives offline. Enforcing a poorly calibrated bound could cost more than observing it, so the restraint is a feature of the record, not a gap in it. Governance conversion does not mean turning every newly discovered relationship into a blocking mechanism. Sometimes the system is ready for authority. Sometimes it is ready only for better knowledge. The question mark belongs in the book.

<!-- point: evidence-is-honest-about-which-rung-it-reaches | Deployed, cleared checkers, and measured fidelity are three different rungs, and none is end-user access. | terms: field-report, soft-aims-hard-holds -->
And measurement is not the same as safety. DocAble was deployed, it cleared the institutional checkers a document is required to pass, and it measured its own fidelity — but none of those three is the same as a blind student finding the document usable. This part is the evidence, and the evidence is honest about which rung it reaches.

## A note on restraint

<!-- point: one-model-was-confirmed-not-strengthened | The unified detect-and-repair engine invites a failure-strengthening arc and does not earn one. | terms: soft-aims-hard-holds, governance-conversion -->
One history refuses the arc, and reporting that is the point. The unified detect-and-repair engine — one rule engine that ended a duplication tax where two separate checkers had scanned the same document in two languages a round-trip apart — was a real consolidation with a real motivation. But the record does not show a later failure of that model forcing it stronger. The nearest event, an audit prompted by a routing incident elsewhere, confirmed the model's boundary rather than breaking it. So I report a confirmation, not a forced strengthening. A book that makes every model fail and recover on the same beat is telling a formula, not a history: some models were forged in an incident and hardened by three more, others were built from a clean motivation and simply held. Reporting the difference is what makes the rest of this archaeology worth trusting.

## What the failures taught

<!-- point: amplification-is-neutral-judgment-is-scarce | Amplification is neutral; judgment is the scarce resource. | terms: judgment-into-infrastructure -->
Step back from the failures. What they share is not a technology but a posture. The agent amplified
whatever judgment I brought, good and bad, with equal enthusiasm, so every failure here was a place
my own judgment had been thin, and every mechanism was that judgment made explicit and permanent.
Amplification is neutral. The fleet made good judgment cheap to repeat and bad judgment cheap to
amplify. The method emerged from learning to tell the difference, and from turning the good calls
into infrastructure before attention was asked to make them again.

<!-- point: a-single-case-field-report-part-6-takes-the-question-up | A single-case field report; Part 6 takes the question up. | terms: judgment-into-infrastructure, field-report -->
That is where this field report hands off. My evidence is a single-person project without real
customers, run against one vendor's models, and the specifics will not all generalize. But the shape
held across every failure above: convert judgment into infrastructure, let the machinery hold what
your attention cannot, and spend your scarce judgment on the calls the machinery cannot make. The
clean method is what remains after the ugly failures were converted into structure. What that does to
the shape of the profession — the roles, the org chart, who is trusted to build the walls, and who in
an organization is even empowered to convert a recurring failure into shared practice — is the
question Part VI takes up next.
