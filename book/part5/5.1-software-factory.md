# The Software Factory

<!-- point: the-printer-described-a-machine-not-a-production-system | The printer analogy explained the fabricator; it does not explain mass production through fabricators — for that we need the factory. | terms: printer-metaphor -->
Earlier in this book, I compared coding agents to 2D and 3D printers.
The comparison was about fabricative technology: unlike a specific-purpose tool such as a stapler,
 a fabricator can realize a broad range of artifacts from a description of what should be made.

That analogy helps us understand the agent.
It does not tell us what happens when fabricative capacity becomes a means of mass production.
For that problem, we need to turn to an older engineering history.
We need the factory.

<!-- point: the-factory-question | Every factory must answer one question — which judgments must the fabricator make, and which can move into the production system surrounding fabrication — and software has now faced it under two different economics. -->
Every factory must answer one question: which judgments must be made by the fabricator, and which
 can be moved into the production system surrounding fabrication?
Physical manufacturing spent two centuries building its answer.
Software has now faced the question twice, once before commodity intelligence and once after, and
 the two encounters differ in their economics rather than in the question.

<!-- point: mechanical-engineers-met-this-problem-first | Mechanical engineers confronted fabrication outrunning supervision long before software did, and answered with tolerances, metrology, interchangeable manufacture, and process control. -->
Long before software engineers confronted agents producing implementation faster than people could
 inspect it, mechanical engineers confronted their own versions of the same problem.
New designs demanded manufacturing precision that existing methods could not reliably achieve.
Machine tools increased what could be fabricated.
Tolerances made acceptable variation explicit.
Metrology made consequential properties measurable.
Interchangeable manufacture required independently produced parts to correspond to shared
 specifications.
Inspection and process control provided ways to supervise production without asking an engineer to
 personally reconstruct every act of fabrication.

<!-- point: capacity-changed-what-the-factory-had-to-know | Increasing fabrication capacity changed what the factory itself had to know and enforce, not merely how fast it ran. -->
The history matters because increasing fabrication capacity did not merely make factories faster.
It changed what the factory itself had to know and enforce.

<!-- point: three-failures-not-a-triumphalist-history | The chapter reads the history as three productive failures — capability arriving before the representation and control needed to supervise it — not as a tour of progress. -->
What follows is not a tour of progress.
It is three episodes in which new production capability arrived before the knowledge and control
 needed to supervise it, and the production system had to change in response.
A steam engine that could not be built to its own design.
An interchangeability that was announced a generation before it could be manufactured.
A volume of production that made inspecting every artifact untenable.
Each failure forced a different piece of engineering out of the fabricator's head and into the
 factory, and each piece will matter when we return to software.

## From Craft to Precision

<!-- section-terms: tolerance -->

<!-- point: craft-worked-while-each-artifact-was-reconciled-by-hand | Craft production worked while fabrication knowledge resided in the craftsperson and each artifact was individually reconciled. -->
Before industrial precision, skilled craftspeople could make complicated mechanisms by fitting
 individual pieces to one another.
A gunsmith filed each lock part against its neighbors; a millwright trued each bearing to its shaft.
That works when the knowledge of fabrication resides in the craftsperson and each artifact can be
 individually reconciled.
Nothing needs to be written down, because the person shaping the part is also the person judging it.

<!-- point: watts-design-outran-realization | Watt's separate condenser was sound as a design, but no workshop could bore a cylinder true enough to realize it. -->
Steam raised the stakes.
James Watt saw how to fix the great waste of the Newcomen engine — condense the steam in a separate
 vessel, so the working cylinder stays hot — and patented the improvement in 1769.[cite: winchester2018]
The design was sound.
The engine was nearly unbuildable.
No workshop of the day could bore a large cylinder round and true enough to seal against its piston;
 steam leaked past, and the efficiency the design promised existed only on paper.
Watt could know what his engine should do.
The engine could not achieve it if the cylinder could not be manufactured to the necessary tolerance.

<!-- point: wilkinsons-machine-changed-what-could-be-realized | Wilkinson's boring machinery, built for cannon, changed what could be reliably realized and made Watt's design achievable. -->
The ironmaster John Wilkinson had patented machinery for boring cannon from solid iron.
Adapted to steam-engine cylinders, his boring machine held the cutting head rigid along the full
 length of the bore, and it produced cylinders true — by Watt's account — to the thickness of a worn
 shilling across a bore of fifty inches.[cite: winchester2018]
With Wilkinson's cylinders, the Boulton & Watt engine finally performed as designed.
The invention was not enough.
The production environment had to acquire the capability to reliably realize it.

<!-- point: the-episode-separates-four-things | The episode separates design from realization, design capability from production capability, craft knowledge from externalized specification. -->
The episode separates several things that software engineering has usually been able to leave fused:

- **Design is not realization.** Watt held the design for years while the engine could not be built.
- **The capability of a design is not the capability of the production system.** The engine's
  efficiency was a property of the pair, not of the drawing.
- **Craft knowledge is not an externalized specification.** The fit a craftsperson judges by hand
  cannot be handed to another shop; a stated tolerance can.
- **A better fabricator can enable a qualitatively different system.** Wilkinson's machine did not
  make old engines cheaper; it made a new class of engine possible.

### The Tolerance Is a Model

<!-- point: tolerance-is-a-purposeful-reduction | A tolerance is a model of what matters about a part for a particular purpose — a purposeful reduction, not a complete description. | terms: tolerance -->
Follow precision one step further and it becomes a form of representation.
Once a consequential property can be stated as an allowable range, the factory no longer needs a
 master craftsperson to decide anew whether every part fits well enough.
A tolerance is a model of what matters about a part for a particular engineering purpose.
Not a complete description of the part.
A purposeful reduction.
Readers of {{chapter:modeling}} will recognize the claim; here it arrives from history rather than
 from method.
The first thing mass production externalized was not labor.
It was the judgment of fit.

## From Precision to the Factory

<!-- point: one-precise-part-is-not-production | One precise part is not yet production; repeatable production required parts made independently to correspond to a shared specification. -->
One precise cylinder is not yet production.
The next episode is about making precision repeatable — and it too begins with a failure.

<!-- point: whitney-announced-interchangeability-before-the-system-existed | Whitney's 1801 interchangeability demonstration was staged; the claim preceded, by decades, the production system that could make it true. -->
In 1798 Eli Whitney contracted with the United States government to deliver ten thousand muskets
 built from uniform parts, and in 1801 he assembled musket locks before federal officials from piles
 of parts presented as interchangeable.
The demonstration was theater: the parts had been prepared in advance for the occasion, and
 Whitney's armory never achieved interchangeable manufacture in production.[cite: hounshell1984]
The claim was decades ahead of the production system that could make it true.
Announcing a capability is not the same as building the factory that delivers it.

<!-- point: armory-practice-built-the-real-thing | Real interchangeability came from decades of armory practice: model artifacts, drawings, gauges, fixtures, and inspection shared across shops. -->
What actually delivered interchangeable parts was several decades of unglamorous work in the federal
 armories at Springfield and Harpers Ferry and the private shops around them — a body of technique
 that contemporaries came to call armory practice.[cite: hounshell1984]
A model weapon and its drawings served as shared authority for what every part must be.
Gauges checked each consequential dimension against that authority, so a part's acceptability no
 longer depended on who examined it.
Special-purpose machine tools and fixtures constrained fabrication so that conforming parts were the
 expected outcome rather than the lucky one.
Inspection records tied accepted work to the gauge and the maker.
By mid-century, parts made in different shops could meet in one working weapon.

<!-- point: gauges-gave-the-specification-consequences | The drawing states what matters; the gauge gives the statement consequences — representation and enforcement arrived as a pair, and neither suffices alone. -->
Notice the division of labor inside that system, because it recurs.
The drawing and its tolerances state what matters about a part; they enforce nothing.
The gauge gives the statement consequences: a part that fails the gauge does not enter the weapon,
 whatever its maker believes about it.
Representation and enforcement arrived as a pair, and neither is sufficient alone — an unenforced
 drawing is advice, and a gauge with no drawing behind it is an arbitrary obstacle.
Whitney's failure can be read in exactly these terms: he possessed the claim and some of the
 machinery, but not the authoritative representations and conformance checks that make
 interchangeability a property of the system rather than a property of a demonstration.

<!-- point: interchangeability-means-explicit-properties | The significance of interchangeable parts is that independently made parts participate in a common system because consequential properties are explicit and repeatable. -->
The significant change is not "we can manufacture more."
It is that parts made independently — by different hands, in different shops, at different times —
 can participate in a common system, because the properties that matter have been made sufficiently
 explicit and sufficiently repeatable.
Correspondence to a shared specification replaced reconciliation by a single craftsperson.

<!-- point: mature-production-grew-surrounding-machinery | Mature industrial production required surrounding machinery: representations, measurement, tooling, process structure, and oversight. -->
Around that core, mature industrial production accumulated its surrounding machinery:

- **Representations** — drawings, specifications, tolerances;
- **Measurement** — gauges and metrology;
- **Fabrication machinery** — machine tools and the fixtures that hold work to its intended geometry;
- **Process structure** — process plans and routing;
- **Oversight** — inspection, traceability, quality gates, and supervisors.

<!-- point: knowledge-moved-into-the-production-system | As fabrication scaled, engineering moved consequential knowledge out of the individual fabricator and into the production system. -->
As fabrication scaled, engineering moved consequential knowledge out of the individual fabricator
 and into the production system.
Not because factory workers became unintelligent.
Not because judgment disappeared.
Because production beyond craft scale required the factory itself to carry more of the knowledge
 needed for repeatable production.

## The Supervisor Does Not Watch the Machine

<!-- point: steam-capacity-created-a-control-problem | Steam power increased the capacity of the fabricator; precision manufacturing created a corresponding problem of control that examining every artifact could not answer. -->
Interchangeable manufacture succeeded, and its success produced the third failure.
Steam power increased the capacity of the fabricator.
The engine that had once been unbuildable for want of a true cylinder now drove the machinery of
 production, and a powered works turned out parts at a rate no craftsperson's judgment of fit could
 follow.
Precision manufacturing created a corresponding problem of control.
Engineers had to state how much variation a part could tolerate and determine whether the parts
 being produced remained within those limits.
Examining every finished artifact could not carry that burden: inspection at production volume was
 uneconomic, and a rejected part says nothing about whether the process that made it was sound.

<!-- point: the-widget-question-answered | Mature factories learned that the quality team cannot simply examine every widget; tolerances and metrology are central, and the rest of the production system is organized to keep production within tolerance. -->
The Preface asked whether the quality team truly needs to examine every widget.
Mature factories learned long ago that the answer could not simply be yes.
Two ideas are central.
Tolerances state which variation is acceptable.
Metrology makes the relevant properties measurable.
Once those are available, the rest of the production system can be organized around keeping what is
 produced within tolerance: process controls constrain production, sampling provides evidence about
 it, and traceability connects escaped defects back to their origin.[cite: shewhart1931]

<!-- point: supervision-through-surfaces-not-watching | A supervisor stays in control through the factory's supervisory surfaces — plans, production state, measurements, alarms, exceptions — not by watching every machine cycle. -->
A supervisor of an automated factory does not remain in control by personally reconstructing every
 fabricated part or watching every machine cycle.
The factory supplies other supervisory surfaces: plans, production state, tolerances, measurements,
 alarms, inspection results, exceptions, traceability.
Some properties are prevented structurally, by tooling and fixtures that make the wrong geometry
 hard to produce.
Some are measured continuously; some are inspected; some are sampled.
Some raise alarms.
Some still require expert human judgment — and the system's design determines how much.

<!-- point: defects-change-the-factory | A recurring defect changes the factory itself — fixture, tolerance, process, inspection regime, tooling, training, or design — not merely the defective artifact. -->
And when a defect does escape, notice what changes.
Not merely the defective artifact.
The factory: a fixture is corrected, a tolerance revised, a process changed, an inspection added,
 tooling replaced, training updated, or the design itself amended.
A recurring failure is treated as evidence that the production system is missing structure, and the
 structure is added.
[ref:factory-progression] compresses the three episodes into the progression they form.

## Software as Craft with Industrial Tooling

<!-- point: software-kept-engineer-and-fabricator-fused | Software's ordinary organization never made this separation: its engineers were also its fabricators, and source code stayed rich because its author was also its interpreter. -->
Software's ordinary organization never made this separation, because its engineers were also its
 fabricators.
The programmer could carry architectural knowledge, implementation knowledge, conventions,
 exceptions, and local judgment together.
Source code became an extraordinarily rich artifact because the person manipulating it was
 simultaneously interpreting it.
Software engineering has been, in this one respect, a craft discipline with industrial tooling.

<!-- point: we-built-pieces-of-a-factory | Software built pieces of a factory — compiler, build, tests, source control, CI, deployment, observability — while consequential knowledge stayed in human cognition or in the implementation. -->
We nevertheless built pieces of a factory around that craftsperson: compiler, build, tests, source
 control, continuous integration, deployment, observability.
Some of those pieces are genuine supervisory surfaces of the kind the previous section described — a
 type checker prevents a class of defect structurally; a test suite measures; a monitoring alarm
 fires on an exception.
But much consequential engineering knowledge remained either in human cognition or recoverable
 primarily by reading the implementation.
The tests pin the properties someone thought to state; the conventions live in review comments and
 folklore; the reasons behind consequential choices are recoverable, if at all, from the code that
 embodies them.
That arrangement can work while fabrication capacity and engineering capacity are approximately
 coupled — while the people producing the code are the same people who hold what it means.

<!-- point: leaving-choices-open-was-economical | The craft arrangement persisted for a good reason: the skilled programmer could resolve the design questions met during implementation, so leaving them open until then was often the economical choice. -->
There was good reason for the arrangement.
The skilled programmer doing the work of implementation was also capable of resolving many of the
 design questions encountered along the way: questions of behavior, structure, interfaces, failure
 handling, performance.
It was often economical to leave such choices open until implementation rather than determine and
 represent every decision beforehand.
The programmer did not merely realize a determined design.
The programmer completed the design while realizing it.

## Software's Earlier Factories

<!-- point: software-attempted-the-factory-before | Software attempted factory organization long before generative AI: enterprise architecture, outsourced and offshore implementation, model-driven development, organizations named software factories. -->
Software organizations attempted to change that arrangement long before generative AI.
Enterprise-architecture groups produced specifications, UML models, interface definitions, and
 contractual gates, then delegated implementation to lower-cost programmers in separate
 organizations — outsourced, often offshore, sometimes under names as direct as *software factory*.
Model-driven development pursued the same separation with executable representations.
The intent was exactly the armories' move: determine the consequential decisions upstream, in
 shared representations, and make downstream fabrication comparatively mechanical.

<!-- point: the-obstacle-was-economic-not-conceptual | The separation was defeated economically rather than conceptually: specify enough for a low-discretion implementer and the architect has paid the realization cost in another notation. -->
What defeated these factories was economic rather than conceptual.
To make implementation substantially mechanical, the upstream engineers had to resolve and
 communicate most of the decisions that would otherwise be made during programming, and the more
 completely they specified the realization, the more engineering effort they spent specifying it.
The limit is awkward.
An architect who describes a system in enough detail that a low-discretion implementer can reliably
 produce it has done much of the intellectual work of implementing it.
The architect has paid the realization cost in another notation.

<!-- point: open-decisions-move-downstream | Decisions left open do not disappear; they move downstream to an implementer who must be skilled enough to resolve them, and the separated organization still had to establish correspondence between design and implementation. -->
Leaving more decisions open reduces the cost of specification, but the open degrees of freedom do
 not disappear.
They move downstream to the implementer, who must possess enough expertise to resolve them; the
 implementer becomes a designer, completing the design while realizing it, and the craft coupling
 returns.
The separated organization also acquired a second obligation: establishing that the realized system
 corresponded to the intended design, for which reading the implementation was often the only
 instrument.
Software therefore retained a craft organization even where its production was organized as a
 factory.
Substantial engineering judgment remained economically coupled to fabrication.

<!-- point: craft-and-factory-are-economic-regimes | Craft remains economical while moving judgment out of fabrication costs as much as leaving it with the skilled fabricator; factory production becomes economical when reusable structures carry consequential judgment more cheaply. | terms: tolerance -->
The general form covers the armories and the architecture groups alike.
Craft production remains economical when transferring enough judgment out of fabrication would cost
 as much as leaving that judgment with the skilled fabricator.
Factory production becomes economical when reusable production structures can carry consequential
 judgment more cheaply than repeatedly exercising it during fabrication.
Tolerances, gauges, and process control crossed that threshold for physical parts.
Architecture documents, models, and contractual interfaces, for most software, did not.

## Commodity Intelligence Changes the Economics

<!-- point: agents-break-the-coupling | Agents break the craft coupling: the new fabricator is inexpensive and capable at once, so engineers need not specify every choice to make delegation economical. | terms: commodity-intelligence -->
Agents break the coupling.
Software has acquired the equivalent of extremely fast automated machine tools; in the software
 factory, implementation is fabrication — the two words name the same work in its engineering role
 and in its production-system role.
And the new fabricator has a property no earlier arrangement could buy: it is inexpensive and
 capable at once.
Engineers no longer need to specify every implementation choice merely to make delegation
 economical, because a capable agent can resolve ordinary realization decisions itself.
The engineer can concentrate on the degrees of freedom whose resolution is consequential —
 behavior, architecture, resource bounds, security properties, ownership, policy — the obligations
 the organization needs to understand or control.

<!-- point: cheap-implementation-makes-structure-more-economical | Cheap implementation makes explicit engineering structure more economical, not less, because the structure no longer has to carry the whole implementation. -->
The result is a different division of labor, not the old one restored.
The engineer does not replace implementation with a complete executable specification; that would
 recreate the old economic problem in another representation.
The engineer determines or constrains the consequential choices and leaves the remaining
 realization freedom to commodity intelligence.
Cheap implementation therefore makes explicit engineering structure more economical, not less,
 because the structure no longer has to carry the whole implementation.
The factory does not eliminate intelligence from fabrication; it makes capable fabrication
 abundant.

<!-- point: decide-which-freedom-to-control | The objective is not to eliminate degrees of freedom, as the specification-heavy factory thinking drifted toward, but to decide which ones engineering must control; the tolerance is the physical ancestor of that posture. | terms: degrees-of-freedom, tolerance -->
This changes what the factory wants from degrees of freedom.
The objective is not to eliminate them; the specification-heavy factory thinking of the earlier
 attempts drifted toward exactly that.
The objective is to decide which degrees of freedom engineering must control and which it can
 economically delegate.
The tolerance is the physical ancestor of this posture, and of the margin that
 {{sec:constraints-sensors-validators-gates}} reads off a quantitative obligation: state the
 acceptable region, and allow fabrication freedom inside it.
Earlier industrialization often gained scale partly by narrowing what the individual fabricator
 needed to know.
The agentic factory strikes a different bargain: constrain the consequential degrees of freedom,
 and leave enormous numbers of inconsequential ones open to an extremely capable fabricator.

## Natural Language Shifts the Burden

<!-- point: earlier-implementers-received-more-explicit-artifacts | Historical warning: the earlier factory attempts gave implementers more explicit artifacts than contemporary coding agents receive, and remained difficult anyway. -->
The history carries a warning for the contemporary factory that runs on prompts.
Earlier attempts to separate design from implementation gave their implementation teams
 substantially more explicit artifacts than most coding agents receive today: architecture
 descriptions, interface specifications, models, detailed requirements.
The arrangement remained difficult anyway.
Those representations did not eliminate the consequential decisions encountered during
 implementation, and making them complete enough to do so consumed much of the effort the
 separation was meant to save.

<!-- point: natural-language-assumes-more-of-the-fabricator | Handing an agent a natural-language description does not escape the specification problem; it makes a stronger assumption about the fabricator, shifting the missing judgment from representation into inference. -->
A factory that instead gives a coding agent a natural-language description and expects satisfactory
 implementation has not escaped this problem.
It has made a stronger assumption about the fabricator.
The agent must infer missing relationships, recover incompletely articulated intent, and resolve
 unspecified degrees of freedom acceptably.
Reducing explicit specification does not remove the missing judgment; it shifts the burden from
 representation into inference.

<!-- point: shifting-into-inference-can-be-rational | The shift into inference is often the right economics — a cheap agent reconstructing intent in minutes is a bargain — but repeated inference costs money, variance, and durable organizational knowledge. -->
Sometimes that shift is exactly the right economics.
A two-dollar agent spending five minutes to reconstruct what an engineer would need an hour to
 model is a bargain, and commodity intelligence is why the bet can now be rational.
But repeated inference has costs of its own: model expense, retries, variance, mistakes, and the
 same intent reconstructed anew on the next task.
The least visible cost is the loss of durable organizational knowledge, because judgment that lives
 only in inference is never written anywhere the organization can keep.
At some point it becomes cheaper, or safer, to externalize the judgment.

<!-- point: two-conditions-for-natural-language | Natural language suffices where the agent can reliably reconstruct what was left unstated and the open degrees of freedom are safe to delegate; where either condition fails, the old specification problem returns. -->
The conditions can be stated exactly.
Natural language can be sufficient where the agent can reliably reconstruct what was left unstated
 and where the remaining degrees of freedom are safe to delegate.
Where either condition fails, the old specification problem returns.

## Supervision Still Reads the Artifact

<!-- point: correspondence-is-the-other-half | Delegation settles only half the factory's question; the organization must also establish that fabrication stayed within its obligations, and contemporary practice establishes this by reading the code. -->
Delegation settles only half of the factory's question.
However the judgment is divided, the organization must still establish that what was fabricated
 stayed within its obligations — the correspondence problem the earlier software factories met with
 little more than reading the implementation.
The contemporary supervisory arrangement attaches almost entirely to the fabricated artifact:
 intent goes to an agent, the agent produces code, a human reads the code, and admission follows the
 reading.

<!-- pullquote -->
> We automated fabrication while leaving supervision attached to the fabricated artifact.

<!-- point: faster-fabrication-swamps-artifact-attached-supervision | Making the agent ten times faster does not make artifact-attached supervision more capable; it produces ten times more of the thing supervision must read. -->
That is the diagnosis, and the factory's history says why it cannot hold.
Make the agent ten times faster and you have not made that supervisory system ten times more
 capable.
You have produced ten times more of the thing through which supervision must occur.
Every earlier episode ends the same way: the answer was never merely a faster fabricator, and it was
 never more heroic inspection of the fabricated artifacts.
The production system around the fabricator had to become more engineered.

## Engineering the Supervisory Layer

<!-- point: mage-components-read-as-production-engineering | Read against the factory's history, the method of the earlier chapters describes components of an industrial production system. -->
Only now, with the history in place, name the method.
Read against two centuries of production engineering, the chapters this book has already developed
 describe components of an industrial production system:

- **Modeling** ({{chapter:modeling}}) externalizes consequential engineering knowledge — analogous
  in function, not identity, to the plans, tolerances, and process models through which a physical
  factory becomes supervisable.
- **Alignment** ({{chapter:alignment}}) gives those representations consequences, through
  constraints, sensing, evidence, validation, and admission.
- **Governance conversion** changes the production environment when recurring failures or judgments
  reveal missing structure — the software form of the factory that responds to a defect by revising
  a fixture, a tolerance, or an inspection regime.
- **Skills** preserve recurring procedures, as process plans preserved routings.
- **Humans** supervise through these structures while retaining responsibility, and intervene where
  judgment remains necessary.

> **Software factory.** The engineered production system through which software is realized from
> engineering intent and evaluated, admitted, deployed, and improved.

<!-- point: the-factory-distinction-is-judgment-and-its-cost | Not every organized development process is a factory in this sense; the distinction is how much consequential judgment the production system requires the fabricator to supply anew, and at what cost the surrounding structures could carry it instead. -->
Not every organized software-development process is a factory in this sense.
The distinction is not the sophistication of the tooling, and it is not the expertise of the
 workforce; the earlier factories employed skilled implementers, and a highly capable agent can
 still serve as the fabricator in one.
The distinction is how much consequential judgment the production system requires the fabricator to
 supply anew in each act of fabrication, and whether the structures around fabrication could carry
 that judgment more cheaply.

<!-- point: mage-does-not-replace-the-factory | MAGE does not replace the software factory; it supplies representations and supervisory mechanisms through which engineers exercise informed control over automated realization. -->
The relationship between the method and the factory should be stated carefully.
MAGE does not replace the software factory.
It provides representations and supervisory mechanisms through which engineers can exercise informed
 control over increasingly automated realization.

> **Agentic software factory.** A software factory in which capable agents perform a substantial
> share of realization under engineered structures for directing, evaluating, admitting, and
> improving their work.

<!-- point: agentic-modifies-who-realizes | Agentic names who performs realization; the supervisory machinery around that realization remains part of the factory. -->
*Agentic* names who performs realization.
The supervisory machinery around that realization remains part of the factory.

<!-- point: the-factory-is-a-functional-layer | The software factory's supervisory layer is functional, not a separate technology stack; one artifact can participate in production and in supervision at once. -->
Nor is the supervisory layer another physical technology stack, a product to install beside the
 build system.
It is functional: the same artifact can participate in production and in supervision at once.
A test suite helps realize the product and constitutes evidence about it.
A schema shapes what agents produce and rejects what they must not.
A permission boundary is part of the deployment machinery and part of the control system.
The factory metaphor earns its keep precisely because it does not demand new machinery — it names
 the supervisory function that existing machinery, deliberately arranged, can carry.

<!-- point: mage-occupies-the-middle-position | The history locates MAGE between two failure modes: modeling everything recreates the old economics, and trusting natural language plus intelligence asks the fabricator to reconstruct consequential intent; model the degrees of freedom worth controlling and delegate the rest. | terms: degrees-of-freedom -->
The history also locates the method between two failure modes.
Do not model everything: that recreates the economics that made the earlier software factories
 unattractive.
Do not assume that natural language plus intelligence makes representation unnecessary: that merely
 asks the fabricator to reconstruct consequential intent, act after act.
Model and constrain the degrees of freedom worth controlling; delegate the rest.

<!-- point: modeling-and-alignment-in-factory-terms | In factory terms, Modeling represents the consequential distinctions not left to the fabricator, and Alignment establishes that fabrication stayed within the corresponding obligations. | terms: modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->
In the factory's terms, the book's two principles divide its question between them.
Modeling is how you represent the consequential distinctions you have decided not to leave to the
 fabricator.
Alignment is how you establish that fabrication stayed within the corresponding obligations.

<!-- point: per-decision-economics-points-at-theory | Decision by decision, encoding upstream competes with resolving during fabrication; when encoding costs as much as skilled implementation the separation buys nothing, and commodity intelligence lowers the fabrication side per degree of freedom. | terms: commodity-intelligence -->
The economics beneath this chapter can be stated decision by decision.
For each design decision, an organization either pays to resolve and encode it upstream or pays for
 a fabricator capable of resolving it during realization.
When encoding enough to make inexpensive implementation reliable costs about as much as the skilled
 implementation it replaces, the separation buys nothing; that was the earlier factories' position.
Commodity intelligence lowers the fabrication side of that comparison, degree of freedom by degree
 of freedom, which is why the factory can now afford to specify only what is consequential.
{{chapter:theory}} develops this account quantitatively, following what must happen for a decision
 to be encoded, interpreted, and realized.

<!-- point: factory-in-a-box-and-the-design-space | A purchased factory cannot arrive knowing an organization's consequential intent — if it works only when the software is described almost completely, the old economics are back — and the observed factories, including the model-first case, are points in this design space. -->
The same comparison frames the factory a vendor sells.
A purchased factory can arrive with a capable fabricator and generic supervisory machinery, but not
 with your organization's consequential intent; if it reliably produces your software only when you
 describe that software almost completely, the old economics have been recreated.
The factories observed in {{sec:mage-in-the-wild}} divide these costs differently — the model-first
 case among them is a point in this design space, not the destination MAGE implies.

<!-- point: history-is-grounding-not-evidence | The industrial history is grounding, not evidence for MAGE: it supplies the shape of the transition, and the warning that the solution was never merely a faster fabricator. -->
One caution, before the evidence.
The Industrial Revolution is grounding for this chapter, not proof of anything in this book.
Watt's cylinder demonstrates nothing about coding agents, and no analogy substitutes for evidence.
What the history supplies is the shape of a recurring engineering transition — when fabrication
 capacity scales beyond direct human craft, the production system must carry more of the knowledge
 and control required for supervision — and a warning drawn from every episode above: the solution
 has never been merely a faster fabricator.
Whether agentic software engineering is undergoing such a transition is a question the rest of this
 chapter puts to observed systems, beginning with one built under exactly that hypothesis.

<!-- point: the-problem-the-evidence-must-answer | The problem the evidence must answer: increasing the capacity of the fabricator does not necessarily increase the capacity of the supervisor. -->
The problem those systems face is the one this history isolates.
Increasing the capacity of the fabricator does not necessarily increase the capacity of the
 supervisor.
