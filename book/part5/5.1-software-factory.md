# The Software Factory

<!-- point: the-printer-described-a-fabricator | The printer analogy explained fabricative technology — a machine that realizes a broad range of artifacts from a description of what should be made. | terms: printer-metaphor -->
Earlier in this book, I compared coding agents to 2D and 3D printers.
The comparison was about fabricative technology: unlike a specific-purpose tool such as a stapler, a
 fabricator can realize a broad range of artifacts from a description of what should be made.

<!-- point: the-factory-is-the-analogy-for-a-means-of-production | To understand what happens when fabricative capacity becomes a means of production, a different analogy is useful: the factory. -->
To understand what happens when that capacity becomes a means of production, a different analogy is
 useful: the factory.

<!-- point: production-at-scale-moves-knowledge-into-the-environment | The useful part of the metaphor is the production system surrounding fabrication: a craftsperson carries that knowledge personally, and production at larger scale moves selected parts of it into the environment around the fabricator. -->
The useful part of the factory metaphor is the production system surrounding fabrication.
A craftsperson can carry much of the knowledge required to produce an acceptable artifact
 personally: what matters, how the work should proceed, which variations are acceptable, and whether
 the result is good enough.
Production at larger scale moves selected parts of that knowledge into the environment around the
 fabricator.

<!-- point: process-design-and-tolerance | Two developments from manufacturing carry that knowledge: process design determines how production proceeds, and tolerance determines which properties must be controlled without prescribing every detail of realization. | terms: tolerance, degrees-of-freedom -->
Two developments from manufacturing are particularly useful here:

- **Process design** determines how production proceeds. Work is sequenced, machinery and
  responsibilities are arranged, measurements occur at selected points, and acceptance is separated
  from fabrication. The process carries knowledge that otherwise would have to be reconstructed by
  each worker for each artifact.
- **Tolerance** determines which properties must be controlled without prescribing every detail of
  realization. A drawing can state a required dimension and its allowable variation. A fixture can
  constrain production toward that range. A gauge can determine whether the realized part falls
  within it. The factory need not prescribe every motion of the machinist or every physical detail of
  the part. It needs sufficient control over the properties that matter.

<!-- point: the-surrounding-machinery-scaled-manufacturing-past-craft | Drawings and tolerances represented consequential properties, machine tools and fixtures constrained fabrication, gauges made properties measurable, process plans organized work, and inspection determined what could proceed — so a defect could change the process rather than only the artifact. -->
These developments allowed manufacturing to scale beyond production governed primarily by the
 judgment of the individual craftsperson.
Drawings and tolerances represented consequential properties.
Machine tools and fixtures constrained fabrication.[cite: winchester2018]
Gauges and metrology made selected properties measurable.[cite: hounshell1984]
Process plans organized work.
Inspection and quality controls determined what could proceed.[cite: shewhart1931]
A defect could lead to a change in the process, tooling, tolerance, or inspection regime rather than
 merely repair of the defective artifact.

<!-- point: the-factory-reallocated-judgment-rather-than-eliminating-it | The result was not the elimination of skilled judgment but a different allocation of it: some decisions stayed with the fabricator, others were embodied in the production system. -->
The result was not the elimination of skilled judgment.
It was a different allocation of it.
Some decisions remained with the fabricator.
Others were embodied in the production system so that acceptable production did not require the same
 judgment to be exercised anew on every artifact.

## What a Software Factory Produces

<!-- point: what-the-software-factory-repeatedly-produces-is-change | The analogy needs one adjustment. A physical factory repeatedly produces instances of a product; a software factory repeatedly produces change — features, repairs, adaptations, migrations, and other transformations of a maintained system. -->
The analogy requires one adjustment.
A physical factory repeatedly produces instances of a product.
Software usually does not.
Another copy of a program may cost almost nothing, while a software service may consist of one
 continuously evolving system serving millions of users.
What the software factory repeatedly produces is change: features, repairs, adaptations, migrations,
 and other transformations of a maintained system.

<!-- point: a-software-factory-is-a-production-system-for-controlled-change | Change is the object named by this book's definition of software engineering, so a software factory is a production system for controlled change. -->
This is the same object named by the definition of software engineering used throughout this book.
**Software engineering is the engineering discipline of controlled change in software systems.
It creates and evolves software while preserving sufficient control over the properties and
 consequences that matter.**[cite: davis2026sehandbook]
A software factory is therefore a production system for controlled change.

> **Software factory.** The engineered production system through which software is realized from
> engineering intent and evaluated, admitted, deployed, and improved.

> **Agentic software factory.** A software factory in which capable agents perform a substantial
> share of realization under engineered structures for directing, evaluating, admitting, and
> improving their work.

<!-- point: agentic-modifies-who-realizes | Agentic names who performs realization; the supervisory machinery around that realization remains part of the factory. -->
*Agentic* names who performs realization.
The supervisory machinery around that realization remains part of the factory.

## Why Agents Change the Production System

<!-- box-family: inset -->
<!-- inset-domain: SOFTWARE ENGINEERING -->
> #### Inset — Software factories before agents {#inset-software-factories-before-agents}
>
> Software has pursued versions of the factory idea before. Software factories, model-driven development, enterprise architecture, outsourced development, build systems, test automation, continuous integration, and deployment pipelines all moved selected knowledge or work out of an individual programmer and into a larger production system. But software retained an unusual coupling between engineering and fabrication. A skilled programmer did more than mechanically realize a completed design. While implementing, the programmer routinely resolved open questions about behavior, structure, interfaces, failure handling, and performance.
>
> Earlier attempts to separate these roles therefore faced an awkward tradeoff. Making downstream implementation mechanical required engineers to determine and communicate many of those decisions beforehand; leaving them open required a downstream implementer capable of exercising the missing judgment. Software factories were always organizationally possible, but transferring enough judgment out of fabrication could cost as much as leaving that judgment with the skilled fabricator.

<!-- point: commodity-intelligence-changes-the-tradeoff | Commodity intelligence changes the tradeoff: the new fabricator is inexpensive and capable at once, so the production system can constrain the consequential properties and leave the agent substantial freedom over realization. | terms: commodity-intelligence, degrees-of-freedom -->
Commodity intelligence changes this tradeoff.
The new fabricator can be both inexpensive and capable.
An engineer need not specify every realization choice merely to delegate implementation to an agent.
The production system can instead constrain the consequential properties and leave the agent
 substantial freedom over realization.
In the language of {{chapter:modeling}} and {{chapter:alignment}}, the engineer can decide which
 degrees of freedom must be controlled and which can safely remain open.

<!-- point: process-design-is-also-an-organizational-idea | Process design is also an organizational idea: organizations coordinate work through policies, procedures, roles, delegated authority, and accountability, and those mechanisms operate on people who have reasons to respond to them. -->
Process design is also an organizational idea.
Large organizations have long coordinated work through policies, procedures, roles, delegated
 authority, and systems of accountability rather than requiring managers to supervise every act
 directly.
These mechanisms operate on people who have reasons to respond to them.[cite: marchsimon1958]
Employees can be rewarded, promoted, disciplined, fired, held professionally responsible, or, where
 the stakes warrant it, subjected to legal sanction.

<!-- point: an-agent-cannot-close-the-control-loop-through-accountability | A coding agent occupies a different position in the production system: instructions shape its behavior, but the organization cannot rely on motivation or accountability to close the control loop, so for consequential properties tolerance supplies the complementary mechanism. | terms: tolerance -->
A coding agent occupies a different position in the production system.
Claude and Codex do not desire to do good work, pursue promotion, fear dismissal, or bear
 responsibility for what they produce.
Instructions and policies can shape their behavior, and better context can improve their
 interpretation, but the organization cannot rely on motivation or accountability to close the
 control loop.
For consequential properties, some of that burden must therefore move into the production system
 itself.
Tolerance provides the complementary mechanism: represent the bounds that matter, measure the
 realized artifact against them, and reject production that falls outside them.

## Engineering the Supervisory Layer

<!-- section-terms: tolerance -->

<!-- point: tolerance-is-a-purposeful-reduction | A tolerance is a model of what matters about a part for a particular engineering purpose — a purposeful reduction, not a complete description. | terms: tolerance -->
Follow tolerance one step further and it becomes a form of representation.
Once a consequential property can be stated as an allowable range, the factory no longer needs a
 master craftsperson to decide anew whether every part fits well enough.
A tolerance is a model of what matters about a part for a particular engineering purpose.
Not a complete description of the part.
A purposeful reduction.
Readers of {{chapter:modeling}} will recognize the claim; here it arrives from production rather
 than from method.

<!-- point: mage-components-read-as-production-engineering | Read as production engineering, the method supplies what process design does not: Modeling represents consequential properties as tolerances do, Alignment measures and enforces them, degrees of freedom name the permitted variation, and conversion changes the factory when a defect exposes missing structure. | terms: modeling-principle, alignment-principle, degrees-of-freedom -->
Process design directs production; it does not say which realizations are acceptable.
That is where this book's method enters the factory argument, because its components describe
 parts of a production system:

- **Modeling** ({{chapter:modeling}}) represents the consequential properties the fabricator must
  not resolve on its own — analogous in function, not identity, to the drawings, tolerances, and
  process models through which a physical factory becomes supervisable.
- **Alignment** ({{chapter:alignment}}) supplies the corresponding measurement and enforcement,
  through constraints, sensing, evidence, validation, and admission.
- **Degrees of freedom** name the permitted variation — everything a tolerance deliberately leaves
  open to the fabricator.
- **Governance conversion** changes the production environment when recurring failures reveal
  missing structure, as a factory answers a defect by revising a fixture, a tolerance, or an
  inspection regime; **skills** preserve recurring procedures, as process plans preserved routings.
- **Humans** supervise through these structures while retaining responsibility, and intervene where
  judgment remains necessary.

<!-- point: mage-does-not-replace-the-factory | MAGE does not replace the software factory; it supplies representations and supervisory mechanisms through which engineers exercise informed control over automated realization. -->
The relationship between the method and the factory should be stated carefully.
MAGE does not replace the software factory.
It provides representations and supervisory mechanisms through which engineers can exercise informed
 control over increasingly automated realization.

<!-- point: the-factory-is-a-functional-layer | The supervisory layer is functional, not a separate technology stack; one artifact can participate in production and in supervision at once. -->
Nor is the supervisory layer another technology stack, a product to install beside the build system.
It is functional: the same artifact can participate in production and in supervision at once.
A test suite helps realize the product and constitutes evidence about it.
A schema shapes what agents produce and rejects what they must not.
A permission boundary is part of the deployment machinery and part of the control system.

<!-- point: the-factory-distinction-is-judgment-and-its-cost | Not every organized development process is a factory in this sense; the distinction is how much consequential judgment the production system requires the fabricator to supply anew, and at what cost the surrounding structures could carry it instead. -->
Not every organized software-development process is a factory in this sense.
The distinction is not the sophistication of the tooling, and it is not the expertise of the
 workforce; the earlier factories employed skilled implementers, and a highly capable agent can
 still serve as the fabricator in one.
The distinction is how much consequential judgment the production system requires the fabricator to
 supply anew in each act of fabrication, and whether the structures around fabrication could carry
 that judgment more cheaply.

<!-- point: mage-occupies-the-middle-position | The method sits between two failure modes: modeling everything recreates the economics that defeated the earlier software factories, and trusting natural language plus intelligence asks the fabricator to reconstruct consequential intent act after act. | terms: degrees-of-freedom -->
Two failure modes sit on either side of that position.
Do not model everything: that recreates the economics that made the earlier software factories
 unattractive.
Do not assume that natural language plus intelligence makes representation unnecessary: that merely
 asks the fabricator to reconstruct consequential intent, act after act.
Model and constrain the degrees of freedom worth controlling; delegate the rest.
