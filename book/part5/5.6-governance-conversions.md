# Conversions in Practice

The method's third beat — convert recurring failures into engineering capital — is a claim, and
this chapter is where you watch it run at the source. Every elegant mechanism in the built system
began life as an ugly, recurring problem. DocAble is where the loop was first turned, so its

full: the failure, the mechanism it forced into being, and the capital that mechanism banked. Read
them as the raw material of governance conversion — the place the aphorisms were paid for.

## The instruction satisfied to the letter

<!-- point: an-instruction-satisfied-to-the-letter-and-voided-in-spirit | An under-specified instruction is satisfied to the letter and voided in spirit. | terms: stringly-typed, explicitness-is-essential -->
**Problem.** By April the system was 300,000 lines that worked but were janky — a Rube Goldberg
machine, because it had started as an MVP built only to prove the thing was possible at all. I had
imposed real architecture on the parts I knew were sensitive; the rest — the web layer, the front
end — I hoped the agent would get right, and they turned crufty.

**Initial attempt.** So I told it to use typed Python. It did, in a sense: it used the type
`string` for everything. Stringly-typed — the word "type" satisfied to the letter and voided in
spirit, no safety the compiler could give. Told to retrofit real types, it turned on the checker
and made everything strings and ints, without building the abstractions I actually wanted. The
same thing happened porting JavaScript to TypeScript that week: each ported the cheapest way,
because the agent had no model and did not know the names of things.

**Why it failed.** The failure is not laziness. It is the shape of an under-specified instruction
met by a system that optimizes for the next green checkmark. "Use types" names a checkbox, not the
abstractions, and the agent found the reading that turned the check green with the least work.

<!-- point: the-fix-was-a-typed-model-per-dense-region-and-lints-to-blocking | The fix: a structured model per dense region, every commodity lint driven to blocking. | terms: audits-into-lints, make-lint-blocking, dense-primitive-region -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The fix was the recipe the method chapters describe, run on my own
codebase. I turned on every lint from Ruff, Pylint, and ESLint — hundreds of commodity smell
checks — and burned weeks squashing them, making each blocking as it hit zero so it could never
come back. Then I worked region by region: find the dense primitive regions — the stretches doing
many low-level operations by hand, raw calls and untyped strings and ints passed around with no
abstraction that owns them — and induce a structured model that owns each one, rewriting the region
to route through it. Each lint driven to zero was one primitive smell given a name and a home; the
coarse model-view-controller decomposition was the same move at the coarse grain.

<!-- point: structure-is-compression-and-the-models-now-shape-every-task | Structure is compression; the models now shape every later task. | terms: governance-conversion, brownfield-migration -->
**General lesson.** A month later I had 200,000 lines that were not janky. The system got smaller
as it got better — structure is compression, and the primitive sprawl had been the bloat. The
project's measured churn corroborates the shape: the add-and-delete peaked exactly during this
work, then the deletions collapsed and later windows went net-additive — the signature of a
one-time replacement, not steady thrashing. [data: churn] In MAGE terms, that month was governance
conversion at brownfield scale. The recurring failures and the same rediscoveries, over and over,
became the models, links, lints, and gates that now shape every later task. The capital was not the
200,000 lines. It was the enforcement that holds the models, the architecture, and the code
together every time the code changes.

## The architecture was the constraint

<!-- point: optimizing-within-an-architecture-that-was-itself-the-constraint | I optimized within an architecture that was itself the constraint. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
<!-- index-def: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
**Problem.** The service was costing thirty to forty dollars a day to run idle on a Kubernetes
cluster.

**Initial attempt.** So I set the agents on a scale-to-zero push: keep the cluster, keep the custom
autoscaler, and squeeze the idle pods down to nothing. For weeks they made it better and better.

**Why it failed.** It never paid off, because scaling a cold pod back up took sixty to three
hundred seconds, and a five-minute cold start is a worse problem than the bill. I was optimizing
*within* the architecture when the architecture itself was the constraint. The real move was to
change it: go serverless, and let the platform own the scaling I had been hand-building against its
own ceiling.

<!-- point: a-recurring-wall-is-the-signal-to-price-a-paradigm-change | A fix that keeps hitting the same wall is the signal to price a paradigm change. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
**Mechanism introduced.** Coding agents accelerate within the frame you give them, even when the
frame is wrong, so a wrong frame gets very good very fast. This one had a tell, visible only in
hindsight: the optimization kept colliding with the *same* wall. When an in-paradigm fix keeps
hitting the paradigm's own limit, that recurring wall is the mechanism the failure hands you — not
a lint, but a decision rule. Price out the paradigm change instead of buying another round of
in-frame optimization.

<!-- point: some-conversions-produce-judgment-not-machinery | Some conversions bank judgment, not machinery. | terms: governance-conversion -->
**General lesson.** The capital here is not a wall the environment enforces; some conversions
produce judgment, not machinery. What those weeks bought was a cheap detector for a wrong frame: a
fix that recurs against the same ceiling is evidence about the ceiling, not the fix. It is the same
signal, one level up, as the second occurrence of a failure earning its lint — the recurrence is
the information.

## The decision left open

<!-- point: an-unmade-decision-is-where-the-agent-guesses-worst | The unmade decision is where the agent guesses worst. | terms: optionality-is-poison -->
<!-- index-def: optionality-is-poison -->
**Problem.** My merge-train cron kept stalling when two agents edited the same file.

**Initial attempt.** It proposed a one-flag fix: tell the merge to just take one side and drop the
other on any conflict. I signed off without asking what "drop the other side" meant.

**Why it failed.** For four days it silently deleted real work from the main branch every time two
changes overlapped — the same commit heading landing again and again, each time a little smaller —
while I chased the symptoms one missing function at a time. The failure was not the flag. It was
the decision I left open. The agent filled it with the cheapest thing that made the error go away,
because an open choice at the point of execution becomes a choice someone has to make now, and the
agent makes it to unblock itself, with the consequences nowhere in its objective.

<!-- point: decide-conflict-handling-up-front-name-the-resolver | Decide conflict handling up front; name the resolver. | terms: optionality-is-poison, explicitness-is-essential -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The right answer was a merge resolver that understood the files it was
merging, and I could have decided that up front. The mechanism the failure earned is the discipline
of naming the consequential dimensions before execution — the architecture, the types, and,
precisely here, what happens on a conflict — and delegating only the rest. The narrower the agent's
search, the more of its speed lands on the target instead of scattering.

<!-- point: the-capital-was-a-conflict-policy-the-environment-now-carries | The capital was a conflict policy the environment now carries. | terms: governance-conversion, judgment-into-infrastructure -->
**General lesson.** What the four days bought was a standing rule: no destructive default on a
conflict, and a resolver that reads the files instead of picking a side. The decision, made once
and encoded, is one no later agent has to re-guess at midnight. That is the move the whole method
turns on — convert the judgment into infrastructure so the call is made once and held.

## "Done" was a claim

<!-- point: two-green-reports-that-were-not-true | Two green reports, neither of them true. | terms: done-is-a-claim -->
<!-- index-def: done-is-a-claim -->
**Problem.** Two incidents, close together, made "done is a claim" non-negotiable.

**Initial attempt.** I trusted the reports. Once, a lint gate hit a stray error, caught it in a
catch-all that returned success, and let a run of commits land completely unlinted while the log
said fine. Another time a chain of pin tests passed green.

**Why it failed.** A botched merge had landed those pin tests onto a tree that was missing the very
field they were supposed to check, so they confirmed a field that existed and never touched the one
that mattered. Both reported done. Neither was. These are agent-shaped failures: a report of "done"
describes the agent's intent, not the state of the repository, and the gap between the two is
exactly where a confident green hides.

<!-- point: the-mechanism-was-a-gate-the-agent-cannot-talk-past | The mechanism: a gate the agent cannot talk its way past. | terms: done-is-a-claim, audits-into-lints -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The mechanism both failures earned is verification the agent cannot
author: re-run the tests yourself, re-run the lints the work was supposed to satisfy, compare the
counts against the claim. The catch-all taught a sharper rule — a gate that swallows its own error
is worse than no gate, because it reports success it did not earn, so a control must fail loud and
never silent-catch. The pin tests taught that a check is only as trustworthy as the tree it ran
against.

<!-- point: the-capital-was-a-control-system-around-the-work | The capital was a control system around the work, not more code. | terms: governance-conversion, judgment-into-infrastructure -->
**General lesson.** Out of these, my job changed shape. It shifted from writing implementation to
running the control system around it — decomposing work, cross-checking invariants, hardening
tests, re-running the gates against the real tree, and deciding whether the architecture was
genuinely cleaner or merely different. The capital is that control system: the standing habit and
machinery that treat every "done" as the start of verification, not the end.

## A content-free detector, measured on the wrong world {#fieldnote-content-free-detector}

<!-- point: a-detector-that-measured-the-right-quantity-on-the-wrong-world | A detector measured the right quantity on the wrong world. | terms: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
<!-- index-def: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
**Problem.** Late in the same stretch, an agent wrote me a content-free detector: if an image's
pixels barely vary, skip the vision model and mark it decorative. Clean, typed, careful.

**Initial attempt.** Its own comment promised a false positive was "not possible" — real photos and
charts vary twenty times more than the cutoff, so nothing real could slip under. The margin was
true.

**Why it failed.** It was measured on the wrong world. Hand it a Rothko, or Malevich's black
square, and the pixels barely vary; the detector marks the painting decorative and drops it, and a
blind student reading an art book gets silence where the subject should be. No threshold saves you,
because a blank spacer and a Malevich are identical in the one quantity the code looked at.
"Content-free" was never a fact about pixels. It is a fact about an image in a context of use, and
the use here is teaching, and teaching includes the one tradition built to prove that a picture
need not vary. The confident "not possible" was the tell: it marks the spot where the model's
picture of its own inputs was too small.

<!-- point: when-the-cheap-proxy-is-unsure-describe-anyway | When the cheap proxy is unsure, describe anyway. | terms: tests-for-agent-failure-modes -->
**Mechanism introduced.** The mechanism is a rule about proxies: when the cheap proxy is unsure,
describe the image anyway. A content-free detector that has never seen a Rothko will tell you a
Rothko is nothing, so the detector does not get the last word — it defers to the fuller model at
the margin. And the test that guards it is built for the agent's failure mode, not a human's: pin
the class — the picture that varies little but carries everything — rather than the example.

**General lesson.** Build tests for the failure modes the agent actually has, not the ones a person
would. The next confident, coherent, subtly-wrong subsystem should meet a sensor instead of a
checkbox.

## Lessons that recur without a single scene

<!-- point: some-lessons-recur-across-every-failure | Some lessons recur across every failure, not one. | terms: governance-conversion -->
Four of the aphorisms are not paid for by any single failure above — they run underneath all of
them. They carry here as lines; the method chapter earns them in full.

- **Vibe-coding treats the program as an I/O device.** Every failure above shared a root: evidence
  taken from the outside — does the demo run, does the output look right — while the thing that
  mattered was structural and unseen. Engineering treats the program as a system and checks the
  invariant, not just the output.
- **What lives only in your head is invisible to the machine.** The stringly-typed sprawl, the
  unnamed conflict policy, the too-small picture of the detector's inputs — each was a structure
  that lived in my head and never in the environment. Make it explicit, queryable, executable, or
  the agent cannot hold it.
- **Soft aims; hard holds.** Most of these conversions ended by moving a failure from a convention
  that aims to a mechanism that holds. When a promotion is owed and how far right it belongs is the
  subject of [Governance Conversion](4.5-governance-conversion.html), not this chapter.
- **The second time a failure shows up, it has earned its mechanism.** None of the five was
  converted on its first appearance; each earned its wall by recurring. The loop that reads the
  recurrence and prices the mechanism is stated in full in the method chapter.

## What the failures taught

<!-- point: amplification-is-neutral-judgment-is-scarce | Amplification is neutral; judgment is the scarce resource. | terms: judgment-into-infrastructure -->
Step back from the five. What they share is not a technology but a posture. The agent amplified
whatever judgment I brought — good and bad, with equal enthusiasm — so every failure here was a
place my own judgment had been thin, and every mechanism was that judgment made explicit and
permanent. Amplification is neutral. It moved the scarce resource from implementation, which became
cheap, to judgment, which did not.

<!-- point: a-single-case-field-report-part-6-takes-the-question-up | A single-case field report; Part 6 takes the question up. | terms: judgment-into-infrastructure, field-report -->
That is where this field report hands off. My evidence is a single-person project without real
customers, run against one vendor's models, and the specifics will not all generalize. But the
shape felt durable across every failure above: convert judgment into infrastructure, let the
machinery hold what your attention cannot, and spend your scarce judgment on the calls the
machinery cannot make. What that does to the shape of the profession — the roles, the org chart,
who is trusted to build the walls, and who in an organization is even empowered to convert a
recurring failure into shared practice — is the question Part VI takes up next.
