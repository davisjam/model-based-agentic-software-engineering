The system is [DocAble]({{product_url}}), live in beta at [scholaccess.com]({{product_url}}). It takes

## A real law, a real deadline

<!-- section-terms: doj-2024-rule, wcag-conformance -->

<!-- point: title-ii-now-means-digital | Title II now governs a university's digital communication. | terms: ada-title-ii -->
The Americans with Disabilities Act requires a public university to communicate with people who have
disabilities as effectively as it communicates with everyone else. That obligation is old; what
changed is where communication lives. Teaching has moved into digital artifacts — PDFs, slide decks,
Word documents, spreadsheets, learning-management systems — and materials a screen reader cannot read,
or that carry their meaning only through color or layout, hand some students a worse version of the
university than others. The hazard is legal as well as moral: institutions have settled accessibility
suits for sums in the {{lawsuit_settlement_range}} [cite: CarlsonHigherEdAccessibilityLawsuits].

<!-- point: doj-rule-made-access-part-of-service | The 2024 DOJ rule made access part of the service. | terms: doj-2024-rule, digital-sediment -->
For most of that history universities complied reactively: a student hit an inaccessible document,
requested an accommodation, and the institution scrambled to respond — the burden of discovering the
barrier falling on the person it had already excluded. In 2024 the Department of Justice changed the
shape. A final rule set a blunt premise: if a public entity provides a service through a website, an
app, or the documents distributed through them, the accessibility of that content is *part of the
service*. It set a technical standard — {{doj_technical_standard}} — and, for large institutions, a
deadline. The burden moved from the excluded student to the institution, and from the single flagged
artifact to everything already published. Universities are not sitting on three PDFs and a cheerful
little homepage. They sit on decades of accumulated digital sediment: course sites, admissions forms,
scanned readings, lecture videos. The deadline landed on a mountain of files no one had ever been
asked to climb.

Each issue takes a couple of minutes to fix at best, and the denser the figure, the longer the fix.
Scale one deck to a course and one teaching load's materials run to over {{diy_cost_per_course}} of
faculty time; scale that to the thousands of courses at one institution and the number stops being a
budget line and starts being a reason the whole thing gets quietly deferred. Every escape hatch closes
on inspection: de-digitizing satisfies the letter of the rule and betrays its spirit; external vendors
quote on the order of {{vendor_cost_per_page}} a page and are not staffed to annotate graduate-level
astrophysics; and the faculty who hold the subject knowledge are promoted on research, not on a large,
uncompensated, recurring remediation chore. What is left is a genuine, unmanufactured problem — a
federal mandate with real consequences, a mountain of heterogeneous files, expertise only the busiest
people possess, and no affordable path from here to there.

## Why a document is not just a file

<!-- section-terms: vision-language-model, semantic-gap -->

<!-- point: a-document-is-a-semantic-artifact | A document is a visual and semantic artifact. | terms: semantic-artifact -->
Underneath the institutional bind sits a technical problem deeper than it looks — the reason this
became a software project worth building rather than a policy memo worth writing. A document is not
merely a file. It is a *visual and semantic* artifact: text, figures, tables, reading order,
hierarchy, emphasis — the small tricks by which humans convey meaning to other humans. To make it
accessible, a machine has to recover enough of that meaning to present it through another channel: to
a screen reader, to a keyboard, to a student who cannot see the figure the lecturer drew.

<!-- point: vlms-recover-document-meaning | Vision-language models now recover a slide's semantic content. | terms: vision-language-model -->
<!-- point: capability-is-not-a-system | Raw model capability is not a compliance strategy. | terms: semantic-gap, demoable-vs-productizable, infinite-midwit -->
Earlier systems could automate fragments of the task — pulling text, flagging a missing title,
generating thin alt text — but general semantic recovery from an arbitrary instructional slide stayed
too weak. The turn came with frontier vision-language models. Feed one a rendered slide and it
describes the figure, reads the equation, names what the slide is trying to say. That capability is
not a compliance strategy, though, and this is the gap the engineering had to fill. The model
understands the slide and understands nothing about university procurement, disability-resource
workflows, or faculty incentives — fluent knowledge, no situated judgment; an infinite midwit that
knows everything and understands nothing. A flash of capability is not a system. You get one only by
bounding what you ask, typing what comes back, and validating before you believe.

<!-- point: novelty-and-consequence-both-raise-required-structure | Novelty and consequence both raise the structure you owe. | terms: novelty-axis, demoable-vs-productizable, assurance-criticality, thesis-modeling -->
And DocAble sat far from anything the model had already seen. It combined two properties that make
weakly governed generation particularly risky: the domain was poorly represented by familiar
application patterns, and the consequence of a silent error was high. The agents were fluent with
ordinary web infrastructure and repeatedly struggled with the internals of PDF and OpenXML — the same
split the *novelty axis* predicts, familiar classes cheap to vary on, unfamiliar ones not. Whatever
portion of that gap came from training-data fit rather than intrinsic complexity, the engineering
consequence was the same: the unfamiliar portions required substantially more explicit structure. This
is why a working demo settled nothing. On novel, consequential ground an agent's prototype is
*demoable, not productizable* — and the distance between the two is exactly the modeling and the
checks a skilled engineer still has to supply.

<!-- point: context-is-law-deadline-cost-and-gap | The context: real law, deadline, cost, bridgeable gap. | terms: document-accessibility, semantic-gap -->
That is the bar. A real law, a real deadline, a real cost, and a real technical gap that had just
barely become bridgeable — on ground unfamiliar enough that a wrong answer was both easy to produce
and expensive to ship. The case demanded both extraordinary implementation velocity and unusually
strong assurance. The rest of Part V is what the system did against it.
