[DocAble]({{product_url}}), now deployed in beta at [scholaccess.com]({{product_url}}), accepts
PowerPoint, Word, Excel, and PDF documents and remediates them toward the accessibility standards a
public university must meet. This chapter sets the bar before discussing agents or method: why the
workload was large enough to demand automation, why document remediation requires semantic judgment
rather than simple file rewriting, and why a plausible model output was nowhere near sufficient
evidence of a correct result.

## A real law, a real deadline

<!-- section-terms: doj-2024-rule, wcag-conformance -->

<!-- point: title-ii-now-means-digital | Title II now governs a university's digital communication. | terms: ada-title-ii -->
The Americans with Disabilities Act requires a public university to communicate with people who have
disabilities as effectively as it communicates with everyone else. That obligation is old; what
changed is where communication lives. Teaching has moved into digital artifacts — PDFs, slide decks,
Word documents, spreadsheets, learning-management systems — and materials a screen reader cannot read,
or that carry their meaning only through color or layout, hand some students a worse version of the
university than others.  The obligation is legal
as well as practical: inaccessible digital content exposes institutions to complaints, litigation,
and remediation costs — settlements have reached sums in the {{lawsuit_settlement_range}}
[cite: CarlsonHigherEdAccessibilityLawsuits].

<!-- point: doj-rule-made-access-part-of-service | The 2024 DOJ rule made access part of the service. | terms: doj-2024-rule, digital-sediment -->
For most of that history universities complied reactively: a student hit an inaccessible document,
requested an accommodation, and the institution scrambled to respond — the burden of discovering the
barrier falling on the person it had already excluded. In 2024 the Department of Justice changed the
shape. A final rule set a blunt premise: if a public entity provides a service through a website, an
app, or the documents distributed through them, the accessibility of that content is *part of the
service*. It set a technical standard — {{doj_technical_standard}} — and, for large institutions, a
deadline. The burden moved from the excluded student to the institution, and from the single flagged
artifact to everything already published. Universities are not sitting on three PDFs and a cheerful
little homepage. They sit on decades of accumulated digital sediment: course sites, admissions forms,
scanned readings, lecture videos. The deadline landed on a mountain of files no one had ever been
asked to climb.

Even routine findings take time, and semantically dense figures take more. Multiplied across a course
and then across an institution, the manual burden becomes large enough to explain why remediation is
so often deferred.[^cost-receipt] Outsourcing does not make the semantic problem disappear: a vendor
can repair structure, but a graduate-level figure may require subject knowledge only the instructor
possesses. The resulting bind was genuine — large heterogeneous inventories, specialized judgment, a
compliance deadline, and no manual path that scaled comfortably to the estate.

## Why a document is not just a file

<!-- section-terms: vision-language-model, semantic-gap -->

<!-- point: a-document-is-a-semantic-artifact | A document is a visual and semantic artifact. | terms: semantic-artifact -->
Underneath the institutional bind sits a technical problem deeper than it looks — the reason this
became a software project worth building rather than a policy memo worth writing. A document is not
merely a file. It is a *visual and semantic* artifact: text, figures, tables, reading order,
hierarchy, emphasis — the small tricks by which humans convey meaning to other humans. To make it
accessible, a machine has to recover enough of that meaning to present it through another channel: to
a screen reader, to a keyboard, to a student who cannot see the figure the lecturer drew.

<!-- point: vlms-changed-the-boundary-not-the-system | VLMs changed the feasible boundary; they did not make a production system. | terms: vision-language-model, demoable-vs-productizable -->
Earlier systems could automate pieces of the problem: extract text, flag a missing title, detect
structural defects, generate limited descriptions. Frontier vision-language models changed the
feasible boundary. A model could look at a rendered instructional slide, recover text and visual
relationships, and often describe what the figure was trying to communicate. That capability made a
new class of automation plausible. It did not make a production accessibility system.

<!-- point: the-missing-work-was-engineering | The missing work was engineering around the model. | terms: semantic-gap, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
The missing work was engineering. A model that can interpret a slide does not thereby know which parts
of an output may change, how accessibility findings map to standards, which transformations preserve
document semantics, what evidence should justify admission, or when a decision still requires human
judgment. Those obligations live in the system around the model. Commodity intelligence supplied a
powerful new component; representation, evidence, authority, and operating structure made the
component usable inside a consequential system.

<!-- point: an-uneven-capability-surface-raised-required-structure | An uneven capability surface raised the structure the system owed. | terms: novelty-axis, demoable-vs-productizable, assurance-criticality -->
DocAble also crossed an uneven capability surface. The agents handled ordinary web and cloud
infrastructure fluently and struggled much more with PDF and OpenXML internals. Whatever portion of
that difference came from training-data fit, implementation complexity, or both, the engineering
consequence was observable: unfamiliar and consequential regions demanded more explicit
representation, more independent evidence, and more deliberate human judgment. A demo therefore
settled very little. The bar was a real obligation, real cost, heterogeneous files, and silent errors
expensive enough that plausible output was not enough. The rest of Part V is what the system did
against it.
