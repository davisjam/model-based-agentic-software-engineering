# The Road to MAGE

> ### This chapter illustrates {#illustrates}
>
> ✓ The Modeling Thesis · ✓ The Alignment Thesis · ✓ The Governed Engineering Environment · ✓ Residual Human Judgment

<!-- point: chapter-asks-what-changed-in-the-division-of-labor | The Build gave chronology; this chapter asks what changed in the division of engineering labor, and reconstructs the delegation staircase. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The Build established chronology and scale. This chapter asks what changed in the
division of engineering labor as the environment matured. I will show the
destination once, then trace a few structures whose histories matter, and finally
reconstruct the delegation staircase: the sequence by which work moved out of
direct human inspection and into a combination of explicit models, environmental
authority, and residual human judgment.

<!-- point: a-snapshot-makes-architecture-look-inevitable | A snapshot makes architecture look inevitable; the history shows which structures were designed, forced, or hardened, and what each let the engineer stop doing by hand. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The distinction matters. A snapshot of the finished system can make its
architecture look inevitable. The history shows which structures were designed
cleanly, which were forced by failure, which later had to be hardened, and what
each change allowed the engineer to stop doing by hand.

## What ultimately got built

<!-- section-terms: governance-target-product, thesis-modeling -->

<!-- point: system-remediates-end-to-end | One upload crosses six product responsibilities and returns with standards findings, fidelity evidence, and provenance. | terms: governance-target-product -->
A user uploads a file. The front door authenticates the request, meters quota, and
queues the job; a dispatcher divides the document into chunks; the remediation core
applies deterministic rules or bounded model calls through structured document
representations; the check engine evaluates remaining findings against accessibility
standards; a fidelity validator looks for content lost while accessibility improved;
and provenance records what changed. The corrected artifact returns with the evidence
those mechanisms produced. [ref:system-overview] gives the path.

<!-- label: system-overview -->
<!-- figure: assets/system-overview.svg | *What Got Built.* One upload crosses six product responsibilities and returns with standards findings, fidelity evidence, and provenance. Probabilistic interpretation is bounded inside the remediation core; deterministic machinery owns the surrounding workflow and the checks it can actually decide. -->

<!-- point: the-environment-around-the-product | The larger engineering environment around the product path contains the models, tests, and machinery that made this degree of delegation possible. | terms: governed-engineering-environment -->
That is the product path. The larger engineering environment around it — the subject
of the previous chapter — contains the models, tests, lints, orchestration, and other
machinery that made this degree of delegation possible.

<!-- point: docable-narrowed-repair-into-a-small-action-surface | DocAble narrowed document repair into a small action surface and used both sides of the Alignment Thesis. | terms: thesis-alignment, governance-target-product -->
Read across those responsibilities, the product path shows one deliberate direction. DocAble
narrowed document repair into a deliberately small action surface. Models could propose bounded
remediation verbs; typed document models owned every mutation; the system progressively removed
direct access to the lower-level format libraries underneath; and fidelity validators checked
properties that no single step could guarantee locally. The architecture therefore used both sides
of the Alignment Thesis. It made invalid actions unavailable where the action space could be closed,
and it independently validated properties — the preservation of source content chief among them —
that only became legible once a transformation had already run.

## The model as a bounded subroutine {#model-as-bounded-subroutine}

<!-- section-terms: probabilistic-component, llm-as-function-call, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: model-is-a-bounded-subroutine | Deterministic code decides when interpretation is needed, packages a bounded input, requires a typed output, and evaluates the candidate before incorporating it. | terms: llm-as-function-call, deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->
DocAble uses frontier models where the task genuinely requires probabilistic
interpretation, but it does not let that component own the workflow around itself.
Deterministic code decides when interpretation is needed, packages a bounded input,
requires a typed output, and evaluates the candidate against whatever obligations the
environment can decide mechanically before incorporating it.

<!-- point: the-answer-is-a-candidate-not-a-verdict | The model's answer is a candidate; the trust boundary is only as strong as the obligations the validator can actually evaluate, and semantic residuals remain probabilistic or human. | terms: probabilistic-component, trust-boundary -->
The model's answer is therefore a **candidate**, not a verdict. A schema can reject
malformed output; a standards rule can reject a mechanically invalid claim; a fidelity
check can catch some classes of destructive transformation. None of those proves that
an alt-text description perfectly captures the meaning of a figure. The trust boundary
is only as strong as the obligations the validator can actually evaluate. Semantic
residuals remain probabilistic or human. [ref:llm-function-call] draws the caller, the
typed contract, and the validator.

<!-- point: proof-checking-analogy-is-useful-but-limited | The analogy to proof checking is useful but limited: the validator is not a proof kernel; its value is that the producing component does not define success for itself. | terms: llm-as-function-call -->
The architectural analogy to proof checking is useful but limited: separate the
powerful producer from the smaller mechanism that decides what evidence is sufficient
for admission [cite: ringer2019qed]. The validator here is not a proof kernel and does
not make probabilistic semantics formally true. Its value is that the producing
component does not get to define success for itself.

<!-- label: llm-function-call -->
<!-- figure: assets/llm-as-function-call.svg | *Model as a Bounded Subroutine.* A deterministic caller supplies a typed task; the model returns a typed candidate; independent validation rejects failures covered by the implemented obligations before the candidate is incorporated. Properties outside those checks remain outside the guarantee. -->

<!-- point: agents-are-the-same-boundary-at-a-larger-grain | The coding agents pose the same trust-boundary problem at a larger grain, but the full MAGE response adds more than typed I/O. | terms: thesis-alignment, probabilistic-component -->
The coding agents that built DocAble pose the same trust-boundary problem at a larger
grain, but the full MAGE response adds more than typed I/O: explicit system
representations, authority at appropriate boundaries, independent evidence, and
governance conversion. You have seen the move in the small, on a vision model writing
alt text. The rest of the book adds the structure that typed input and a validator do
not, by themselves, supply.

## Some structure banked options; some was forced by failure

<!-- section-terms: reactive-seam, edit-language, transformation, governance-conversion, thesis-modeling -->

<!-- point: not-every-structure-was-born-from-failure | Some consequential structure banked an option it was never built for; these are dividends of prior structure, not evidence every abstraction must be bought by an incident. | terms: reactive-seam, edit-language -->
Not every consequential structure was born from failure. The backend became reactive
and stateless early: work moved through queues, workers carried no durable truth, and
a fan-in step assembled the result. That choice later made the migration from a
cluster that billed around the clock to a serverless carrier much cheaper than it
might have been; the business logic largely survived while the deployment carrier
changed. Likewise, the document editor routed both human gestures and automated
remediation through one closed edit vocabulary over the same document representation.
That seam later supported a second producer without creating a second mutation path.
These were **dividends of prior structure**, not evidence that every useful
abstraction must first be purchased by an incident.

<!-- point: other-seams-begin-with-a-failure | Other seams begin with a concrete failure and let us trace the response in layers; the finished boundary hides the sequence that produced it. | terms: governance-conversion -->
Other seams were different. Their histories begin with a concrete failure and let us
trace the response in layers. Those are more informative for the method, because the
finished boundary hides the sequence that produced it.

<!-- point: a-convenient-call-silently-corrupted-the-product | A convenient one-line call stripped the accessibility structure the product exists to add, and nothing caught it. | terms: thesis-modeling -->
**The format seam.** An early path used a convenient scripting-language library to
slice a document. The operation worked visually and silently destroyed the internal
tag structure carrying the accessibility the product existed to add. The failure
exposed two missing things: an architectural decision — one canonical implementation
should own mutation of that format — and environmental authority capable of keeping
other paths from bypassing it.

<!-- point: the-format-seam-hardened-in-layers | The response came in layers, and the sharpest was the enforcement itself mis-reporting green; the model stated the boundary, the API and check gave it authority, and the authority still had to be maintained. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The response came in layers, and [ref:pdf-seam] draws the climb. A policy named the
canonical library. A typed format model and API concentrated mutation through one
seam. A build-time check refused raw access from elsewhere. Then the check itself
failed: a stale repository root caused real findings to be reported as green. That
second-order incident forced the gate to be hardened too. What the seam *is* today
belongs to [Modeling Structure and Ownership](2.5-modeling-structure-and-ownership.html);
here I care how it got there. **The model stated the boundary; the API and check gave
selected obligations over that boundary authority. The authority itself still had to
be maintained.**

<!-- label: pdf-seam -->
<!-- figure: assets/pdf-seam-layers.svg | *One Seam, Hardened in Layers.* A convenient scripting-language library call silently corrupted the very accessibility structure the product exists to add, and that failure motivated a canonical-library policy. Representation and a typed seam then make the boundary explicit, and a build-time ban gives selected obligations over it authority against the paths that would otherwise bypass it. Each layer answers a specific failure rather than a plan drawn in advance. Later the enforcement itself reported green when a stale repository root hid real findings, and that second-order incident forced the control to be hardened too. The figure is an engineering history, not a system model. -->

<!-- point: a-protection-boundary-works-only-over-the-paths-that-cross-it | A file path crossed a service boundary; a typed client made the mistake unrepresentable, and a human review found a bypass the tests missed — a boundary works only over the paths that actually cross it. | terms: thesis-modeling -->
**The service seam.** Another failure sent a local file path across a service
boundary, where it became meaningless in the callee's process. The replacement client
could accept file content but not a local path, while an explicit service graph
represented which services were permitted to call which. A later human review exposed
a second problem: an optional direct-client path could bypass shared rate limiting,
key rotation, failover, and cost tracking entirely. The lesson was not "model first,
then gate." It was more precise: **a protection boundary works only over the paths
that actually cross it. Representation tells you what the boundary is meant to cover;
independent inspection and controls are still needed to establish that reality crosses
it.**

## The delegation staircase

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, mbse, governed-engineering-environment -->

<!-- point: mage-was-not-designed-as-an-org-chart | The five rung labels are retrospective names for the changing center of gravity of my own work, not a career ladder. | terms: governance-conversion -->
MAGE was not designed as a five-rung organization chart. The labels below are
retrospective names for the changing center of gravity of my own work: **co-coder,
QA, review lead, tech lead, architect**. What matters is not the title but what the
engineering environment had become capable of carrying at each stage.

<!-- point: the-staircase-has-two-interacting-supports | Alignment moved repeatable authority out of direct human review; Modeling moved system knowledge out of my head; human judgment moved toward the gaps the environment could not yet represent or decide. | terms: thesis-alignment, thesis-modeling -->
The staircase has two interacting supports. **Alignment** moved repeatable authority
out of direct human review: deterministic checks, permissions, boundaries, and gates
could carry obligations I no longer inspected line by line. **Modeling** moved system
knowledge out of my head: named zones and later explicit models let agents reason over
larger engineering questions without reconstructing the whole repository each time.
Human judgment did not disappear. It moved toward the gaps the environment could not
yet represent or decide honestly.

<!-- point: delegation-climbed-as-far-as-the-environment-could-carry-it | Delegation climbed as far as the engineering environment could carry it; when representation or authority was too weak, the operator fell back into tactical work. | terms: governed-engineering-environment -->
Delegation therefore climbed as far as the **engineering environment** could carry it.
When representation was too weak, I fell back into reconstructing the map. When
authority was too weak or unreliable, I fell back into reading diffs and verifying
outcomes manually. A miss could send the staircase backward.

<!-- point: rung-co-coder | Direct human inspection carried everything; it failed as an operating model once generation outran review. | terms: human-as-quality-gate -->
**Co-coder — direct human inspection carried everything.** The first system was small
enough that I read the agent's changes, judged them, and merged them. The human
supplied both the system context and the admission decision. That worked while the
volume stayed inside one person's attention. It failed as an operating model once
generation outran review.

<!-- point: rung-qa | Evaluation was delegated before authority was; the audit's value was diagnostic, and its decidable findings revealed questions that no longer needed a language model. | terms: audits-into-lints -->
**QA — evaluation was delegated before authority was.** I began dispatching a second
agent to audit another agent's work. That removed some reading from my hands but did
not make the result authoritative: the audit ran episodically, produced probabilistic
findings, and still depended on someone to decide what followed. Its value was
diagnostic. Repeated decidable findings revealed questions that no longer needed a
language model at all.

<!-- point: rung-review-lead | Judgment was specialized and the decidable residue dropped into machinery; the probabilistic reviewers kept the questions the checks could not settle. | terms: specialist-crew, audits-into-lints -->
**Review lead — judgment was specialized and the decidable residue dropped into
machinery.** One generalist audit over a growing codebase produced too much
undifferentiated advice, so review split by concern and by region. Architecture,
security, duplication, and naming could be judged in the context where they mattered.
When a finding turned out to be mechanically decidable, it became a deterministic
check; the probabilistic reviewers retained the questions those checks could not
settle.

<!-- point: rung-tech-lead | Representation made reasoning local; named zones externalized ownership so an agent could reason inside one region, and selected rules over the zone model received authority. | terms: component-zone-model, context-window -->
**Tech lead — representation made reasoning local.** Specialized reviewers were still
finite reasoners confronting an expanding repository. Named zones externalized
ownership and architectural boundaries so an agent could reason inside one region
without first recovering the whole system. Boundary checks constrained some cross-zone
edits. This stage combined the two theses explicitly: the zone model reduced
reconstruction; selected rules over that model received authority.

<!-- point: rung-architect | Explicit models carried system-level knowledge; declaration did not make them true, so model drift became its own failure class answered by traceability. | terms: executable-source-of-truth, refactoring-is-free -->
**Architect — explicit models carried system-level knowledge.** {#refactor-cost} By
the final rung I rarely read implementation directly. Architectural, behavioral,
ownership, and other models supplied the system views I needed for planning and
review; agents used the same representations to localize changes. This did not make
the models true by declaration. Model drift became its own failure class, which is why
traceability and correspondence checks eventually became part of the environment. When
the cost of restructuring collapses, the economics invert — a cross-format migration
that touched fifty-eight files and rewrote five thousand lines took about five hours of
agent time over a dinner break, and postponing the right shape becomes the expensive
choice. The hard part was never touching fifty-eight files; it was knowing that
fifty-eight files should be touched.

> **Field note — the map that pointed at a ghost.** One model named a code symbol that
> was later moved and renamed. Nothing invalidated the reference. The model continued
> to look authoritative, agents continued to reason from it, and the failure surfaced
> days later during deployment. That incident forced live trace resolution:
> model-to-code edges were re-resolved against current symbols so a stale pointer
> failed at the change that broke it rather than downstream. Part II carries the
> mechanism in full.

<!-- point: the-incident-represented-a-class | A later audit showed the incident was a class, not a one-off; mechanical correspondence checks retired a recurring class of stale-reference failure. | terms: governance-conversion -->
> **Evidence — a reasoning surface creates a maintenance obligation.** A later audit
> showed that the incident represented a class rather than a one-off. Re-running closed
> work against current code found genuine model-to-code drifts that had escaped a green
> definition of done — the prod-blocking pointer drift among them; subsequent checks
> caught further drift that reading had missed. [appendix: appendix-evidence-ledger]
> carries the counts. The within-case result is simpler: **a model used as a reasoning
> surface created a new maintenance obligation, and mechanical correspondence checks
> retired a recurring class of stale-reference failure.**

<!-- point: modeling-found-real-bugs | Forcing an engineering relation into an explicit representation creates new questions the environment can ask; in three cases those questions exposed shipping defects green tests had missed. | terms: orphan-test -->
Formalizing and instrumenting previously weakly represented seams also exposed live
defects that green tests had not. The useful claim is not that "models find bugs." It
is that **forcing an engineering relation into an explicit representation creates new
questions the environment can ask**, and in these three cases those questions exposed
shipping defects.

The same instrumentation measured its own reach. A traceability tracer counted the exercised
code that traced to no model claim — the unmodeled surface — and that fraction drained from a
majority to under a tenth as the model-loop Epics closed, each aimed at the largest remaining
orphan cluster. [appendix: appendix-evidence-ledger] carries the run-by-run series and the
missing-model, missing-anchor, and below-grain discrimination that keeps the number honest.

> **What modeling found that green tests missed.** Three live defects, each forced into
> view by the act of modeling a seam:
>
> - **A cross-runtime exit code** one runtime emitted and the other had never enumerated, so a genuine failure was silently misfiled as a generic error.
> - **Asymmetric cleanup after redelivery** — a failure path purged a job's database rows and blobs but skipped the queue structures its sibling path always cleaned, leaking chunks on a rare edge.
> - **A last-write-wins editor race** in which two concurrent saves silently discarded one's work.
>
> None was a hypothetical the model raised. Each was a live defect the modeling exposed.

## Delegation climbed as far as the environment carried it

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, governed-engineering-environment, thesis-modeling -->

<!-- point: no-rung-was-planned-as-a-stage-of-mage | The project began with one human reading every line and ended with most implementation moving through an environment inspected through models, evidence, and exceptions; no rung was planned. | terms: governed-engineering-environment -->
The project began with one human reading every line and ended with most implementation
moving through an environment I primarily inspected through models, evidence, and
exceptions. No rung was planned as a stage of MAGE. Each appeared when the current
division of labor stopped scaling: direct review became a queue; generalist audit
became noise; finite-context reviewers needed bounded regions; hardcoded architectural
knowledge rotted; explicit models themselves then created a drift problem that required
traceability.

<!-- point: delegation-depends-on-what-the-environment-can-carry | Representation determines which larger questions no longer require reconstruction; authority determines which repeatable obligations no longer require human admission; human judgment fills the remaining gap. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The important result is not that the staircase always climbs. It is that delegation
depends on what the surrounding engineering system can carry. **Representation
determines which larger questions no longer require reconstruction. Authority
determines which repeatable obligations no longer require human admission. Human
judgment fills the remaining gap.** When one of those supports fails, the operator is
pulled back into tactical work.
