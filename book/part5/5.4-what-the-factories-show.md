# What the Factories Show

<!-- point: the-chapters-question-resolves-into-three-conclusions | The chapter's question — what happens to the software factory when agents substantially increase its capacity for realization — resolves into three conclusions drawn from one deeply observed system and seven public reconstructions. -->
{{chapter:software-factories}} asked what happens to the software factory when agents substantially
increase its capacity for realization. One deeply observed system and seven public reconstructions
suggest three conclusions.

<!-- point: finding-1-the-factory-is-the-unit-of-analysis | First conclusion: the factory, not the agent, is the useful unit of analysis — capable agents sit inside production systems whose knowledge, controls, evidence, and admission machinery decide what a fabricator can know, do, and successfully submit. -->
**1. The factory, not the agent, is the useful unit of analysis.**

Across the cases, capable agents sit inside production systems that carry different kinds of
engineering knowledge and authority. Repositories, service catalogs, context graphs, skills,
policies, permissions, estate models, tests, simulators, benchmarks, provenance records, and
admission gates all shape what a fabricator can know, do, and successfully submit. Some of these
representations help an agent interpret the work. Others constrain action, provide independent
evidence, determine admission, or preserve knowledge for later changes.

The particular arrangement varies. Uber invests heavily in context, reusable skills, measurement,
and optimization of the factory itself. Cloudflare turns institutional policy into increasingly
machine-actionable requirements and review. Spotify structures fleet-scale targeting, verification,
and admission. Shopify makes organizational knowledge reusable across agent work. Zenseact separates
generic platform machinery from domain knowledge and authority. GitLab packages much of the generic
production machinery for other organizations to connect to their own engineering environment.
Siemens begins farther upstream, with explicit engineering models from which realization proceeds.

The common result is not a canonical architecture. It is a separation between realization capacity
and supervisory capacity. Increasing the former does not automatically increase the latter. As
realization scales, engineering work moves toward the representations, evidence, controls, and
decisions through which the resulting production can remain understood and governed.

<!-- point: finding-2-supervision-moves-to-machine-checkable-properties | Second conclusion: MAGE moves supervision from generated implementation to machine-checkable engineering properties — the difference is not that DocAble uses representations but what selected representations are asked to do, and Alignment is what gives selected obligations consequences. -->
**2. MAGE moves supervision from generated implementation to machine-checkable engineering properties.**

The difference in DocAble is not that it uses representations while the other factories do not.
Knowledge graphs, service catalogs, policy corpora, skills, and repository instructions are
representations too. The difference is what selected representations are asked to do. DocAble
represents consequential properties and relationships of the governed system in machine-checkable
engineering models, joins those models where their relationships matter, and uses Alignment to
establish selected obligations against the realized system.

That changes the engineer's supervisory surface. Modeling gives the human engineer an explicit
account of the properties, relationships, and constraints that matter without requiring the engineer
to inspect every realization decision. The same models give agents structured knowledge of the
system and the expectations their work must preserve. Alignment then closes the loop. It gives
agents feedback about whether candidate realizations preserve consequential obligations, allowing
them to explore the remaining degrees of freedom rather than requiring the engineer to specify every
implementation decision. And it gives the human engineer evidence that selected obligations survived
realization rather than merely confidence that the agent probably understood them.

These functions are distinct. A knowledge graph can make an agent better informed while leaving the
consequences of its interpretation entirely with the agent. A prompt can tell an agent what an
engineer wants while leaving satisfaction of that instruction to another act of probabilistic
interpretation. A machine-checkable model can instead expose a property independently of the
particular realization, and Alignment can make preservation of that property a condition of
acceptance. The engineer retains control over what must remain true while delegating substantial
freedom over how the system is realized.

**Information available to an agent is not the same thing as control available to an engineer.**
Connect an agent to a graph carrying ownership, dependency, architecture, service, and policy
information and it may reason better for it. But unless some consequential relation in that graph
has been turned into an obligation with independently established consequences, the engineer has
gained no control over what the agent will infer from it. Uber's context graph is engineering
infrastructure of real value. Connecting an agent to a context graph is not by itself a mechanism of
engineering control.

This is why degrees of freedom matter. MAGE does not seek to replace capable agents with mechanical
derivation from complete specifications. The engineer identifies consequential obligations and
supplies representations adequate to govern them. The agent remains free to search the much larger
realization space between those obligations. When Alignment detects a violation, the agent can
revise its realization and try again. The control resides in the boundary around acceptable
realizations, not in prescribing the path by which the agent reaches one.

DocAble demonstrates that arrangement at unusual depth. I supplied the engineering intent, model
concepts and invariants, architectural and design decisions, consequential controls, and criteria
for evidence and admission; agents performed the implementation. I inspected almost none of their
implementation directly. My supervisory work attached instead to the models, their correspondence
with the system, the evidence produced by Alignment, consequential exceptions, and failures that
indicated that the factory itself should change.

The industrial accounts say little about what an engineer actually does once realization is
delegated. Moving up the stack is the usual phrase, and it does not say much. This arrangement gives
a more substantive answer. The engineer decides which properties are consequential, represents them
adequately, establishes how evidence bears on them, decides which degrees of freedom may safely be
delegated, and remains responsible for revising those structures when experience shows that the
current representations or controls are inadequate.

{{chapter:theory}} separates the two moves this conclusion joins. Context engineering improves an
agent's interpretation of what it was given; Modeling and Alignment change the structure of the
problem for selected properties. Neither is free. Models drift from the system they describe,
correspondence checks require maintenance, controls conflict with one another, evidence ages, and
engineering capital can depreciate. DocAble demonstrates that the arrangement is possible and shows
what it costs to operate. It does not establish that richer modeling is always worthwhile.

<!-- point: finding-3-a-provider-can-sell-machinery-for-control | Third conclusion: a factory provider cannot pre-populate a customer's engineering models, but it can sell the machinery through which customers construct them — representation schemes, correspondence mechanisms, evidence association, and admission machinery — which is machinery for engineering control rather than for realization alone. -->
**3. A factory provider can sell machinery for engineering control, not only machinery for realization.**

An internal factory can build machine-checkable representations around its own system because the
organization possesses the engineering knowledge those representations encode. A factory in a box
cannot arrive knowing the customer's consequential properties. GitLab can supply agents, execution
environments, context machinery, permissions, evidence infrastructure, and admission mechanisms, but
the customer still has to supply the engineering intent that distinguishes an acceptable change to
its system from an unacceptable one.

MAGE suggests that this boundary creates a product opportunity. A factory provider cannot
pre-populate the customer's engineering models, but it can provide the machinery through which
customers construct them. It can provide representation schemes for requirements, architecture,
invariants, ownership, deployment, and other consequential properties; mechanisms for relating those
models to repositories and running systems; ways to associate obligations with evidence; and
admission machinery that gives adequately represented obligations consequences.

The distinction matters. A provider that merely connects more organizational knowledge to an agent
improves the information available for probabilistic reasoning. A provider that helps the customer
turn consequential engineering knowledge into machine-checkable models and Alignment mechanisms can
increase the customer's control over delegated realization. The first helps the fabricator make
better decisions. The second helps the engineer establish which decisions the factory may accept.

This suggests a richer meaning for factory in a box. The box need not contain the customer's factory
in finished form; that is impossible, because the provider does not possess the customer's
engineering intent. It can instead contain the machinery for constructing and operating the
customer-specific supervisory layer around commodity realization. The customer supplies the
consequential engineering knowledge. The provider supplies increasingly powerful ways to represent
it, maintain its correspondence to the system, evaluate realizations against it, and give the
resulting evidence consequences.

<!-- point: what-these-cases-do-not-establish | The limit: one longitudinal case and seven selected public accounts support neither a ranking of the factories nor a causal claim about productivity, quality, or safety — and silence in a public source is not the absence of a mechanism. -->
**What these cases do not establish**

These cases do not establish that MAGE is the unique or optimal organization for agentic software
engineering. DocAble is one longitudinal system, not a controlled comparison. The seven industrial
reconstructions are selected public accounts rather than a representative sample, and their silence
does not establish that an organization lacks a mechanism it does not describe. The evidence
therefore supports neither a ranking of the factories nor a causal claim that any particular
arrangement improves productivity, quality, or safety.

<!-- point: handoff-to-theory | The handoff: the cases establish the phenomenon the theory chapter must explain — when realization capacity outruns the surrounding capacity to represent, evaluate, and govern the work, the engineering problem moves into the production system around the fabricator. -->
It does establish the phenomenon {{chapter:theory}} must explain: when realization capacity
increases faster than the surrounding capacity to represent, evaluate, and govern the resulting
work, the engineering problem moves into the production system around the fabricator.
{{chapter:theory}} asks why that happens, when additional representation and enforcement repay their
cost, and what predictions follow if the account is right.
