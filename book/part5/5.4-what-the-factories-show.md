# What the Factories Show

<!-- point: synthesis-not-another-argument | The closing section synthesizes the chapter's cases into five findings; it argues nothing new. -->
{{chapter:software-factories}} opened on a question — what happens to the software factory when agents
substantially increase its capacity for realization? — and put it to one deeply observed system and
eight reconstructed ones. This closing section synthesizes what those factories show. It argues
nothing new; five findings and one limit.

<!-- point: finding-1-the-factory-is-the-unit-of-analysis | First finding: the software factory is the right unit of analysis for agentic engineering — agent capability alone does not describe the resulting engineering system. -->
First, the software factory is the right unit of analysis for agentic engineering. Agent capability
alone describes none of the systems this chapter examined. What distinguished them was never the
model in use; it was the production system around it — what knowledge the factory carries, what its
machinery enforces, where its evidence comes from, and what its people still decide. A question
about agents kept resolving into a question about the factory built around them.

<!-- point: finding-2-realization-and-supervision-are-distinct | Second finding: realization and supervision are distinct capacities, and increasing one does not automatically increase the other — every case exhibits the asymmetry from its own angle. -->
Second, realization and supervision are distinct capacities, and increasing one does not
automatically increase the other. DocAble met the distinction as a lived condition — implementation
volume outran one reader within weeks. The industrial reports expose the same asymmetry at
organizational scale: realization capacity arriving as a purchased commodity, supervisory capacity
still having to be engineered. Nothing in the evidence suggests the second follows from the first.

<!-- point: finding-3-supervision-need-not-run-through-implementation | Third finding: supervision need not operate through implementation alone — DocAble demonstrates a representationally rich arrangement in which models, joins, evidence, and conversion carry the supervisory load. -->
Third, supervision need not operate through implementation alone. DocAble demonstrates one much more
representationally rich arrangement: models expose consequential properties; joins expose
relationships between them; Alignment turns selected knowledge into evidence and consequences; and
governance conversion changes the environment as new lessons emerge. The other factories exhibit
partial versions of the same move — policy corpora, estate models, context graphs, permission
structures — each shifting some supervision off the fabricated artifact and onto a purpose-built
surface. The supervisor of the most heavily automated factory in this chapter read almost none of
its implementation, and remained in informed control of it.

<!-- point: finding-4-no-canonical-architecture | Fourth finding: there is no evidence for one canonical factory architecture — the cases differ in what they model, mechanize, and leave human; DocAble gives depth and mechanism, the cases give breadth and variation. -->
Fourth, there is no evidence here for one canonical factory architecture. The organizations exhibit
different mixtures of explicit knowledge, bounded delegation, validation, human review, controls,
and production infrastructure; some deliberately keep admission human, others push toward
fleet-scale acceptance; only one case shows models of the governed system joined and enforced.
DocAble supplies depth and mechanism; the independent cases supply breadth and variation. The
variation is data, not noise.

<!-- point: finding-5-materially-different-treatments-of-repeated-judgment | Fifth finding: the cases expose four materially different treatments of repeated judgment — improve the reasoner's odds, independently detect and reject failure, change the representation until the judgment becomes tractable or decidable, or deliberately leave it to an expert. -->
Fifth, the cases expose materially different engineering treatments of repeated judgment. Four are
visible in the assembled evidence. An organization can improve the probability that the reasoner
gets the judgment right: Uber compiles context and selects models against benchmarks built from
real workloads; DocAble's zone and system models let an agent reason locally rather than
reconstruct the repository. It can independently detect and reject failure, as Docker does with an
independent reviewer and deterministic tests, and as DocAble does by regenerating required evidence
at the admission boundary. It can change the representation until the judgment becomes tractable or
even mechanically decidable — Cloudflare externalizes its standards corpus into machine-checkable
requirements; DocAble kept converting decidable review findings into blocking checks. Or it can
deliberately leave the judgment with an expert: Docker keeps final admission human on purpose, and
every factory in this chapter still routes some semantic decisions to a person. The treatments
differ, and the organizations mix them. What the cases do not say is which treatment a given
judgment deserves, or when each is worth its cost.

<!-- point: the-evidentiary-limit-stated-plainly | The limit: these cases do not establish MAGE as unique or optimal; they establish the phenomenon from which the next chapter's theory is induced. -->
Then the limit, stated plainly. These cases do not establish that MAGE is the unique or optimal
organization for agentic software engineering. One deeply observed system cannot establish
population-level effects or show that MAGE uniquely explains its own development; selected
industrial accounts provide variation but less process history, and they are not a representative
sample. Neither form of evidence establishes that adopting any of these arrangements causes greater
productivity, quality, or safety. What the cases establish is the phenomenon that requires
explanation: increased realization capacity changes the engineering problem when the surrounding
environment does not provide a corresponding increase in the capacity to understand, evaluate, and
govern the resulting work.
