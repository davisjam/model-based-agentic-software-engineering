# Two Problems of Factory Engineering

<!-- point: the-factories-place-engineering-structure-in-different-parts-of-production | The factories differ in scale, organization, and purpose, and they place engineering structure in different parts of production: the commercial systems arrange how production proceeds, while much of DocAble's explicit structure represents properties of the software being produced. -->
The factories examined in this chapter differ substantially in scale, organization, and purpose.
They also place engineering structure in different parts of production. The commercial systems in
{{sec:mage-in-the-wild}} devote substantial machinery to arranging how production proceeds:
supplying context, routing work, controlling tool access and permissions, coordinating people and
agents, arranging review, and deciding what may be admitted. DocAble has production machinery as
well, but much of its explicit structure represents properties of the software being produced and
establishes whether successive realizations preserve them.

<!-- point: production-capacity-and-supervisory-capacity-need-not-grow-together | The comparison also exposes a constraint: the capacity to produce changes and the capacity to supervise and verify them need not grow together, as Uber, Spotify, Cloudflare, and Shopify each show, so process design includes the allocation of scarce supervisory capacity and not only the organization of realization. -->
The comparison also exposes a constraint that becomes more important as production accelerates. The
capacity to produce changes and the capacity to supervise and verify them need not grow together. Uber
treats reviewer attention as a finite production resource; Spotify reports that change volume
increased faster than some of its verification controls could adapt; Cloudflare moved some checks
earlier in response to review latency; and Shopify names the social failure that occurs when cheap
production transfers expensive inspection to another engineer. Process design therefore includes the
allocation of scarce supervisory capacity, not only the organization of realization.

<!-- point: process-design-and-tolerance-are-both-problems-of-factory-engineering | Process design determines how production proceeds and tolerance determines what variation in the product is acceptable; both are problems of factory engineering, and the cases show how differently a factory can distribute its effort between them. | terms: tolerance -->
The distinction recalls the two techniques with which this chapter began
([ref:process-design-and-tolerance]). Process design determines how production proceeds. Tolerance
determines what variation in the resulting product is acceptable.
Both are problems of factory engineering. The cases make visible how differently an agentic software
factory can distribute its effort between them.

## Process Design and Tolerance

<!-- point: docables-tolerance-emphasis-followed-from-its-circumstances | DocAble's emphasis on tolerance followed from its circumstances: accessibility supplied external legal obligations, and one engineer with no spare humans to inspect agent output had little process to design and every reason to encode requirements precisely. | terms: tolerance -->
DocAble's emphasis on tolerance was partly a consequence of the problem that produced it.
Accessibility supplied external requirements, including legal obligations, that the resulting
documents had to satisfy. The project was also a side effort run by one engineer. There was little
organizational process to design, and neither time nor appetite to inspect every implementation
decision made by coding agents. The practical objective was therefore to encode consequential
requirements precisely enough that agents could change the system freely while the resulting
software continued to satisfy them. At the time, this struck me as the obvious way to build an
agentic software factory. Having now compared it with commercial factories, I can see that it was
specifically the obvious way for an "organization" with one human engineer and no spare humans to
supervise the agents.

<!-- point: commercial-factories-emphasize-process-because-agents-work-inside-organizations | Commercial agents work inside large engineering organizations where work moves among people, authority must be bounded, and changes enter systems many people maintain, so those factories emphasize process design; the difference is where the visible machinery is concentrated. -->
Commercial factories operate under different conditions. Their agents work inside large engineering
organizations, where work must move among people and agents, organizational knowledge must reach the
right work, authority must be bounded, tools must be exposed safely, and changes must eventually
enter systems maintained by many people. Their public descriptions consequently place substantial
emphasis on process design. The difference is where the visible engineering machinery is
concentrated.

<!-- point: industrial-factories-have-tolerance-machinery-but-much-intent-stays-in-natural-language | Tests, static checks, simulators, benchmarks, and policy predicates are genuine tolerance machinery, but much of the intent in the public accounts stays in natural language, which does not itself provide a machine-checkable boundary around acceptable realizations. | terms: tolerance -->
Tolerance is present in these factories, but generally less explicit and less tightly represented.
Tests, static checks, simulators, benchmarks, and policy predicates all bound selected properties of
acceptable realizations. They are genuine tolerance machinery. But much of the engineering intent
described in the public accounts remains in natural-language instructions, skills, policy corpora,
knowledge graphs, or human review. These mechanisms can make an agent better informed and improve
the probability that it interprets the intent correctly, but they do not themselves provide a
machine-checkable boundary around acceptable realizations. The fabricator still has to interpret the
instruction, and another act of interpretation may be required to decide whether the result
satisfied it.

**Information available to an agent is not the same thing as control available to an engineer.**

<!-- point: mage-represents-the-obligation-rather-than-the-checking-technique | DocAble's tolerance is distinctive: MAGE represents the consequential property explicitly and uses Alignment to maintain correspondence with the realized system, so the tolerance is the represented obligation rather than any particular checking technique, which permits tighter tolerances than instructions or isolated checks alone. | terms: tolerance, modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->
DocAble's approach to tolerance, however, is distinctive. MAGE represents selected consequential
properties explicitly and uses Alignment to maintain correspondence between those representations
and the realized system. Tests, property-based testing, static analysis, measurements, and other
evidence mechanisms can participate in establishing those properties, but the tolerance is the
represented engineering obligation rather than any particular checking technique. This permits
tighter tolerances than instructions or isolated checks alone: the factory can represent what must
remain true, identify the realization freedom left open, independently establish whether the
resulting system remains within those bounds, and make failure consequential for admission.

<!-- point: externalized-knowledge-carries-its-own-maintenance-burden | The representations a factory uses become part of its maintenance burden — GitLab, Cloudflare, and Shopify all describe machinery for keeping inherited context current — so externalizing engineering knowledge exchanges repeated judgment for the carrying cost of production structure, and whether the exchange pays depends on reuse and upkeep. -->
The representations used by the factory also become part of its maintenance burden. GitLab,
Cloudflare, and Shopify all describe machinery for keeping instructions, documentation, or other
inherited context current. Externalizing engineering knowledge therefore does not eliminate the cost
of exercising that knowledge repeatedly; it exchanges some of that repeated judgment for the carrying
cost of production structure. Whether that exchange pays depends on how widely the structure is reused
and how expensive it is to keep accurate.

<!-- point: process-design-and-tolerance-respond-differently-to-a-better-fabricator | Process design encodes assumptions about how production must proceed, which a better fabricator can change; tolerances describe properties the product must preserve, which a better fabricator does not change. | terms: tolerance -->
The distinction matters because process design and tolerance respond differently to improvements in
the fabricator. Process design often encodes assumptions about how production must proceed: how work
is decomposed, what information is supplied, which tools are available, and where supervision
occurs. Those assumptions can change as the fabricator and its interfaces improve. Tolerances
describe properties of the product that production must preserve. A more capable fabricator may
require a different process — or much less process — without changing the memory bound, security
boundary, accessibility requirement, or deployment constraint that the resulting system must
satisfy.

<!-- point: mage-concentrates-on-the-tolerance-side-of-the-factory | MAGE concentrates on the tolerance side and does not prescribe a general process, so the distinction is not between factories that have tolerances and factories that do not, but between ways of representing them, establishing them, and making them consequential. | terms: tolerance -->
That difference also helps locate MAGE within the larger problem of factory engineering. MAGE
concentrates primarily on the tolerance side of the factory. Process design remains part of the
production system, but MAGE does not attempt to prescribe a general process for agentic software
development. The distinction is therefore not between factories that have tolerances and factories
that do not. It is between different ways of representing tolerances, establishing them, and making
them consequential in production.

## The Engineer in the Factory

<!-- point: both-problems-require-judgment-that-shapes-later-production | Both problems require human judgment — about decomposition, information, authority, review, and admission on one side, and about which properties are consequential and what evidence establishes them on the other — exercised once so it shapes subsequent production, with the industrial cases showing the leverage directly. | terms: tolerance -->
In both aspects of factory engineering, human engineers use their judgment to decide what the
production system should carry. Process design requires decisions about decomposition, information,
authority, tools, review, and admission. Tolerance requires decisions about which properties are
consequential, how they should be represented, and what evidence should establish them. These
structures allow judgment exercised once to shape subsequent production rather than requiring the
same judgment to be exercised again for every realization. The industrial cases show the leverage
directly: one policy, skill, verifier, or shared representation can govern a class of later work.

<!-- point: agents-sit-outside-the-accountability-mechanisms-that-delegate-judgment-to-people | Organizations delegate judgment to people partly through incentives and accountability, and agents sit outside every one of those loops, so the human engineer decides which decisions may remain with the agent and which need stronger control through the production system. -->
But there is an important difference between supervising human and agentic production. Organizations
can delegate judgment to people partly through incentives and accountability: people can be trained,
rewarded, promoted, disciplined, fired, held professionally responsible, or subjected to legal
sanction.[cite: marchsimon1958] Codex and Kimi and Claude do not desire to do good work, pursue
promotion, fear firing, or face jail time for what they produce. Instructions, context, and process
can shape their behavior, but they cannot reproduce the organizational mechanisms through which
consequential judgment has traditionally been delegated to people. The human engineer therefore
remains responsible for deciding which decisions may safely remain with the agent and which require
stronger control through the production system.

<!-- point: tolerance-preserves-control-without-specifying-the-realization | A tolerance preserves engineering control without specifying the realization: the engineer names the consequential property and the evidence required for it, and the agent keeps the remaining degrees of freedom. | terms: tolerance -->
Tolerance provides one way to preserve that control without specifying the realization completely. A
memory model need not specify the implementation that satisfies the memory bound, nor must a
deployment model determine the internal structure of every service. The engineer identifies the
consequential property and the evidence required for it; the agent retains the remaining degrees of
freedom. Process design can similarly constrain where and how the agent works. In either case, the
engineer's task is to decide where judgment must remain durable in the factory and where the
fabricator may be allowed to supply it during realization.

## Factories Inside and Outside the Organization

<!-- point: the-cases-differ-in-who-supplies-the-factory | The cases differ in who supplies the factory: some are internal production systems whose owners engineer the machinery their agents work through, while others move toward a reusable factory in a box supplied to organizations the provider does not own. -->
The cases also differ in who supplies the factory. DocAble and several systems in
{{sec:mage-in-the-wild}} are substantially internal production systems: the organization engineering
the software also engineers much of the machinery through which agents participate in its
production. Other systems move toward a factory in a box: a reusable production system supplied to
organizations whose software, requirements, and engineering knowledge the factory provider does not
own.

<!-- point: every-factory-joins-capability-to-organization-specific-intent | Every software factory must join productive capability to organization-specific engineering intent; an internal factory builds that connection directly, and a factory in a box must solve the same problem across an organizational boundary. -->
Every software factory must join productive capability to organization-specific engineering intent.
An internal factory can build that connection directly into its representations, processes,
controls, and evidence mechanisms because the organization owns both the production machinery and
the knowledge it must carry. A factory in a box must solve the same problem across an organizational
boundary.

<!-- point: a-provider-cannot-know-which-properties-are-consequential | A provider can supply agents, orchestration, context, execution, permissions, evidence infrastructure, and generic controls, but cannot know which properties of a customer's software are consequential, so the customer's engineering knowledge must enter generic production machinery. -->
The provider can supply agents, orchestration, context machinery, execution environments,
permissions, evidence infrastructure, and generic controls. It cannot know in advance which
properties of a customer's software are consequential, which variation is acceptable, or what
evidence should establish that an obligation has been satisfied. The customer must supply that
engineering knowledge, and the factory must provide machinery for making it effective in
production. The external factory therefore makes an existing integration problem harder:
organization-specific intent must enter generic production machinery without returning every
consequential judgment to human inspection of the resulting implementation.

<!-- point: joining-capacity-to-control-remains-an-open-design-space | How a factory in a box exposes machinery for process design, for product tolerances, or for both remains an open design space; the narrower point is that productive capacity and engineering control need not come from the same place. | terms: tolerance -->
This remains an open design space. A factory in a box might expose richer machinery for process
design, for representing product tolerances, or for both. Improvements in the underlying agents may
also change which machinery is worth carrying. The important point for the cases in this chapter is
narrower: productive capacity and engineering control need not come from the same place, and an
agentic factory must somehow join them.
