# Coda: Engineering the Factory

<!-- point: process-design-and-tolerance-describe-the-differences-without-a-maturity-model | A software factory arranges engineering knowledge, production capacity, evidence, and authority; those choices determine delegation, protected obligations, and the next production constraint. | terms: tolerance -->

The software factory is an arrangement of engineering knowledge, production capacity, evidence, and authority. The cases in this chapter show that these elements can be organized in substantially different ways. A factory may rely on explicit models, inherited organizational procedures, human reviewers, independent checks, or combinations of them. Those choices determine how much implementation can be delegated, which obligations remain protected, and where additional production capacity encounters its next constraint.

<!-- point: supervision-capacity-and-carrying-cost-become-visible-as-realization-gets-cheaper | Process design and tolerance are complementary responses to differing organizational conditions; finite supervision and the upkeep of representations influence where engineers place control. | terms: tolerance -->

Process design and tolerance help explain those choices ([ref:process-design-and-tolerance]). DocAble's accessibility obligations and one-engineer staffing made explicit requirements and automated rejection especially valuable: I had little inherited human process to accommodate and no spare reviewers for every implementation decision. Coordinating agents still required work orders, scheduling, and completion checks. In an established organization, the factory must also fit existing teams, knowledge, and authority. Both settings require judgments about which properties to preserve and how to organize work around them. A more capable agent may change the process needed to produce a system without changing its memory bound or deployment restriction. Engineers can leave implementation choices open within those bounds, while weighing the cost of supervision against the cost of maintaining the representations and checks that support it.

<!-- point: process-design-and-tolerance-give-machinery-for-the-theory-chapter | Human incentives and accountability remain distinct from agent capability; engineering authority determines delegation and controls, and the theory chapter examines their costs and effects. | terms: tolerance -->

Capability also does not settle accountability. Organizations delegate consequential judgment to people partly through incentives and responsibility, including professional obligations and institutional consequences[cite: marchsimon1958]. An agent does not bear those responsibilities or respond to those incentives in the same way a human engineer does. Its instructions and permissions can be changed, and its work can be stopped, but those actions do not transfer human accountability to it. Engineering authority must therefore decide which judgments to delegate, what evidence to require, and which obligations need controls. {{chapter:theory}} examines the costs and effects of these choices: how representation and independent evaluation change the economics of realization, and how experience changes the engineering structures worth maintaining.
