# Coda: Engineering the Factory

<!-- point: the-cases-differ-but-all-are-production-systems-around-fabricators | The cases differ in what they produce, how they organize work, and where they place engineering authority, yet all can be read as production systems surrounding increasingly capable fabricators, differing in where they place their engineering emphasis. -->
The cases in this chapter differ substantially in what they produce, how they organize work, and
where they place engineering authority. Yet all can be understood as production systems surrounding
increasingly capable fabricators. Contemporary agentic factories, as observed in 2026, differ
especially in where they place their engineering emphasis.

<!-- point: process-design-investment-versus-tolerance-investment | Most commercial factories invest heavily in process design — routing, context, permissions, tool constraints, review sequencing, admission — while DocAble invests less in organizational process and more in tolerance; the two are different responses to one supervisory problem and can be combined. | terms: tolerance -->
Most of the commercial factories examined here invest heavily in process design. They route work,
supply context, assign permissions, constrain tools, sequence review, and control admission. Much of
this machinery limits the scope within which an agent acts and coordinates its work with the larger
organization. DocAble invests less in organizational process and more in tolerance: selected
consequential properties are represented independently of the agent's instructions and evaluated
against what the agent produces. These are different responses to the same supervisory problem, and
they can be combined.

<!-- point: a-purchased-factory-cannot-manage-agents-through-accountability | The problem sharpens when the productive capacity comes from outside: a customer cannot manage purchased agents through the incentives and accountability by which organizations govern human work, and the vendor cannot know the customer's obligations, so control has to run through the factory itself. -->
The problem is sharper when the productive capacity comes from outside the organization. A company
purchasing a factory in a box can acquire capable agents, orchestration, context machinery,
permissions, and admission infrastructure, but it cannot manage those agents through the incentives
and accountability structures by which organizations govern human work. Nor can the vendor know the
customer's consequential engineering obligations. The customer must therefore retain control through
the factory itself. A useful factory in a box must provide machinery through which customers can
state the bounds that matter and establish whether production remained within them.

<!-- point: the-cases-establish-the-problem-not-its-answer | The cases establish the production problem but not how much of an engineering environment should be explicit, which properties justify independent enforcement, or when the machinery costs more than it returns — which is where the theory chapter begins. -->
The cases establish the production problem. They do not tell us how much of an engineering
environment should be made explicit, which properties justify independent enforcement, or when the
resulting machinery costs more to maintain than it returns. {{chapter:theory}} turns to those
questions.
