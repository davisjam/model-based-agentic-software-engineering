# Coda: Engineering the Factory

<!-- point: supervision-capacity-and-carrying-cost-become-visible-as-realization-gets-cheaper | DocAble developed production arrangements as implementation outpaced individual supervision; established organizations adapted existing processes, validation suites, and review practices. | terms: tolerance -->

The software factory is itself an object of engineering. Its processes determine how work is selected, divided, performed, and evaluated; its models and controls establish which decisions can be delegated and which properties must be preserved ([ref:process-design-and-tolerance]). DocAble developed these arrangements as implementation outpaced my ability to supervise individual changes. Established organizations, by contrast, already had production processes, validation suites, and review practices into which agents could be introduced. In both settings, increasing implementation capacity creates new demands on the surrounding production system. The factory must evolve alongside the software it produces.

Organizations need not begin with fully autonomous implementation. Some use agents to review pull requests or propose changes that people still inspect. Others allow agents to implement and validate changes before routing selected decisions to human reviewers. These arrangements delegate different amounts of work and authority, but they face the same engineering questions: what must remain true, what evidence establishes it, and which decisions require human approval? MAGE applies to those questions even when most implementation remains under direct human supervision.

<!-- point: process-design-and-tolerance-give-machinery-for-the-theory-chapter | Human incentives and accountability remain distinct from agent capability; engineering authority determines delegation and controls, and the theory chapter examines their costs and effects. | terms: tolerance -->

Capability also does not settle accountability. Organizations delegate consequential judgment to people partly through incentives and responsibility, including professional obligations and institutional consequences [cite: marchsimon1958]. An agent does not bear those responsibilities or respond to those incentives in the same way a human engineer does. Its instructions and permissions can be changed, and its work can be stopped, but those actions do not transfer human accountability to it. Engineering authority must therefore decide which judgments to delegate, what evidence to require, and which obligations need controls. {{chapter:theory}} examines the costs and effects of these choices: how representation and independent evaluation change the economics of realization, and how experience changes the engineering structures worth maintaining.
