# Coda: Engineering the Factory

<!-- point: the-cases-differ-but-all-are-production-systems-around-fabricators | The cases differ in what they produce, how they organize work, and where they place engineering authority, yet all can be read as production systems surrounding increasingly capable fabricators, differing in where they place their engineering emphasis. -->
The cases in this chapter differ substantially in what they produce, how they organize work, and
where they place engineering authority. Yet all can be understood as production systems surrounding
increasingly capable fabricators. Contemporary agentic factories, as observed in 2026, differ
especially in where they place their engineering emphasis.

<!-- point: process-design-investment-versus-tolerance-investment | Most commercial factories invest heavily in process design — routing, context, permissions, tool constraints, review sequencing, admission — while DocAble invests less in organizational process and more in tolerance; the two are different responses to one supervisory problem and can be combined. | terms: tolerance -->
Most of the commercial factories examined here invest heavily in process design. They route work,
supply context, assign permissions, constrain tools, sequence review, and control admission. Much of
this machinery limits the scope within which an agent acts and coordinates its work with the larger
organization. DocAble invests less in organizational process and more in tolerance: selected
consequential properties are represented independently of the agent's instructions and evaluated
against what the agent produces. These are different responses to the same supervisory problem, and
they can be combined.

<!-- point: every-factory-joins-generic-capability-to-specific-intent | Every software factory must join generic production capability to organization-specific engineering intent; an internal factory builds that connection directly, while a factory in a box must solve the same problem across an organizational boundary, which makes an existing problem harder. -->
Every software factory must join generic production capability to organization-specific engineering
intent. An internal factory can build that connection directly into its representations, processes,
controls, and evidence mechanisms because the organization owns both the production machinery and
the knowledge it must carry. A factory in a box must solve the same problem across an organizational
boundary. Its provider can supply agents, orchestration, context machinery, execution environments,
evidence infrastructure, permissions, and generic controls, but it cannot know in advance which
properties of a customer's software are consequential or what evidence should establish them. The
customer must supply that engineering knowledge, and the box must provide machinery through which it
can become part of production. The external factory therefore makes an existing problem harder:
integrating organization-specific intent into generic production machinery without returning every
consequential judgment to human inspection of the resulting implementation.

<!-- point: the-metaphor-gives-machinery-for-the-theory-chapter | Process design and tolerance let us model how engineering work is redistributed as realization becomes cheaper and more capable, and the theory chapter builds a mathematical model and a dynamic model of engineering practice, then derives predictions from them. | terms: tolerance -->
The factory metaphor now gives us machinery for going further. Process design asks how work,
information, authority, evidence, and admission are arranged around a capable fabricator. Tolerance
asks which variation the factory permits and which properties it must keep under engineering
control. Together, those ideas let us model how engineering work is redistributed as realization
becomes cheaper and more capable, why some judgments are worth moving into durable production
structures, and where the costs of representation, evidence, and human attention should appear.
{{chapter:theory}} develops a mathematical model of agentic engineering and a dynamic model of
engineering practice, then uses them to derive predictions about how agent capability, engineering
structure, and the economics of controlled change interact.
