## A noble idea with a crushing implementation

<!-- section-terms: document-accessibility, ada-title-ii -->

<!-- point: ada-promises-equal-access | The ADA promises people with disabilities equal access. | terms: ada -->
The Americans with Disabilities Act starts from a simple promise: people with disabilities
should have equal access to public life — to work, travel, study, vote, and use services
without barriers society could reasonably remove. The wheelchair ramp and the elevator are its
famous results. The software problem grows out of a quieter corner of the statute.

<!-- point: title-ii-now-means-digital | Title II now governs a university's digital communication. | terms: ada-title-ii -->
That corner is Title II, which covers state and local governments — including K–12 schools and
public universities. It says a public entity must communicate with people who have disabilities
*as effectively* as it communicates with everyone else. Title II always reached communication;
what changed is where communication lives. In 1990 the focus was physical: buildings, lectures,
office hours. By 2026 much of a university's communication had moved into digital artifacts —
websites, PDFs, slide decks, Word documents, spreadsheets, learning-management systems, videos,
forms, exams, syllabi, and the thousand little files that pile up wherever people upload things.

<!-- point: inaccessible-materials-are-legal-hazard | Inaccessible materials exclude students and invite lawsuits. | terms: document-accessibility -->
If those materials cannot be read by a screen reader, cannot be navigated by keyboard, lack
captions or alternative text, or carry their meaning only through color or layout, then some
students get a worse version of the university than others. The hazard is legal as well as
moral: accessibility lawsuits are filed every year by students who were not reasonably
accommodated, and institutions have settled them for sums in the {{lawsuit_settlement_range}}
[cite: CarlsonHigherEdAccessibilityLawsuits].

## The rule that changed the burden

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | The rule that changed the burden — the reported count is inflated by the burden-shift table's cells; the prose body is three short paragraphs within the cap, and the table is the section's side-by-side reference, not prose to break up. -->

<!-- section-terms: doj-2024-rule, wcag-conformance -->

<!-- point: compliance-was-reactive-and-unjust | Reactive compliance put the burden on the excluded. | terms: reactive-compliance -->
Universities have owed Title II compliance since 1990, but for most of that time they complied
reactively: a student hit an inaccessible document, requested an accommodation, and the
institution scrambled to respond. The shape is morally awkward — the inaccessible artifact
exists first, and the burden of discovering it and asking for a fix falls on the person the
barrier has already excluded.

<!-- point: doj-rule-made-access-part-of-service | The 2024 DOJ rule made access part of the service. | terms: doj-2024-rule -->
In 2024 the Department of Justice changed the shape. A final rule updated the Title II
regulations for web content and mobile apps on a blunt premise: if a public entity provides a
service through a website, an app, or the documents distributed through them, the accessibility
of that content is *part of the service*. Just as stairs can exclude a wheelchair user from a
government building, inaccessible web content and documents can exclude people from government
services — and just as Americans do not call ahead to ask whether a building has a ramp, a
student should not have to negotiate with a professor over whether the slides will be readable.
[ref:the-burden-shift] lays the shift out side by side.

<!-- label: the-burden-shift -->
<!-- table: The 2024 DOJ rule shifted the accessibility burden from reactive accommodation to proactive access. [short: The burden shift] -->
| Dimension | Reactive accommodation (the old posture) | Proactive access — operational implication for the institution |
|---|---|---|
| Who bears the burden | The excluded student — discover the barrier, then ask for a fix | The institution — build access in before anyone is turned away |
| When access is created | After a barrier is hit, one accommodation at a time | Built in when the content is provided or made available, as part of the service |
| What triggers the work | An accommodation request | Putting the document in front of students |
| The DOJ's own analogy | Calling ahead to ask whether a building has a ramp | The ramp is simply there |
| The standard | Ad hoc, case by case | {{doj_technical_standard}}, on the rule's compliance timeline |
| The scope | The single artifact a student flagged | Decades of accumulated digital sediment |

<!-- point: rule-set-standard-and-deadline | The rule set WCAG 2.1 AA and a deadline. | terms: wcag-conformance, digital-sediment -->
The rule set a technical standard — {{doj_technical_standard}} — and, for large institutions, a
deadline. Universities are not sitting on three PDFs and a cheerful little homepage. They sit on
decades of accumulated digital sediment: course sites, admissions forms, scanned readings,
lecture videos, and more. A deadline is a deadline, and this one landed on a mountain of files
no one had ever been asked to climb.

## What it costs to make one document accessible {#nearly-free}

<!-- section-terms: remediation-cost -->

<!-- point: one-deck-carries-dozens-of-issues | One real deck flags dozens of distinct accessibility issues. | terms: remediation-cost -->
Compliance carries a cost, and the cost is easiest to feel on a single deck. Open a PowerPoint
slide deck — say, the "Syllabus Day" deck for a graduate course — and click the *Accessibility*
tab in Microsoft PowerPoint. On a typical real deck it reports dozens of distinct problems:
images missing alt text, slides missing titles, low-contrast text, tables without headers, a
reading order the tool cannot verify. One real deck examined this way flagged {{deck_issue_count}}
separate issues across its slides.

<!-- point: per-course-hand-cost-is-prohibitive | Remediating a course by hand costs weeks of work. | terms: remediation-cost -->
Addressing one issue takes a couple of minutes at best, and the denser the figure, the longer
the fix. Scale that to a course, then to an institution. The assumptions above put one teaching
load's materials at over {{diy_cost_per_course}} of faculty time, and a research professor
teaches more than one course. Multiply by the thousands of courses at one institution and the
number stops being a budget line and starts being a reason the whole thing gets quietly deferred.

<!-- point: every-escape-hatch-closes-on-inspection | Every escape hatch from the cost closes on inspection. | terms: escape-hatch, remediation-cost -->
There are escape hatches, and each one closes on inspection:

- **Pull the plug.** A university could satisfy the letter of the rule by de-digitizing — removing the online materials so there is nothing inaccessible to fix. It would satisfy the letter and betray the spirit. Digital materials help everyone: captions serve the student in a noisy room and the student still learning English, not only the student who is deaf. No sane institution wants to become the one that took the slides away.
- **Hire it out.** External remediation vendors exist, and they quote on the order of {{vendor_cost_per_page}} per page or slide — roughly {{vendor_cost_per_course_low}} to {{vendor_cost_per_course_high}} for one course's materials at the vendor's own rate. No vendor is staffed with people who can annotate graduate-level astrophysics or quantum mechanics, and no university is eager to pay that per course across every course it offers.
- **Do it yourself.** The professor holds the required subject knowledge, but the institution's promotion and workload systems give that remediation labor little formal value. At a research university, faculty are promoted on research; a large, uncompensated, recurring teaching-service chore is exactly the kind of work the incentive structure teaches them to avoid. Left standing, the burden does worse than go undone: it discourages updating course materials at all, freezing students into whatever version existed when the deadline hit.

<!-- point: the-result-is-a-genuine-problem | Together these make a genuine, unmanufactured problem. | terms: remediation-cost -->
Put these together and you have a genuine problem — no one had to manufacture it. A federal
mandate with real consequences, a mountain of heterogeneous files, expertise that only the
busiest people possess, and no affordable path from here to there.

## Why a document is not just a file

<!-- section-terms: vision-language-model, semantic-gap -->

<!-- point: a-document-is-a-semantic-artifact | A document is a visual and semantic artifact. | terms: semantic-artifact -->
Underneath the institutional bind sits a technical problem deeper than it looks — the reason
this became a software project worth building rather than a policy memo worth writing. A document
is not merely a file. It is a *visual and semantic* artifact: text, figures, tables, diagrams,
reading order, hierarchy, emphasis, and all the small tricks by which humans convey meaning to
other humans. To make it accessible, a machine has to recover enough of that meaning to present
it through another channel — to a screen reader, to a keyboard, to a student who cannot see the
figure the lecturer drew.

<!-- point: spine-ascent-commodity-intelligence | The frontier vision-language model is the spine's commodity intelligence. | terms: vision-language-model, thesis-modeling -->
<!-- point: vlms-recover-document-meaning | Vision-language models now recover a slide's semantic content. | terms: vision-language-model -->
Earlier systems could automate fragments of the task — pulling text, flagging a missing title,
generating thin alt text — but general semantic recovery from an arbitrary instructional slide
stayed too weak for the system this needed to become. The turn came with frontier vision-language
models. Feed one a rendered slide and it looks at the image and recovers useful semantic content:
it describes the figure, reads the equation, names what the slide is trying to say. That is one of
the core technical problems of document accessibility, and it is now solvable well enough to build
on.

<!-- point: spine-ascent-context-problem | The model's missing situated judgment is the context problem. | terms: semantic-gap, infinite-midwit -->
<!-- point: capability-is-not-a-system | Raw model capability is not a compliance strategy. | terms: semantic-gap, demoable-vs-productizable, infinite-midwit -->
Raw model capability is not a compliance strategy, though, and this is the gap the engineering
had to fill. The model looks at a slide and understands something real. It understands nothing
about university procurement, disability-resource workflows, or faculty incentives — fluent
knowledge and no situated judgment; an infinite midwit that knows everything and understands
nothing. As the earlier chapters kept showing, a flash of capability is not a system. You get one
only by bounding what you ask, typing what comes back, and validating before you believe.

<!-- point: context-is-law-deadline-cost-and-gap | The context: real law, deadline, cost, bridgeable gap. | terms: document-accessibility, semantic-gap -->
That is the bar. A real law, a real deadline, a real cost, and a real technical gap that had just
barely become bridgeable. This chapter set the first of the two dials that fixed how much
structure the work required — the *consequence* dial, why correctness had to be earned. The next
chapter turns to the other dial: why this particular system sat on such unfamiliar ground that the
apparatus had to grow as large as it did.
