## Theory Formation and Comparative Analysis

This book did not begin by surveying industrial practice and inducing its common themes. It began
with one intensive engineering case: the design, construction, and operation of DocAble, a production
document-accessibility system built over several months while directing a fleet of frontier coding
agents. The prolonged engagement with a single system made more than its successful mechanisms
observable. It made visible the failures, the redesigns, and the reasoning that produced those
mechanisms in the first place. So the book's constructs — engineering capital, governance conversion,
the Modeling and Alignment theses — were developed as explanatory constructs, not extracted as
recurring terminology.

Once that framework had stabilized, I turned outward to contemporary engineering reports published by
independent organizations. These reports describe agentic engineering in settings that differ sharply
in scale, domain, organizational structure, and technical emphasis. Rather than coding them inductively
into a taxonomy of current practice, I reconstructed each system in the MAGE vocabulary and put three
questions to it:

- **Which mechanisms** the organization describes does the theory naturally explain?
- **Where does the organization expose phenomena** the originating case could not observe, so the theory
  must be refined or extended to hold?
- **Where does the theory predict** mechanisms or relationships not yet explicit in the industrial account?

This sequence does a different job than an inductive synthesis of practitioner reports. Those reports are
the right instrument for breadth, but they emphasize deployed mechanisms over the process by which the
mechanisms emerged. The two evidence sources are asymmetric, and the asymmetry is why they compose.
DocAble supplied mechanism depth — full process visibility, but weak external diversity, since it is one
system in one setting. The industrial cases supplied variation — heterogeneous contexts, but weak
internal process tracing, since a finished retrospective rarely shows the months of failure behind the
finished machinery. Together they reach further than either could alone. And together they support
**analytic** generalization rather than statistical generalization: the aim is not to estimate how often
organizations adopt a given practice, but to develop and refine a coherent explanation for why
organizations that differ in nearly everything still converge on similar engineering structures.

Read the industrial analyses accordingly. They are neither independent replications of the originating
case nor evidence that every successful organization must adopt the full MAGE method. They are
comparative reconstructions. Each tests whether the theory stays useful outside the environment it
emerged from, marks where its boundaries fall, and shows where the account can be sharpened. Practitioner
reports cannot establish causation; these corroborate the theory's central mechanisms and expose their
limits, and that is the epistemic weight the book asks them to carry.

## A noble idea with a crushing implementation

<!-- section-terms: document-accessibility, ada-title-ii -->

<!-- point: ada-promises-equal-access | The ADA promises people with disabilities equal access. | terms: ada -->
The Americans with Disabilities Act starts from a simple promise: people with
disabilities should have equal access to public life, able to work, travel, study,
vote, and use services without barriers society could reasonably remove. Passed in 1990
and signed by George H. W. Bush, the ADA extends the American civil-rights commitment
to equal participation for people with disabilities. The wheelchair ramp and the
elevator are its famous results, and they are welcome. But the software
problem grows out of a quieter corner of the statute.

<!-- point: title-ii-now-means-digital | Title II now governs a university's digital communication. | terms: ada-title-ii -->
That corner is Title II, which covers state and local governments — including K–12
schools and public universities. Title II says a public entity must communicate with
people who have disabilities *as effectively* as it communicates with everyone else.
Title II has always reached communication, programs, and services. What changed is where
they live. In 1990 the practical focus was largely physical and in-person: buildings,
lectures, office hours. By 2026 much of a university's communication and service delivery
had moved into digital artifacts — websites, PDFs, slide decks, Word documents,
spreadsheets, learning-management systems, videos, forms, exams, syllabi, and the
thousand little files that pile up wherever people are allowed to upload things.

<!-- point: inaccessible-materials-are-legal-hazard | Inaccessible materials exclude students and invite lawsuits. | terms: document-accessibility -->
If those materials cannot be read by a screen reader, cannot be navigated by keyboard,
lack captions, lack alternative text, or carry their meaning only through color or
layout, then some students get a worse version of the university than others. The
hazard is live, and it is legal as well as moral: accessibility lawsuits are filed
every year by students who were not reasonably accommodated, and institutions have
settled them for sums in the {{lawsuit_settlement_range}} [cite: CarlsonHigherEdAccessibilityLawsuits].

## The rule that changed the burden

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | The rule that changed the burden — the reported count is inflated by the burden-shift table's cells; the prose body is three short paragraphs within the cap, and the table is the section's side-by-side reference, not prose to break up. -->

<!-- section-terms: doj-2024-rule, wcag-conformance -->

<!-- point: compliance-was-reactive-and-unjust | Reactive compliance put the burden on the excluded. | terms: reactive-compliance -->
Universities have been obligated to comply with Title II since 1990. For most of that
time, they complied reactively. A student would hit an inaccessible document, request an
accommodation, and the institution would scramble to respond. That process carries a
morally awkward shape: the inaccessible artifact exists first, and the burden of
discovering it and asking for a fix falls on the person the barrier has already excluded.

<!-- point: doj-rule-made-access-part-of-service | The 2024 DOJ rule made access part of the service. | terms: doj-2024-rule -->
In 2024 the Department of Justice changed the shape. A final rule updated the Title II
regulations for web content and mobile apps, and its premise was blunt. Public entities
should not wait for people with disabilities to discover and report every barrier. The
DOJ put it in the language of physical access: just as stairs can exclude a wheelchair
user from a government building, inaccessible web content and documents can exclude
people from government services. If a public entity provides a service through a
website, an app, or the documents distributed through them, the accessibility of that
content is *part of the service*. Just as Americans do not call ahead to check whether a
building has a ramp, a student should not have to negotiate with a professor over
whether the slides will be readable. [ref:the-burden-shift] lays the shift out side by side.

<!-- label: the-burden-shift -->
<!-- table: The 2024 DOJ rule shifted the accessibility burden from reactive accommodation to proactive access. [short: The burden shift] -->
| Dimension | Reactive accommodation (the old posture) | Proactive access — operational implication for the institution |
|---|---|---|
| Who bears the burden | The excluded student — discover the barrier, then ask for a fix | The institution — build access in before anyone is turned away |
| When access is created | After a barrier is hit, one accommodation at a time | Built in when the content is provided or made available, as part of the service |
| What triggers the work | An accommodation request | Putting the document in front of students |
| The DOJ's own analogy | Calling ahead to ask whether a building has a ramp | The ramp is simply there |
| The standard | Ad hoc, case by case | {{doj_technical_standard}}, on the rule's compliance timeline |
| The scope | The single artifact a student flagged | Decades of accumulated digital sediment |

<!-- point: rule-set-standard-and-deadline | The rule set WCAG 2.1 AA and a deadline. | terms: wcag-conformance, digital-sediment -->
The rule set a technical standard — {{doj_technical_standard}} — and, for large institutions, a
deadline. Universities are not sitting on three PDFs and a cheerful little homepage. They
are sitting on decades of accumulated digital sediment: course sites, admissions forms,
scanned readings, lecture videos, and more. A deadline is a deadline, and this one landed
on a mountain of files no one had ever been asked to climb.

Addressing one issue takes a couple of minutes at best, and the denser the figure, the
longer the fix. Scale that to a course, then to an institution. The assumptions above put
one teaching load's materials at over {{diy_cost_per_course}} of faculty time, and a
research professor teaches more than one course. Multiply by the thousands of courses at
one institution and the number stops being a budget line and starts being a reason the
whole thing gets quietly deferred.

<!-- point: every-escape-hatch-closes-on-inspection | Every escape hatch from the cost closes on inspection. | terms: escape-hatch, remediation-cost -->
There are escape hatches, and each one closes on inspection:

- **Pull the plug.** A university could satisfy the letter of the rule by de-digitizing — removing the online materials so there is nothing inaccessible to fix. It would satisfy the letter and betray the spirit. Digital materials help everyone: captions serve the student in a noisy room and the student still learning English, not only the student who is deaf. No sane institution wants to become the one that took the slides away.
- **Hire it out.** External remediation vendors exist, and they quote on the order of {{vendor_cost_per_page}} per page or slide — roughly {{vendor_cost_per_course_low}} to {{vendor_cost_per_course_high}} for one course's materials at the vendor's own rate. No vendor is staffed with people who can annotate graduate-level astrophysics or quantum mechanics, and no university is eager to pay that per course across every course it offers.
- **Do it yourself.** The professor holds the required subject knowledge, but the institution's promotion and workload systems give that remediation labor little formal value. At a research university, faculty are promoted on research; a large, uncompensated, recurring teaching-service chore is exactly the kind of work the incentive structure teaches them to avoid. Left standing, the burden does worse than go undone: it discourages updating course materials at all, freezing students into whatever version existed when the deadline hit.

<!-- point: the-result-is-a-genuine-problem | Together these make a genuine, unmanufactured problem. | terms: remediation-cost -->
Put these together and you have a genuine problem — no one had to manufacture it. A
federal mandate with real consequences, a mountain of heterogeneous files, expertise
that only the busiest people possess, and no affordable path from here to there.

## Why a document is not just a file

<!-- section-terms: vision-language-model, semantic-gap -->

<!-- point: a-document-is-a-semantic-artifact | A document is a visual and semantic artifact. | terms: semantic-artifact -->
Underneath the institutional bind sits a technical problem deeper than it looks, and it
is why this became a software project worth building rather than a policy memo worth
writing. A document is not merely a file. It is a *visual and semantic* artifact. It
carries text, figures, tables, diagrams, reading order, hierarchy, emphasis, and all the
small tricks by which humans convey meaning to other humans. To make it accessible, a
machine has to recover enough of that meaning to present it through another channel — to
a screen reader, to a keyboard, to a student who cannot see the figure the lecturer drew.

<!-- point: spine-ascent-commodity-intelligence | The frontier vision-language model is the spine's commodity intelligence. | terms: vision-language-model, thesis-modeling -->
<!-- point: vlms-recover-document-meaning | Vision-language models now recover a slide's semantic content. | terms: vision-language-model -->
Earlier systems could automate fragments of the task — pulling text, flagging a missing
title, generating thin alt text — but general semantic recovery from an arbitrary
instructional slide stayed too weak for the system this needed to become. The turn came
with frontier vision-language models. Feed one a rendered slide and it looks at the image and recovers
useful semantic content — describes the figure, reads the equation, names what the slide
is trying to say. That is one of the core technical problems of document accessibility,
and it is now solvable well enough to build on.

<!-- point: spine-ascent-context-problem | The model's missing situated judgment is the context problem. | terms: semantic-gap, infinite-midwit -->
<!-- point: capability-is-not-a-system | Raw model capability is not a compliance strategy. | terms: semantic-gap, demoable-vs-productizable, infinite-midwit -->
Raw model capability is not a compliance strategy, though, and this is the gap the
engineering in this book had to fill. The model looks at a slide and understands something
real. It understands nothing about university procurement, disability-resource workflows,
faculty incentives, or the institutional alchemy that turns a federal mandate into a weekly
email nobody reads. It is, as I like to put it, an infinite midwit: it knows everything and
understands nothing. The phrase is deliberately unfair to the component. The real limit is
not intelligence in general but the absence of the institution's goals, workflows, authority,
and consequences — the model has fluent knowledge and no situated judgment. As the earlier
chapters kept showing, a flash of capability is not a system. You get one only by bounding
what you ask, typing what comes back, and validating before you believe. Turning that flash
into a system a university can trust is engineering.

<!-- point: context-is-law-deadline-cost-and-gap | The context: real law, deadline, cost, bridgeable gap. | terms: document-accessibility, semantic-gap -->
That is the context. A real law, a real deadline, a real cost, and a real technical gap
that had just barely become bridgeable. The next chapter tells what happened when one
professor decided to bridge it — and how a one-week undergraduate project became a
production system built almost entirely by machines.
