# The Build

> ### This chapter illustrates {#illustrates}
>
> ✓ The Printer · ✓ The Governed Engineering Environment · ✓ Governance Conversion

<!-- point: methods-are-sediment-not-theory | Every method was learned from a specific failure, afterward. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The last chapter set the bar. This one is the receipt for clearing it: how the build actually went,
from a five-minute experiment in the back of a committee meeting to a production system that, by the
end, was writing almost all of its own code. Every governance idea in this book was learned at a
specific point on that arc, in response to a specific failure. The methods are not a theory that came
first. They are the sediment left by the work.

<!-- point: the-fleet-outproduced-any-human-reader | A fleet produced more code than any human could read. | terms: fleet-scale -->
The scale is easy to state and hard to believe. Over about {{study_weeks}} weeks of focused effort, a
fleet of coding agents and I produced {{prod_loc}} lines of production code, {{support_loc}} lines of
support apparatus, {{iac_loc}} lines of infrastructure-as-code, and {{system_model_loc}} lines of
system models bridging the two (counted at {{measurement_snapshot}}; category definitions live in the
evidence ledger). On a routine day {{agents_parallel}} agents ran in parallel and landed on the order
of {{commits_per_day}} commits — about {{commits_per_week}} a week, sustained. I inspected almost none
of the code. That one fact is the engineering problem this book exists to answer: how a fleet
producing more code than any human can read stays trustworthy.

## It started in a meeting

<!-- section-terms: feasibility-probe, demoable-vs-productizable -->

<!-- point: a-bored-experiment-in-a-meeting | A bored meeting experiment recovered a slide's meaning. | terms: feasibility-probe, vision-language-model -->
The project began in a committee meeting about the very deadline the last chapter described. While the
meeting droned on, I ran a small experiment: I opened a chat model, fed it screenshots from one of my
own slide decks, and asked it to transcribe what it saw. It worked. The model recovered real semantic
content from a rendered slide — the core technical problem of document accessibility, solved well
enough to build on. That single positive result converted a research question into an engineering
project. What it did *not* do defined the gap the rest of the build had to close: it did not export a
corrected file, add reading order, validate against any standard, or understand anything about the
institution the file lived in.

<!-- point: undergrads-declined-so-agents-instead | Students declined, so the project ran on an agent fleet. | terms: supervised-autonomy -->
The first plan was small and conventional — hand a one-week alt-text task to an undergraduate or two —
but no student staffing appeared, so the project proceeded with an agent fleet rather than a
conventional implementation team. I bought a subscription, opened an empty Git repository, and told
myself one month should be plenty.

## Seven stages, drawn afterward

<!-- section-terms: stage-sequence, governance-target-product -->

<!-- point: each-stage-driven-by-a-concrete-pressure | Each stage answered a concrete pressure, enlarging product and machinery. | terms: stage-sequence -->
The build moved through a recognizable sequence of stages — recognizable only afterward. Each was
driven by a concrete pressure (a colleague's request, a user's complaint, a deadline, a failure), and
each enlarged both the product *and* the machinery that kept the product trustworthy. [ref:build-stages]
lays out the sequence.

[short: The seven build stages and what each forced] -->
| Stage | The pressure | What it added to the product | What it forced in the machinery |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · Feasibility probe | A bored moment in the accreditation meeting | Slide screenshots submitted to a vision-language model | Proof that alt-text generation was viable at all |
| 2 · PowerPoint | Colleagues asking for their own decks | A command-line walker over slides: alt text, titles, contrast, reading order | The Microsoft checker as first proxy for "accessible" — useful but incomplete |
| 3 · Format expansion | Documents from many sources | Word and Excel on the same Office walker; PDF as a distinct problem, not "Office with a different extension" | The first real architecture: a type classifier, a vision pass, a two-pass analyze-then-remediate model |
| 4 · SaaS | Real users on real documents | A website, a containerized worker, a cloud deployment | Auth, metered billing, quota and cost controls, a security review — and the limit of trust: some outputs passed the checkers while losing the meaning |
| 5 · Standards conformance | The need for a defensible accessibility claim | Each finding mapped to a specific WCAG 2.1 AA, Section 508, or PDF/UA clause | A standards-grounded check engine, so remediation and verification share one vocabulary |
| 6 · Hardening | A one-year DOJ deadline extension | A stabilized product | Typed commits, isolated worktrees, validation and fidelity gates, provenance stamps, property and regression tests, audit agents, fuzzing, deploy gates |
| 7 · Serverless | An idle Kubernetes cluster costing too much | A function-as-a-service shape, capacity scaling on demand | The decision and first working deploy in one day; ~{{serverless_commits}} commits of rewiring across {{serverless_phase_count}} phased designs |

<!-- point: the-stages-are-drawn-afterward | The clean stages are a line drawn afterward. | terms: stage-sequence -->
These stages are retrospective labels, not a preplanned roadmap. The clean stages read like a plan.
They were not one. They were reconstructed afterward from ordinary engineering events: a context
window exhausted mid-task and forced a handoff protocol; subscription limits turned model access into
a schedulable resource; unattended runs exposed the need for authoritative agent state; an overnight
build generated unexpected cloud charges and forced cost controls; deployment failures exposed
couplings that static checks could not see. Each event either revealed a missing property of the
system or forced a mechanism that later became part of MAGE.

<!-- point: the-apparatus-grew-faster-than-the-product | The governing apparatus grew faster than the product. | terms: governance-target-product, support-ratio -->
The two-line summary of the whole sequence: the product grew, and the support apparatus around it grew
faster. Measured in source size, that apparatus — testing, modeling, orchestration, documentation, and
governance, plus the tooling that runs them — ran about {{support_ratio}} times the size of the
production code, and the test suite alone was roughly the size of the product. A ratio like that reads
as gold-plating until you recall that at {{commits_per_day}} commits a day no human reads the diffs.
The apparatus carries the repeatable review burden no human could perform per diff (more in [the road
to MAGE](5.3-the-road-to-mage.html)); the architectural and semantic judgment it cannot perform stays
with me. It is the price of the velocity.

## The velocity curve {#velocity}

<!-- section-terms: velocity-curve -->

[ref:velocity-curve] plots commits per week: a steep rise as the fleet gained capability, then a dip
during the hardening interval, when the work record shifts toward mechanisms and validation.

<!-- label: velocity-curve -->
<!-- figure: assets/velocity-commits-per-week.svg | Commits per week across the project's history — the velocity curve. Each bar is one week; the curve rises as the fleet gains capability, then dips during hardening. That dip coincides with the hardening interval. -->

<!-- point: the-dip-is-velocity-spent-on-trust | The velocity dip is capability spent making output trustworthy. | terms: velocity-curve -->
The repository's own activity records the shape of the effort. Commits per week rose steeply through
the MVP stages — feasibility, PowerPoint, formats, service — then fell during hardening. Read against
the commit classification, the dip is not a slowdown but a change of character: from producing
features to producing the mechanisms that let features be produced safely. The classification, not the
bar height, tells you what changed. Read that way, the curve is the operator's own shift from coder to
architect, drawn as a line — an interpretation of the coded record, not a measurement the bars make on
their own. A fleet run this hard needs machinery that holds the line when its operator cannot, because
the operator's attention is exactly what runs out first.

## The controls accumulate {#control-growth}

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: the-control-substrate-grew-from-zero | The control substrate grew from zero. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The apparatus shows up as more than lines. It shows up as controls — the lints and gates the
environment accrues one class at a time as the fleet surfaces the failures that demand them, nearly all
discovered ex-post, obligations no up-front specification would have named. [ref:control-growth-curve]
counts its two hardest control surfaces — project-specific lint files and gate scripts — at the same
four windows the build passed through.

<!-- label: control-growth-curve -->
<!-- figure: assets/control-growth.svg | The controls accumulate across four windows — lint files (0 to 336 to 595 to 747) and gate scripts (0 to 20 to 76 to 102). Both start at literal zero: the substrate is post-prototype. The climb is steepest through mechanization and rises after, one class at a time. -->

The curve is the accumulation, not its cause. Those lint files carry 993 registered lint specs at the
final window, each a policy the environment now enforces on every agent. What the count cannot separate
is which controls a failure drove and which the domain suggested up front — the two modes interleave by
design, and no commit trail cleanly splits them. But the discipline that converts a failure is
documented and named: a paired fix-and-lint tag marks the commits that ship a code fix beside the check
that forbids its recurrence, appearing in 208 commits, and 27 lints name a specific dated incident in
their own text, spot-checked as genuine conversions. Read the curve as a documented, growing
discipline, then — not a measured causal rate. [data: control-growth]

## The support ratio: build the environment first {#support-ratio-curve}

<!-- section-terms: support-ratio, governance-target-product -->

<!-- index-def: support-ratio -->
The apparatus outgrowing the product is not a one-time fact; it is a curve. Measure the support
apparatus against the production code at four dated commits and the revealed preference of the whole
build falls out in one line. [ref:support-ratio-curve] plots it.

<!-- label: support-ratio-curve -->
<!-- figure: assets/support-ratio-curve.svg | *The Support Ratio.* Support-apparatus lines divided by production lines, across four windows: it starts below parity (0.85×), passes production at mechanization (2.48×), peaks during hardening (3.68×), then eases to 3.06×. The support line sits above parity for every mature window: the apparatus stayed larger than production across the mature windows. -->

<!-- point: spine-ascent-engineering-capital | The support ratio is the environment banked as capital. | terms: support-ratio, governance-target-product -->
Read the curve as a decision, not an accident. In the prototype the apparatus was smaller than the
product — 0.85×, below parity — because there was barely a product to govern. By mechanization it had
crossed production to 2.48×, and by hardening it peaked at 3.68×, roughly three and a half times the
code it guarded. The governance was built *first*, then the features leaned on it. The final-window
dip to 3.06× is not a retreat: support kept growing in absolute terms; production simply grew faster
as feature work resumed on the finished environment. Lines of code are a coarse proxy for effort, and
this is a revealed preference across one repository, not a controlled comparison — but the apparatus
crossed from sub-parity to about three times the product, built out ahead of the features that leaned
on it.

## The shape of the churn {#churn}

<!-- section-terms: churn -->

Under the ratio sits the raw motion of the code: lines added and deleted, week over week, on the two
paths that carry the product — `web/`, the Python service and worker, and `backend/`, the C# tool and
rule engine. The preface named churn as the wall a fleet hits; traced per path across the four windows
it has a clear silhouette. [ref:churn-per-path] draws it.

<!-- label: churn-per-path -->
<!-- figure: assets/churn-per-path.svg | Repository line motion across four windows — additions rise above the baseline, deletions fall below it, one bar per path. Mechanization is the add-and-delete peak, where backend/ rewrote itself; after it deletions collapse and later windows go net-additive as the environment stabilizes the code. -->

<!-- point: churn-book-level-result | Restructuring peaked at mechanization; deletions then collapsed. | terms: churn -->
Mechanization — the wave where the fleet learned to build the environment — is where both paths move
hardest; `backend/` alone rewrote itself in that window. Two constructs stay apart: *repository
motion* is added and deleted lines, what git counts; *churn in the theoretical sense* is effort spent
undoing and reconciling rather than advancing. The line motion is a proxy for restructuring intensity,
not a direct measurement of wasted effort. The book-level result is the shape: restructuring peaked
during mechanization, then the deletions collapsed and both paths turned net-additive, window after
window — consistent with a period of structural investment followed by stabilization, though the case
cannot establish that governance caused the decline.[^churn-accounting]

[^churn-accounting]: These counts come from git's own line accounting, so generated bundles and vendored trees ride along with hand-authored source. Partitioned by path, `backend/` carries zero generated lines; `web/` carries 14.6% of added lines, almost all in the mechanization window (24.5% there) when the TypeScript-to-JavaScript build output and wire-contract codegen were still tracked, dropping to 1–2% once that output was git-ignored; across both paths combined it is 5.2%, because `backend/` dominates and carries none of it. Read the bars as a churn *signal*, predominantly non-generated tracked source.

That churn had a direct price in tokens. At one point I was running four concurrent Claude Max
subscriptions to keep the fleet fed; once the models carried the context, the same velocity ran on two
— an observation from this one case, not a measured law. The mechanism is the one this section traces:
a model lets each agent find what it needs instead of re-deriving it, so the work stays in-window, off
the compaction cliff where the token bill jumps. That is the **tokenomics** of churn, and it is why
keeping a task inside the window is not merely tidier but cheaper.

<!-- gloss: Tokenomics | How many tokens it takes to get a task done — and it is not linear. Overflow the context window and the cost jumps: the harness compacts (a lossy save), and the agent then burns tokens rebuilding the context it lost. So keeping a task inside the window is not merely tidier, it is far cheaper — which is exactly what a model buys, by shrinking what the agent must hold. -->

The raw counts behind these three curves — support-apparatus and production lines per window, the
per-path churn, and the running control-growth totals — are collected as lookup tables in the evidence

For a stretch of the build, nearly every unwelcome surprise came from deployment, and they shared a
shape. The unit tests stayed green; the lints passed. What broke was never a statement in a file but a
*behavior of the running system* — a cold start a hundred times its warm latency, a gate budget sized
for a regime that no longer held, two services contending for a core, a dependency graph that woke in
the wrong order. None of these lived in the statics. They lived in the dynamics, invisible to any
check that reads one layer alone, because each coupling ran across five layers at once: the code, the
lints that guard it, the tests that exercise it, the infrastructure-as-code that places it, and the
deploy pipeline that sequences it.

That is the case for testing the dynamics, not only the statics. A one-off fix to a cold start clears
today's surprise and leaves the next to be found the same expensive way. The durable answer turns each
discovered dynamic into a mechanism that watches it: a measured cold-start number fed to a model that
recomputes the worst-case path, a budget derived rather than guessed, an ordering asserted rather than
assumed. The coupling is not on the diff, so a finite review never had a chance against it — only a
mechanism watching the whole running dynamic holds the line as the fleet's deploys speed up.

## The kinds of work it required

<!-- section-terms: kinds-of-work, governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: read-it-as-a-catalog-of-work-kinds | The timeline is a catalog of work agentic development needs. | terms: kinds-of-work -->
Read the build as a catalog of the *kinds of work* agentic development turned out to need — more
useful than any feature list. The code was cheap. Everything around it was where the judgment went,
and the same handful of activities recurred at every stage: **framing the problem** (what "accessible"
even meant for a math-dense graduate deck, where the file alone underdetermines the answer);
**discovering abstractions** (recognizing PDF as a distinct problem, and building structured models so
structure was compiler-checked instead of passed as anonymous primitives); **deciding local versus
structural** (for each failure, judging whether it was a one-off bug or the symptom of a missing
mechanism — the single most repeated act in the whole record); **building the checks**
(standards-grounded validation, content-preservation checks that assert the input survives into the
output, hundreds of project lints atop a maxed-out commodity floor, a tiered regression suite from
smoke tests to fuzz campaigns); **governing the fleet** (isolated worktrees, a pre-commit gate whose
passage is proven by a hash-keyed marker file, an agent registry that authoritatively answers "who is
in flight"); and **operating and deploying** (staged deploy gates, quota and cost controls,
observability channels, audit trails, and runbooks for the failures that recur).

<!-- point: the-recurring-move-classify-then-convert | Velocity surfaces failure; judgment converts structural ones to mechanism. | terms: governance-conversion, thesis-alignment -->
Read as a list, those are the chapters of this book. Every one answers a failure this build produced,
and the recurring move that produced them all is the same: *velocity surfaces a failure; judgment
classifies it as local or structural; the structural ones get converted into a durable mechanism that
narrows the next agent's room to fail.*

<!-- point: the-system-reached-production-for-a-few-dollars-a-deck | By the end the system remediated a deck for dollars. | terms: document-accessibility -->
By the end of the study period the system could process Office and PDF files to pass or substantially
improve their accessibility scores — a representative graduate deck in {{per_deck_process_time}} for
{{per_deck_process_cost}} on a warm-start service, against the {{diy_cost_per_course}} a hand-
remediated course would cost. Direct development cost was {{dev_cost_total}}, most of it salary, with a
few thousand each in model inference, cloud hosting, and subscriptions. That is the arc: as
implementation accelerated, engineering effort migrated into the environment around it — into the
apparatus that held the line where a reader no longer could. That migration is the subject of the
chapters that follow.
