# The Build

> ### This chapter illustrates {#illustrates}
>
> ✓ The Printer · ✓ The Governed Engineering Environment · ✓ Governance Conversion

<!-- point: methods-are-sediment-not-theory | Every method was learned from a specific failure, afterward. | terms: governance-conversion -->
The last chapter set the bar. This one is the receipt for clearing it: how the build
actually went, from a five-minute experiment in the back of a committee meeting to a
production system that, by the end, was writing almost all of its own code. Every
governance idea in this book was learned at a specific point on that arc, in response to a
specific failure. The methods are not a theory that came first. They are the sediment left
by the work.

<!-- point: the-fleet-outproduced-any-human-reader | A fleet produced more code than any human could read. | terms: fleet-scale -->
The scale is easy to state and hard to believe. Over about {{study_weeks}} weeks of
focused effort, a fleet of AI coding agents and I produced a repository
of {{prod_loc}} lines of production code, {{support_loc}} lines of support apparatus,
{{iac_loc}} lines of infrastructure-as-code, and {{system_model_loc}} lines of system
models bridging the two (counted at {{measurement_snapshot}} by the categorized line-count
census; category definitions live in the ledger). On a routine day, {{agents_parallel}}
agents ran in parallel and landed on the order of {{commits_per_day}} commits — about
{{commits_per_week}} a week, sustained. I
inspected almost none of the code. That one fact is the engineering problem this book
exists to answer: how a fleet producing more code than any human can read stays trustworthy.

## It started in a meeting

<!-- section-terms: feasibility-probe, demoable-vs-productizable -->

<!-- point: a-bored-experiment-in-a-meeting | A bored meeting experiment recovered a slide's meaning. | terms: feasibility-probe, vision-language-model -->
The project began, fittingly, in a committee meeting about the very deadline described in
the last chapter. In early 2026 an accreditation committee was discussing the ADA
compliance date. Most of the faculty in the room did not know a mandate existed; those who
did had made little progress against it. While the meeting droned on, I did
what an engineering professor does when bored by a conundrum: I ran a small experiment. I
opened a chat model, fed it screenshots from one of my own slide decks, and asked it to
transcribe what it saw. It worked. The model looked at a rendered slide and recovered real
semantic content from the image — the core technical problem of document accessibility,
solved well enough to build on.

<!-- point: one-result-converted-research-to-engineering | One positive result turned a research question into engineering. | terms: feasibility-probe, demoable-vs-productizable -->
That single positive result was the decision point. It converted a research question into
an engineering project. What it did *not* do defines the gap
the rest of the timeline had to close: the model did not export a corrected file, did not
add reading order, did not validate against any standard, and understood nothing about the
institution the file lived in. A flash of capability is not a system.

<!-- point: undergrads-declined-so-agents-instead | Students declined, so he supervised agents instead. | terms: supervised-autonomy -->
The first plan was small and conventional. Draft a one-week project — automated alt-text
for the equations in instructional materials — and hand it to an undergraduate or two over
Spring Break. I posted the offer to twenty of the department's best students.
Not one replied; they had, reasonably, planned to have fun. So I decided to
supervise agents instead of undergraduates. I bought a subscription, opened an empty Git
repository, and told myself one month should be plenty.

## Six stages, then a seventh

<!-- section-terms: stage-sequence, governance-target-product -->

<!-- point: each-stage-driven-by-a-concrete-pressure | Each stage answered a concrete pressure, enlarging product and machinery. | terms: stage-sequence -->
The build moved through a recognizable sequence of stages — recognizable only afterward.
Each was driven by a concrete pressure: a colleague's request, a user's complaint, a
deadline, a failure. And each enlarged both the product *and* the machinery that kept the
product trustworthy. [ref:build-stages] lays out that sequence; the sections after it read the
evidence it left behind.

<!-- label: build-stages -->
<!-- table: The seven build stages — each driven by a concrete pressure, each enlarging both the product and the machinery that governs it. From a feasibility probe in a meeting to a serverless migration; the pressure column is the failure or demand that forced the stage, the last two columns what it added to the product and to the apparatus. [short: The seven build stages and what each forced] -->
| Stage | The pressure | What it added to the product | What it forced in the machinery |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · Feasibility probe | A bored moment in the accreditation meeting | Screenshots of mathematics and figures submitted to a vision-language model | Proof that alt-text generation was viable at all |
| 2 · PowerPoint remediation | Colleagues asking for their own decks | A command-line walker over PowerPoint slides — descriptions written in place, plus slide titles, contrast, reading order, and a falling Microsoft-checker warning count | The checker as first proxy for "accessible" — useful but incomplete, as Stage 4 would force us to confront |
| 3 · Format expansion | Documents from many sources, breaking the clean local decks' assumptions | Word and Excel on the same Office walker; PDF as a distinct problem, not "Office with a different extension" | The first real architecture: a document-type classifier, a vision pass to recover untagged figures, a two-pass "analyze, then remediate" model |
| 4 · SaaS | Real users on real documents | A website, a containerized worker, a cloud deployment, live within days | Authentication, metered billing, quota and cost controls, an early security review — and the limit of trust: a lower warning count is not the job done, and some outputs passed the checkers while losing the meaning |
| 5 · Standards conformance | The need for a defensible accessibility claim | Each finding mapped to a specific WCAG 2.1 AA, Section 508, or PDF/UA clause | A standards-grounded check engine of its own, so remediation and verification share one vocabulary and run after every pass — grounding the checks in the published standards without claiming to be a conformance authority |
| 6 · Hardening | A one-year DOJ deadline extension — emergency response to production | A stabilized product | Typed commits, isolated agent worktrees, validation and fidelity gates, provenance stamps, property and regression tests, audit agents, fuzzing, deploy gates |
| 7 · Serverless | Too much money on a Kubernetes cluster that mostly sat idle | A function-as-a-service shape, so capacity scales on demand rather than billing around the clock | The decision and first working deploy in one day; ~{{serverless_commits}} commits of rewiring in {{serverless_phase_count}} phased designs, each surfacing a handful of judgment-call questions before it ran |

<!-- gloss: Worktree | A private, checked-out copy of the repository given to a single agent, so its in-progress edits never collide with another agent working at the same time. -->

<!-- point: the-apparatus-grew-faster-than-the-product | The governing apparatus grew faster than the product. | terms: governance-target-product, support-ratio -->
The two-line summary of the whole sequence: the product grew, and the *support apparatus
around it grew faster*. Measured in source size, that apparatus — testing, modeling,
orchestration, documentation, and governance, plus the tooling that runs them — ran about
{{support_ratio}} times the size of the production code, and the test suite alone was roughly
the size of the product. A ratio like that reads as gold-plating until you recall that at
{{commits_per_day}} commits a day no human reads the diffs. The apparatus carries the
repeatable review burden no human could perform per diff (more in
[the built system](5.3-the-road-to-mage.html)); the architectural and semantic judgment it
cannot perform stays with me. It is the price of the velocity.

<!-- label: velocity-curve -->

<!-- point: the-dip-is-velocity-spent-on-trust | The velocity dip is capability spent making output trustworthy. | terms: velocity-curve, velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
The repository's own activity records the shape of the effort. Commits per week rose steeply
through the MVP stages — feasibility, PowerPoint, formats, service — then fell during
hardening. Read against the commit classification, the dip is not a slowdown but a change of
character: from producing features to producing the mechanisms that let features be produced
safely. The bar heights mark the interval; the classification, not the height, tells you
what changed. Read that way, the curve is my own shift from coder to architect, drawn as a
line — an interpretation of the coded record, not a measurement the bars make on their own.

<!-- point: the-lesson-is-the-machinery-not-the-grind | The lesson is the machinery, not the operator's grind. | terms: velocity-curve -->
The pace was extreme, and the human cost is part of the record — set down in the field note
below, not celebrated here. The methodological point is the durable one: a fleet run this
hard needs machinery that holds the line when its operator cannot, because the operator's
attention is exactly what runs out first.

<!-- point: a-hot-fleet-needs-machinery-when-the-operator-cannot | A hot fleet needs machinery holding the line unattended. | terms: velocity-curve, governance-target-agent -->

> ### Field note — the unhealthy pace is not the method {#fieldnote-body-schedule}
>
> The pace left marks on the days. Sleep dropped to four or five hours a night; to stay
> ahead of the subscription rate limits I ran up to {{peak_accounts}} top-tier plans in
> round-robin and scheduled my own body across them. Once, to unlock a third account when two
> were saturated, I needed a two-factor code from a student off at a corporate retreat, and a
> second student at the next table walked it over — *three-factor authentication*, I called it.
> The real lesson was plainer: that 2FA code was not authentication so much as another unit of
> synthetic labor coming online. I worked at the kitchen counter while
> the family slept; during a campus network outage I sprinted between two buildings so the
> agents would not stall waiting on me. **This pace is evidence about the experiment, not a
> recommended operating method.** A production discipline should *reduce* dependence on an
> operator behaving this way — the whole point of the machinery in the chapters that follow
> is to let the line be held without it. Keep the detail for what it shows about the system,
> not as something to emulate.

Read the curve as a decision, not an accident. In the prototype the apparatus was smaller
than the product — 0.85×, below parity — because there was barely a product to govern. By
mechanization it had crossed production to 2.48×, and by hardening it peaked at 3.68×:
roughly three and a half times the code it guarded. The governance was built *first*, then
the features leaned on it. The support ratio is the Governed Engineering Environment measured
against the product it governs.

The final-window dip to 3.06× is not a retreat. Support did not fall — it grew another 21%
in absolute terms; production simply grew faster, up 45%, as feature work resumed on the
finished environment. A falling ratio here signals a *stabilized* foundation being spent, not
an abandoned one. Across the whole span the apparatus crossed from sub-parity to about three
times the product while production itself grew roughly {{prod_growth_multiple}} — larger than
production across every mature window, built out ahead of the features that leaned on it.

One honesty note keeps the number from overclaiming. The census fails loud over seven primary
source roots, so the **counting-surface gap is zero** at every window — no file the tool was
asked to count slipped past it. About a fifth of the raw tree sits outside the census by
design (root configuration, assets, non-primary test corpora, this book itself), and that
side-tree share is roughly flat across the mature windows, so the curve tracks a real shift in
how effort was spent, not an artifact of the category map moving under it. Lines of code
remain a coarse proxy for effort; the ratio is a revealed preference across one repository and
one fleet, not a controlled comparison of governed against ungoverned work.


## The shape of the churn {#churn}

<!-- section-terms: churn -->

Under the ratio sits the raw motion of the code: lines added and lines deleted, week over
week, on the two paths that carry the product — `web/`, the Python service and worker, and
`backend/`, the C# tool and rule engine. The preface named churn as the wall a fleet hits.
Trace it per path across the four windows and the wall has a clear silhouette.
[ref:churn-per-path] draws it; [ref:churn-per-path-table] gives the counts.

Mechanization — the wave where the fleet learned to build the environment — is where both
paths move hardest; `backend/` alone rewrote itself in that window. Two constructs stay apart
here: **repository motion** is added and deleted lines, what git counts; **churn in the
theoretical sense** is effort spent undoing and reconciling rather than advancing. The line
motion is a proxy for restructuring intensity, not a direct measurement of wasted effort.
Then the deletions collapse and both paths turn net-additive, window after window — consistent
with stabilization after mechanization and hardening, though the case cannot isolate how much
of the decline the governing apparatus caused rather than ordinary product maturation.

One caveat keeps the numbers honest, and a measurement now bounds it. These counts come from
git's own line accounting, so generated bundles and vendored trees ride along with
hand-authored source. Partitioning each changed line by path shows how much that inflates the
totals. On `backend/` the answer is none: zero generated lines. On `web/` it is 14.6% of added
lines, almost all in the mechanization window (24.5% there), when the TypeScript-to-JavaScript
build output and wire-contract codegen were still tracked; once the compiled output was
git-ignored the generated share dropped to 1-2%. Across both paths combined it is 5.2%, because
`backend/` dominates and carries none of it. So read the bars as a churn *signal*, not a
hand-authored source count — the inflation is bounded and time-localized, and the curve is
**predominantly non-generated tracked source** everywhere else.

For a stretch of the build, nearly every unwelcome surprise came from deployment, and they
shared a shape. The unit tests stayed green; the lints passed. What broke was never a
statement in a file but a *behavior of the running system* — a cold start a hundred times its
warm latency, a gate budget sized for a regime that no longer held, two services contending
for a core, a dependency graph that woke in the wrong order. None of these lived in the
statics. They lived in the dynamics, invisible to any check that reads one layer alone,
because each coupling ran across five layers at once: the code, the lints that guard it, the
tests that exercise it, the infrastructure-as-code that places it, and the deploy pipeline
that sequences it.

That is the case for testing the dynamics, not only the statics — and for the harder move
underneath it. A one-off fix to a cold start clears today's surprise and leaves the next to be
found the same expensive way, on the next real deploy. The durable answer turns each discovered
dynamic into a mechanism that watches it: a measured cold-start number fed to a model that
recomputes the worst-case path, a budget derived rather than guessed, an ordering asserted
rather than assumed. A finite review of a diff never had a chance against these — the coupling
is not on the diff. Only a mechanism watching the whole running dynamic holds the line as the
fleet's deploys speed up.


## The kinds of work it required

<!-- section-terms: kinds-of-work, governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: read-it-as-a-catalog-of-work-kinds | The timeline is a catalog of work agentic development needs. | terms: kinds-of-work -->
Read this timeline as a catalog of the *kinds of work* agentic development turned out to need
— more useful than any feature list. The code was cheap. Everything around the code was where
the judgment went, and the same handful of activities recurred at every stage:

- **Framing the problem.** Deciding what "accessible" even meant for a math-dense graduate deck, where the file alone underdetermines the answer.
- **Discovering abstractions.** Recognizing PDF as a distinct problem, not "Office with a different extension," and building structured models so structure was compiler-checked instead of passed as anonymous primitives.
- **Deciding local versus structural.** For each failure, judging whether it was a one-off bug or the symptom of a missing mechanism — the single most repeated act in the whole record.
- **Building the checks.** Standards-grounded validation, content-preservation checks that assert the input survives into the output, hundreds of project-specific lints atop a maxed-out commodity floor, and a tiered regression suite from smoke tests to fuzz campaigns.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [content-preservation checks](appendix-c-content-validator.html).

- **Governing the fleet.** A dispatch-and-track workflow for parallel agents, isolated worktrees, a pre-commit gate whose passage is proven by a hash-keyed marker file, an agent registry that authoritatively answers "who is in flight," and lifecycle records for every agent that lands or dies.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [pre-commit gate](appendix-a-pre-commit-hook.html).

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [agent registry](appendix-a-agent-registry.html).

- **Operating and deploying.** Cloud deployment with staged gates, quota and cost controls, observability channels, audit trails that reconstruct a finished document's history, and runbooks for the failures that recur.

<!-- point: spine-ascent-governance-conversion | Velocity surfaces failure; judgment converts structural ones to mechanism. | terms: governance-conversion, thesis-alignment -->
<!-- point: the-recurring-move-classify-then-convert | Velocity surfaces failure; judgment converts structural ones to mechanism. | terms: governance-conversion, thesis-alignment -->
Read as a list, those are the chapters of this book. Every one answers a failure this
timeline produced, and the recurring move that produced them all is the same:
*velocity surfaces a failure; judgment classifies it as local or structural; the structural
ones get converted into a durable mechanism that narrows the next agent's room to fail.* That
move, applied over and over, is what the rest of this book laid out, told slowly enough that
you can do it too.

By the end of the study period the system could process Office and PDF files to pass or
substantially improve their accessibility scores. A representative graduate slide deck
processed in {{per_deck_process_time}} for {{per_deck_process_cost}}, under a warm-start
service on the study's model mix; a cold start or a heavier deck costs more, and the metrics

yet adopted or supported as an institutional university service — under the name

is the arc. How it was governed is the story the rest of this book has already told.
