# Inside DocAble's Software Factory

<!-- point: one-engineer-directed-a-fleet | Earlier chapters teach product decisions and governing mechanisms; this section examines the factory through which one engineer directed coding agents. | terms: fleet-scale -->

<!-- point: a-factory-contains-more-than-its-fabricators | DocAble's governed engineering environment was part of a factory that also included people, agents, processes, and arrangements governing engineering decisions. -->

Chapters {{chapter:modeling|num}}–{{chapter:one-problem-many-models|num}} examined engineering decisions that shaped DocAble and the models and controls used to govern its development. Here the focus shifts from the product to the factory that produced it. DocAble is a document-accessibility remediation service; its software factory is the engineered system through which I directed coding agents to build and change that service, evaluated their work, and decided which changes to accept. The governed engineering environment (GEE) developed in the preceding chapters formed part of this factory: its models represented the product and selected engineering obligations, while its tools and controls helped agents perform work and established which changes could be accepted. The factory also included the people and agents performing the work, the processes through which changes moved, and the arrangements governing engineering decisions.

The measurements cover {{seip_weeks}} complete weeks, from March 12 through October 14, 2026. By October 14, DocAble contained {{seip_prod_loc}} lines of production code supported by {{seip_apparatus_loc}} lines of tests, checks, tools, infrastructure, models, and indexed documentation. Its engineering environment contained {{seip_models}} model modules.

<!-- point: the-engineers-role-was-engineering-not-implementation | Goals, users, production failures, standards and institutional use supplied engineering work; the engineer made consequential decisions while coding agents wrote every implementation line. | terms: governed-engineering-environment -->

New work came from my goals for the system, user feedback, production failures, accessibility standards, public guidance from advocacy groups [cite: nfb-accessible-documents-2014], and the demands of institutional use. I decided which pressures warranted engineering work and made consequential decisions about requirements, architecture, validation, and deployment. Coding agents wrote every line of the implementation. I inspected almost none of the code they produced.

<!-- point: product-decisions-became-factory-obligations | Process design organized work and tolerance preserved consequential requirements while agents retained implementation freedom; failures could require changes to both the product and its factory. -->

That division of work required process design and tolerance. Work had to be selected, planned, dispatched, integrated, and evaluated without making me the bottleneck for every implementation decision. At the same time, agents needed freedom to choose implementations without being free to violate consequential requirements. The factory therefore developed models, procedures, tools, and controls that carried selected decisions into later work. Failures often required two repairs: correcting the product and changing the factory to prevent or reject the same class of failure in subsequent agentic work.

The case follows the factory’s development from initial requirements through production operation. Its chronology matters because the machinery did not arrive fully formed. Implementation, institutional use, and observed failures exposed engineering decisions that needed to persist; the factory changed to carry them.

## The Problem {#nearly-free}

In 2024, the U.S. Department of Justice required state and local governments, including public universities and schools, to make covered web content and mobile applications conform to {{doj_technical_standard}} [cite: doj-web-accessibility-2024]. Purdue trained undergraduates to remediate course materials, providing both a practical reference workflow and the corpus used in the evaluation below. A checker can identify some defects, but supplying a useful description of a diagram or restoring the structure of a document requires interpreting its content. {{chapter:problem}} introduced that difficulty; here it becomes a specification for a service.

The requirements were ordinary production-software requirements. What was unusual was the implementation capacity available to one engineer.

## Developing the Product and Factory {#building-the-factory}

### The Feasibility Probe

<!-- point: the-probe-tested-the-uncertain-part-first | The probe supplied initial evidence of semantic reasoning on equations, technical slides and handwritten notes. | terms: feasibility-probe, vision-language-model -->

I tested the uncertain capability first. I gave a multimodal foundation model pictures of mathematical equations, technical slides, and handwritten notes. It produced useful transcriptions and alternative text. Generative AI appeared capable of performing the semantic reasoning I needed.

<!-- point: cost-was-only-one-reason-not-to-hand-the-document-to-the-model | The initial semantic capability supported building a remediation system around the foundation model; preservation, reviewability, conformance, dependable operation, and practical cost remained engineering obligations. -->

I decided not to make the foundation model the whole remediation system. To use its semantic capability in a service, I would also have to establish document preservation, reviewable changes, conformance, dependable operation, and practical cost.

### The Two Rules of Agentic Software Engineering

<!-- point: not-reading-the-code-was-deliberate | Not reading the code was deliberate: inspecting agent-produced implementation would have bounded implementation throughput by the engineer's own reading capacity, so the first rule made reading the last resort and an engineering environment the surface through which work was directed and judged. | terms: fleet-scale -->

I began with two rules.

**Reading the code is the last resort.** If building DocAble required me to inspect the implementation produced by coding agents, then implementation throughput would remain bounded by my capacity to read code. I wanted to build an engineering environment in which I could direct work, understand the system, evaluate evidence, and decide whether changes were acceptable without routinely reconstructing those answers from implementation.

<!-- point: reading-the-code-was-the-escape-hatch | The rule did not forbid reading source: needing to read it pointed to a missing supervisory surface — a model, test, lint, instrument, or architectural boundary — so reading was the escape hatch, not the operating model. | terms: fleet-scale -->

This did not mean that I refused to read source code. It meant that needing to read it pointed to a missing supervisory surface: a model, test, lint, instrument, architectural boundary, or other representation of the property I actually needed to reason about. Reading the code was the escape hatch, not the operating model.

<!-- point: good-fast-and-cheap-became-a-rule-for-the-factory | The second rule required the resulting system to be good, fast, and cheap — partly a DocAble requirement, partly a test of whether agents could help build a production system good enough to trust, fast enough to matter, and cheap enough to deploy at scale. -->

**The resulting system should be good, fast, and cheap.** This applied to both DocAble and its development: the service had to produce useful remediation at practical cost and latency, while the factory had to make building and changing that service economical without sacrificing engineering control.

<!-- point: failing-any-one-of-the-three-fails-the-test | A fast factory producing poor software would fail the test, and so would a high-quality system that stayed prohibitively expensive to develop or operate; agentic implementation was valuable only if the engineering system around it achieved all three. -->

A fast factory producing poor software would fail the test. So would a high-quality system whose development or operation remained prohibitively expensive. Agentic implementation was valuable only if the engineering system around it could achieve all three.

### The Joins: Deriving Evidence and Enforcement

<!-- point: a-repository-of-documentation-is-not-a-supervisory-environment | Connections among models determine required verification, access-grant plans, and recorded evidence, reducing repeated engineering decisions. | terms: alignment-principle -->

In [ref:chapter:2.6-system-knowledge], we saw how connections among models help engineers answer questions about a system. In DocAble’s factory, these connections also determine what verification is required, what permissions must be granted, and what evidence must be recorded. Consider three examples.

<!-- point: the-invariant-chain-derives-checker-obligations | Modeled property facts determine the required verification artifact; a reusable gate checks its existence without establishing that verification has been performed. | terms: alignment-principle -->

<!-- point: the-iam-join-derives-grants | Service relationships, deployment wiring, and runtime identities determine an access-grant plan; the resulting grants still require evaluation. | terms: alignment-principle -->

- **Property facts → verification tier → required artifact → admission** ([ref:3.2-governance-mechanisms:heading:deriving-verification-requirements]). The factory uses modeled properties of each behavioral obligation to determine what kind of verification artifact it requires: a property test, an explicit-state checker, or a temporal specification. A reusable gate checks that the required artifact exists, although it does not establish that the verification has been performed. I need not choose a verification technique anew for every obligation expressed in this modeling vocabulary.
- **Service relationships × deployment wiring × runtime identity → access-grant plan** ([ref:2.6-system-knowledge:heading:docable-connecting-engineering-models] and [ref:chapter:appendix-a-model-driven-deployment]). The factory combines these three representations to determine which service accounts require permission to invoke which services. I need not reconstruct those permissions by hand for each deployment.
- **Mutation → provenance stamp → changelog** ([ref:2.6-system-knowledge:heading:docable-connecting-engineering-models] and [ref:chapter:appendix-a-controlled-mutation]). DocAble records document mutations using a common provenance mechanism, from which it derives customer-facing changelogs. A validator checks that selected mutation paths record the required information or have a justified exemption. When I introduce another remediation operation, I can use the existing machinery to account for its changes rather than construct a separate reporting mechanism.

### Substituting the Reasoning Engine {#exploring-alternative-reasoning-engines}

<!-- point: reasoning-substitution-reuses-engineered-operations | Task-level foundation-model selection and experimental agent orchestration change different parts of DocAble's reasoning while reusing supported document operations; the common PDF changeset interface does not cover every remediation pass. -->

The factory’s accumulated models, tools, and controls helped preserve a separation within DocAble’s architecture: the reasoning that proposes document repairs is distinct from the machinery that applies and evaluates them. This separation allows different reasoning strategies to reuse established document representations, controlled operations, and validation rather than rebuilding those capabilities. It also makes the cost and quality of alternative strategies an engineering question that can be evaluated without redesigning the service.

Substitution occurs at two levels. For individual tasks, ModelSelector assigns a foundation model using task-specific configuration and capability requirements. The remediation graph stays the same while the model supplying a judgment changes. A per-task benchmark compares candidate models on quality, cost, and latency. At a coarser level, a general-purpose agent can choose and sequence repairs instead of following the production graph’s orchestration.

The PDF changeset interface gives these strategies a common way to request supported mutations. A proposed operation names its target and intended change; the applier resolves it against the current document, checks its preconditions, and records the outcome. The structured pipeline and an experimental agent can therefore reuse typed document operations, independent validation, and provenance while deciding differently what to repair. Human-directed editing uses the same operation vocabulary. [ref:docable-reasoning-substitution] shows these architectural alternatives; the experiments below concern automated strategies.

The shared engineering machinery made alternative reasoning strategies practical to investigate. In one experiment, frontier agents inferred PDF structure through repeated tool interactions, while a foundation model given a prepared document scaffold inferred structure in a single pass on a separate prose fixture. Other experiments tested whether additional agentic reasoning justified its cost. On a handwritten PDF in September 2026, the structured pipeline incurred $0.0419 in metered inference expenditure, compared with an estimated $2.29 for an agent using DocAble’s document tools.[^reasoning-substitution-cost-accounting] Repeated processing of accumulated context contributed substantially to the agent’s cost. Both outputs passed the evaluated veraPDF checks. The agent also had access to the reference document structure. A November two-document experiment allowed an agent to inspect remaining findings and request another repair pass. The engineering question is where additional reasoning earns its cost. Difficult semantic cases may justify a frontier agent, and agents can help investigate failures or identify repairs that the specialized pipeline misses. The factory made such comparisons possible: a new reasoning strategy could reuse the document representations, supported operations, and validation machinery. This is engineering capital: accumulated decisions embodied in reusable machinery that remains useful as the reasoning strategies change.

## The Factory in Operation

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, governed-engineering-environment -->

<!-- point: the-mature-production-flow | The November 3 workflow turns priorities into reviewed work orders, dependency-ordered phases, isolated implementation and independently audited completion. -->

How does one engineer keep several agents working without continually prompting each one? I record priorities and deployment prerequisites in a strategy document. An orchestrating agent selects ready work and coordinates its planning, implementation, and evaluation.

A planning agent develops an *epic*: a persistent work order recording the change’s purpose, scope, phases, dependencies, and completion criteria. Its design document identifies consequential properties and the tests or analyses required for them. A separate agent reviews the plan; unresolved engineering decisions return to me. The work order preserves decisions across dispatches while leaving implementation choices within the stated requirements to the agents.

<!-- point: task-scoped-context-connects-models-to-dispatch | An agent's refined target files let Query Repo retrieve relevant models, invariants, tests, and analyses before implementation; a separate check requires that discovery occurred. -->

The phase dependencies determine which agents can work concurrently, as [ref:docable-work-order-flow] shows. The orchestrator dispatches ready phases into isolated Git worktrees. Each agent first inspects its assigned component and records a refined plan, including target files, in its first commit. Before changing the implementation, it supplies those files to *Query Repo*, which retrieves the associated models, invariants, tests, and static checks. A pre-commit check requires a record that this discovery occurred. Known obligations can therefore inform the agent’s implementation choices from the outset.

<!-- label: docable-work-order-flow -->
<!-- figure: assets/docable-work-order-flow.svg | *From engineering intent to completed work.* The orchestrator schedules independent phases from a reviewed work order, then a separate agent audits their combined result against the completion criteria and returns missing or defective work for repair. Closing the work order and authorizing deployment are separate decisions. [short: From engineering intent to completed work] -->

Completed changes pass inexpensive checks before integration. Broader validation runs periodically against the combined code, and the deployment process reruns the full validation suite. Two changes may each work in isolation and still fail when combined, so separate worktrees do not remove the need to check the integrated result. {{sec:engineering-the-environment}} explains the bottleneck that motivated this arrangement and the cost of delaying some checks.

<!-- point: repair-is-cheap-escalation-is-the-signal | Checks and the completion audit return deficient work for repair; unresolved decisions and defects in the environment reach the engineer. -->

Finally, an independent agent audits the epic against its completion criteria. It checks that the expected changes landed and that the required evidence supports completion. Missing work or failed checks lead to repair and another review. Problems the agents cannot resolve, including defects in the factory itself, return to me. The work order closes after the audit accepts the result.

## Improving the Factory

<!-- point: governance-conversion-is-an-empirical-claim-about-durable-structure | Three selected episodes show a controlled product interface, evidence-grounding prompt checks, and integration policy becoming durable machinery. | terms: governance-conversion -->

The factory’s operating process also exposed failures that required changes to the factory itself. Some engineering models came from known requirements; others arose when failures revealed missing distinctions or controls. Three episodes illustrate the latter: preserving document structure during mutation, governing instructions to foundation models, and safely integrating concurrent development work.

Preserving document structure required a controlled mutation boundary. An early PDF-slicing operation used PyMuPDF to copy pages into smaller documents. The resulting pages rendered correctly, but the operation discarded the PDF’s accessibility tag tree, including `/StructTreeRoot` and the page-to-structure relationships. A library call that preserved visible content had silently destroyed information essential to accessibility.

The immediate repair replaced the slicing operation with one that preserved the tags. I then established a canonical-library policy and concentrated PDF mutations in typed operations under `AdaTool.PdfModel`. For example, structure insertion passes through `PdfStructInserter` rather than allowing arbitrary calls to the canonical PDF library’s tag-manipulation APIs. A blocking direct-access lint rejects those calls outside designated modules. The architectural model identifies where PDF structure may be changed; the lint checks that subsequent implementations preserve the boundary.

Semantic instructions required their own engineering model. Agents developing DocAble’s image-description prompts introduced numerical requirements, including a demand for at least three specific facts and a minimum description length. Some source images did not contain enough legible information to satisfy those requirements. The investigation identified padded descriptions and a plot description that asserted unsupported values. The problem was not the particular threshold: an unconditional demand for information absent from the source could produce the same failure.

I responded with a typed prompt-governance model. It classifies prompts by purpose, such as describing, transcribing, or extracting information, and classifies their instructions by the demands they impose. A decision table determines which combinations are permitted. For example, a minimum fact count is prohibited in a description prompt, while an extraction requirement may be permitted when conditioned on information actually present in the source. The model distinguishes requirements appropriate to a task from requirements that could make faithful completion impossible.

A deterministic lint checks registered prompt definitions against this model and blocks illegal combinations. An agent introducing another unconditional fact minimum into a description prompt encounters a failed check rather than relying on a reviewer to recognize the mistake.

Integration automation required a model of safe conflict resolution. DocAble’s agents worked concurrently in separate Git worktrees, often changing overlapping files. The integration machinery ordinarily rebased completed work onto the current repository. When a rebase encountered a conflict, an automatic retry used Git’s `-X theirs` option, favoring one side of each conflicting hunk. In June 2026, concurrent modifications to the integration machinery itself produced an incompatible combination of edits. The resulting Python code failed to load, disabling scheduled integration.

The failure exposed a decision that the automation had been making without an adequate policy: which conflicts may be resolved automatically? I distinguished three cases. A clean rebase can proceed. Certain narrowly defined additive conflicts can use an established resolution procedure. Other conflicts require escalation because the integration machinery cannot establish that combining the changes preserves their intended effects. This decision model separates the presence of a conflict from permission to resolve it automatically. A conflict is evidence that further work may be needed, not authorization to discard one side.

I removed the `-X theirs` retry and changed the integration procedure to follow this policy. Clean integrations and permitted additive resolutions could proceed automatically; unresolved conflicts produced a `conflict-need-help` disposition. That disposition made the remaining work suitable for delegation. Rather than having Git select a resolution without understanding the changes, the disposition provided a defined task that I could assign to an agent: examine the conflicting contributions, reconcile their intended effects, and produce a candidate for validation. The agent could perform the repair, but the integration procedure would not accept an arbitrary conflict resolution merely because it had been generated. The model thus governed both what the automation could decide on its own and how unresolved work could be handed back to an agent.

Across these episodes, I repeatedly observed that coding agents could diagnose defects and implement repairs but seldom proposed the larger changes to models, architecture, or controls that would prevent similar failures. Claude suggested replacing the offending library call, not establishing a controlled mutation boundary. Claude suggested revising the prompt, not modeling which instructions were appropriate to different semantic tasks. Claude suggested repairing the integration script, not reconsidering which conflicts the factory should resolve automatically. I generally had to identify and direct these larger changes, although agents could implement the resulting machinery. Once the decisions were represented explicitly, agents could also undertake work that the new structure made delegable, such as reconciling conflicts identified by the integration policy. Bui and Evangelopoulos distinguish this capacity to execute assigned work from the proactivity needed to recognize worthwhile work independently [cite: bui2026agentic]. My experience illustrates that distinction.

## The Supervisor's Job

As DocAble’s engineering environment developed, my role changed with it. [ref:mage-staircase] summarizes that progression in three stages: co-coder, tech lead, and architect. The staircase represents two related developments: the models and controls available to govern agent work accumulated, allowing me to supervise implementation at increasing levels of abstraction.

Initially, I read, judged, and merged agents’ changes. As implementation outpaced my attention, I delegated evaluation to other agents, then replaced recurring decidable findings with deterministic checks. Specialized reviewers, named ownership zones, and boundary rules allowed me to direct work without inspecting every change. Eventually, architectural, behavioral, and ownership models provided system-level views for planning and evaluation. I could make decisions about the system without routinely reconstructing its implementation.

Some decisions about the factory itself also became easier to realize. Once I had established reusable machinery for evaluating architectural dependencies, for example, I could introduce another dependency restriction by extending the model. I could change what the factory governed without designing another mechanism to govern it.
