# Engineers in the Software Factory

<!-- point: the-metaphor-also-describes-the-engineers-role | The factory metaphor also gives us a way to describe the engineer's role. -->
The factory metaphor also gives us a way to describe the engineer's role.

<!-- point: operator-and-manufacturing-engineer-relate-to-machinery-differently | A physical factory admits two relationships to productive machinery: an operator runs it and keeps production moving; a manufacturing engineer works on the production system around it. -->
In a physical factory, an operator runs productive machinery: supplying work, configuring it,
monitoring production, inspecting output, and responding to exceptions. A manufacturing engineer
works on the production system around that machinery: designing the process, deciding which
tolerances matter, determining where measurement belongs, establishing conditions for acceptance,
and changing the process when experience exposes a weakness.

<!-- point: both-kinds-of-work-appear-in-the-reconstructed-factories | Both kinds of work appear in the factories reconstructed here: engineers supply tasks and context, construct skills, route work, inspect output, and resolve exceptions — and they also engineer parts of the surrounding production system. -->
Both kinds of work appear in the software factories reconstructed here. Engineers supply tasks and
context to agents, construct skills, route work, inspect outputs, resolve exceptions, and improve
the machinery through which agents act. They also engineer parts of the surrounding production
system. Cloudflare encodes policy and attaches automated review to admission. Spotify builds
machinery for fleet-wide change. Uber externalizes context and moves deterministic orchestration out
of model turns. Siemens places engineering models and deterministic evaluation upstream of selected
acts of realization. The other cases make different choices about knowledge, authority, and
evidence.

<!-- point: the-industrial-cases-invested-in-process-design | Read through the two ideas {{sec:the-software-factory}} introduced, the industrial cases show substantial investment in process design: routing, context, tools, permissions, evaluation, admission, and feedback into later production. -->
Read through the two ideas introduced in {{sec:the-software-factory}}, the industrial cases show
substantial investment in process design. They route work, assemble context, provision tools, assign
permissions, sequence evaluation, control admission, and feed experience back into subsequent
production. Several operate production machinery far more sophisticated than anything in the
originating case.

<!-- point: tolerance-is-less-developed-because-natural-language-cannot-bound | Tolerance is less developed: instructions, skills, policy corpora, and knowledge graphs can make an agent better informed, but natural language does not itself provide a machine-checkable boundary around acceptable realizations. -->
Tolerance is less developed. Natural-language instructions, skills, policy corpora, and knowledge
graphs can tell a capable agent what engineers want it to preserve. They can make the agent better
informed and improve the probability that it interprets the intent correctly. But natural language
does not itself provide a machine-checkable boundary around acceptable realizations. The fabricator
still has to interpret the instruction, and another act of interpretation may be required to decide
whether the result satisfied it.

**Information available to an agent is not the same thing as control available to an engineer.**

<!-- point: docables-process-design-is-comparatively-primitive | DocAble's process design is comparatively primitive: one engineer interacting directly with coding agents, with little need for organizational coordination machinery because there is only one human engineer in the factory. -->
DocAble's process design is comparatively primitive. I interact directly with coding agents,
principally through Claude Code, with project instructions, work orders, hooks, tests, and other
machinery around those interactions. I do not need much more process: process helps people
coordinate, and I am the only engineer in the building.

<!-- point: models-are-tolerances-and-alignment-is-their-measurement | DocAble's approach to tolerance is distinctive: MAGE uses machine-checkable models to represent selected consequential properties and Alignment to measure and enforce those tolerances against the realized system. | terms: tolerance, modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->
DocAble's approach to tolerance, however, is distinctive. **MAGE uses machine-checkable models to
represent selected consequential properties and Alignment to measure and enforce those tolerances
against the realized system.** The models tell agents what must remain true while leaving other
realization choices open; Alignment supplies feedback when a candidate realization violates an
obligation and evidence to the engineer about whether it holds. As in physical production, the point
of a tolerance is not to specify the artifact completely. It bounds the variation that matters while
leaving the fabricator freedom inside those bounds.

<!-- point: no-maturity-ladder-different-parts-of-the-production-problem | The comparison places no factory on a maturity ladder: the industrial systems provide stronger evidence about process design at organizational scale, the originating case about machine-checkable tolerances in the supervisory interface, and how they combine is open. -->
The comparison does not place these factories on a maturity ladder. They have invested in different
parts of the production problem. The industrial systems provide much stronger evidence about process
design at organizational scale. DocAble provides unusually direct evidence about what happens when
machine-checkable tolerances become part of the supervisory interface. The open question is how
those approaches combine.

<!-- point: a-vendor-cannot-arrive-knowing-what-is-consequential | The question is sharpest for the factory in a box: a vendor can supply agents, orchestration, execution, context, identity, permissions, and review machinery, but cannot arrive knowing which properties of a customer's software are consequential. -->
The question is particularly sharp for the factory in a box. A vendor can supply agents,
orchestration, execution environments, context retrieval, identity, permissions, review machinery,
and generic controls. It cannot arrive knowing which properties of a customer's software are
consequential or which evidence that customer requires before a change is acceptable.

<!-- point: a-provider-can-productize-the-customers-ability-to-establish-tolerances | GitLab productizes process design well; the missing factory function is tolerance, so a provider can supply the means by which a customer expresses, measures, and enforces its own bounds rather than the bounds themselves. -->
The corpus shows one half of that boundary being productized already. GitLab is very good at
productizing process design: it packages the routing, execution environments, context machinery,
permissions, and review infrastructure an organization would otherwise build for itself. The
integration problem that remains therefore includes the customer's engineering knowledge. Connecting
an agent to that knowledge can improve realization. Turning selected knowledge into models,
evidence, and controls can also make it part of the customer's supervisory machinery. MAGE suggests
machinery for productizing the customer's ability to establish tolerances — not the tolerances
themselves, which the vendor cannot know, but the means by which the customer expresses, measures,
and enforces them.

<!-- point: the-externalization-question-and-the-handoff-to-theory | The open question is how much organization-specific engineering knowledge must be externalized before an off-the-shelf factory can reliably produce controlled changes, and Chapter 6 supplies the way to reason about it. -->
How much organization-specific engineering knowledge must be externalized before an off-the-shelf
factory can reliably produce controlled changes to that organization's software?

{{chapter:theory}} gives us a way to reason about that question. It examines the costs of carrying
explicit representations, the alternative of reconstructing intent through inference, the effect of
Alignment on acceptable realization, and the engineering capital inherited by subsequent changes.
