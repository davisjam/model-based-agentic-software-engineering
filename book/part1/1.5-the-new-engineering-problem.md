# Two Engineering Problems, One Method {#from-the-engineering-problems-to-mage}

<!-- section-terms: engineering-substrate, engineered-environment, representation-problem, enforcement-problem, modeling-principle, alignment-principle, degrees-of-freedom -->

Commodity intelligence changes the cost of implementation, but it does not remove the engineer’s responsibility for deciding what the system must do or establishing that it does so. The properties discussed in {{sec:reasoning-horizon}} suggest two complementary engineering problems. Bounded working state makes it costly to recover and maintain system knowledge across tasks, while probabilistic judgment makes an agent’s own assessment insufficient evidence that important obligations have been met.

> #### Inset — Two Engineering Problems {#two-engineering-problems}
>
> **The Representation Problem.** How should a large, evolving system be represented so that the properties relevant to an engineering question can be reasoned about without continually reconstructing them from implementation?
>
> **The Enforcement Problem.** How should engineering intent be enforced over autonomous work, so that satisfying important obligations does not depend on the agent remembering, agreeing, or self-certifying each time?

<!-- point: modeling-and-alignment-complement-one-another | Modeling makes consequential properties explicit and can reduce reconstruction or support assurance; Alignment connects selected obligations to constraints, evidence-producing sensors, and admission-controlling gates. Modeling can improve the information supplied to an agent, while Alignment can improve feedback and prevent acceptance of violations within the enforcing mechanism’s scope. | terms: representation-problem, enforcement-problem, modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->

MAGE addresses these problems through two complementary activities: Modeling makes consequential knowledge explicit, while Alignment makes selected obligations enforceable. Modeling preserves relationships that people and agents would otherwise have to reconstruct during each task. Providing a model, however, does not ensure that its obligations will be respected. An agent may overlook a constraint, interpret it incorrectly, or violate it while satisfying another requirement. Alignment connects selected obligations to mechanisms outside that producing judgment: constraints restrict actions or states, sensors produce evidence, and gates evaluate evidence and control whether work may proceed. [ref:substrate-to-method] summarizes the primary relationships. The activities are complementary rather than inseparable: a model can make system knowledge available without enforcing it, while a gate can enforce a permission without requiring an elaborate system model.

<!-- figure: assets/substrate-to-method.svg | *From agentic machinery to MAGE.* Finite working state motivates Modeling; probabilistic autonomous work motivates Alignment. The two principles address different engineering problems and operate together. -->

The two activities extend the techniques for steering agents described in [{{sec:reasoning-horizon}}](1.4-the-agentic-substrate.html). Context engineering supplies information relevant to an agent’s work; Modeling supplies information about consequential system properties through explicit engineering representations. Feedback loops help agents revise their work in response to observations; Alignment supplies feedback about whether selected engineering obligations have been satisfied, together with mechanisms that prevent violations from being accepted.

Both activities can increase the probability of successful engineering work. Better information helps agents make appropriate decisions, while better feedback helps them identify and correct mistakes. Alignment goes further: where an obligation can be enforced by a sufficiently complete and correct mechanism, violating it can be made impossible within the mechanism’s scope. The resulting assurance need not depend on the probability that an agent will make the right decision.

These activities are valuable even when agents need little help understanding or implementing a system. Models can make engineering questions easier to answer, while enforcement can reduce the cost of establishing that successive implementations remain acceptable. As agents become more capable, we may need less scaffolding to direct their work and find it economical to construct more sophisticated machinery for engineering and assurance.

The engineer must still decide which properties deserve explicit representation, which obligations require independent enforcement, and which implementation choices can remain open. These decisions depend on the system’s purpose and the consequences of getting them wrong. A model that records every implementation detail may be expensive to maintain and unnecessarily restrict the agent. A check over an inadequate representation may reliably establish the wrong property. Conversely, leaving a consequential requirement implicit forces each new agent or engineering episode to reconstruct it. MAGE asks engineers to make these choices deliberately: preserve the distinctions and obligations that justify durable structure, provide evidence suited to their consequences, and leave genuinely acceptable alternatives open to the implementer.
