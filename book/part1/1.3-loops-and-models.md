# Engineering the Loop

<!-- section-terms: loop-engineering, engineered-agent-loop -->

<!-- point: agent-work-is-iterative-not-one-act | An agent produces software iteratively, not in one act. | terms: loop-engineering -->
<!-- index-def: loop-engineering -->
An agent does not produce software in one indivisible act. It works iteratively:
observe the current state, reason, act, inspect what happened, and continue. That is
what opens the work to engineering — an iterative process gives the engineer places to
intervene. Shaping that loop deliberately, rather than hoping a single prompt lands, is
what the rest of this method turns on.

<!-- point: mage-controls-the-loop-not-the-reasoning | MAGE controls the loop around the model, not the model's reasoning. | terms: loop-engineering -->
MAGE does not depend on controlling the model's internal reasoning. It controls the
engineering loop around it. And a loop the environment owns has control points you set.
The environment determines four of them.

## The four levers

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | The four levers of an engineered loop — the word count includes the inlined loop figure's accessible description and the four lever labels; the prose itself is four short items, one definition inset, and one figure. -->

<!-- section-terms: engineered-agent-loop, loop-engineering, success-metric, model-as-map -->

<!-- point: four-levers-state-actions-criterion-admission | Four levers: state exposed, action surface, success criterion, admission rule. | terms: engineered-agent-loop, success-metric, model-as-map -->
- **The state exposed.** The representation of the problem and the system the agent
  receives. The agent never sees the whole world. It sees the portion the environment
  makes available, in the form the environment supplies.
- **The action surface.** The tools, APIs, files, and sanctioned operations the agent
  may use. An action the environment withholds needs no prohibition in prose — it is
  simply unavailable.
- **The success criterion.** The evidence that separates real progress from
  plausible-looking output. <!-- index-def: success-metric -->A loop without a
  measurable or otherwise evaluable stopping condition has no reliable notion of "done."
- **The admission rule.** What must hold before a result becomes part of the system. The
  agent proposes an output; the environment decides whether that output advances.

<!-- point: engineered-agent-loop-definition | The engineered agent loop names the four-lever feedback process. | terms: engineered-agent-loop, success-metric, model-as-map -->
<!-- index-def: engineered-agent-loop -->
> **Engineered agent loop.** A feedback process in which the environment supplies a
> modeled state, constrains the available actions, measures progress against an explicit
> criterion, and decides whether a result may advance. [ref:the-loop] draws the four
> levers at their stations.

## Where the two theses touch the work

<!-- section-terms: engineered-agent-loop, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: four-levers-are-not-another-decomposition | The four levers are not another decomposition of MAGE; they are where its two activities touch running work. | terms: engineered-agent-loop -->
These four levers are not another decomposition of MAGE. They are where its two central
activities touch running work.

<!-- point: first-lever-previews-modeling | The first lever previews Modeling: what the agent sees bounds what it can reason about. | terms: thesis-modeling, model-as-map -->
The first lever previews **Modeling**. Engineering knowledge and intent have to be
represented in a form the agent can reason through, because what the agent sees
determines what it can reason about. Part 2 builds that representation.

<!-- point: other-three-levers-preview-alignment | The other three levers preview Alignment: intent attached to mechanisms the agent cannot ignore. | terms: thesis-alignment -->
The other three preview **Alignment**. The environment constrains the available actions,
weighs the evidence, and controls admission. Intent becomes more than advice once it is
attached to mechanisms the agent cannot simply ignore. Part 3 builds those.

<!-- point: engineering-the-loop-changes-conditions-not-requests | Prompting requests a behavior; engineering the loop changes the conditions behavior occurs under. | terms: loop-engineering -->
The distinction carries the method. Prompting asks the agent to behave a certain way.
Engineering the loop changes the conditions under which behavior occurs.

<!-- point: why-these-moves-answered-by-the-machine-not-docable | Why these two moves ground a method should follow from the machine, not from DocAble. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
That still leaves a deeper question. Why should these particular moves — explicit models
on one side, authoritative mechanisms on the other — be the basis of an engineering
method at all? The answer should not be "because they worked for DocAble," nor even
"because they make a neat loop." It should follow from the properties of the machine
itself.

That is the next chapter.
