# The New Engineering Problem

<!-- point: engineering-methods-are-read-off-their-materials | An engineering method is read off its material. | terms: engineering-substrate -->
Reinforced concrete carries a building because two materials divide the work:
concrete takes the compression, steel takes the tension, and the method — where the
bars run, how the loads route — follows from those properties. Every engineering
discipline is read off its material this way. So before deciding what agentic
software engineering asks of us, look hard at the material it runs on. It is not a
faster programmer. It is a machine with properties a programmer does not have, and
the method has to answer to those.

## The substrate is a pairing

<!-- section-terms: engineering-substrate, foundation-model, agentic-harness -->

<!-- point: model-plus-harness-form-a-new-engineering-substrate | A foundation model and a harness together form the engineering substrate. | terms: engineering-substrate, foundation-model, agentic-harness -->
<!-- index-def: engineering-substrate -->
The material is two parts, not one. A **foundation model** reasons broadly but
probabilistically. An **agentic harness** — the runtime around it — gives that
reasoner tools, a lifecycle, and points where its work can be watched or stopped.
Neither part alone is the substrate; the **engineering substrate** is the pair, and
each half contributes properties the method must live with.

<!-- point: four-foundation-model-properties | The foundation model brings four properties. | terms: foundation-model -->
The foundation model brings four:

- **Broad semantic reasoning.** It interprets, synthesizes, and searches across
  open-ended domains. Hand it an abstraction — a schema, a state table, a policy —
  and it works over the abstraction, not just a fixed script.
- **Probabilistic execution.** The same input does not yield the same behavior
  twice, and the model's confidence is not evidence. It cannot certify its own
  output.
- **Bounded, reconstructed working state.** The active reasoning state is finite,
  and no durable picture of your system persists between tasks. Every task starts
  cold; the model rebuilds its picture, every time. [The Reasoning
  Horizon](1.3-the-reasoning-horizon.html) is where this property bit hardest.
- **Cheap repeated cognition.** Search, generation, repair, reconciliation —
  cognitive work that once cost an engineer's week now runs at a scale that was
  formerly out of reach.

<!-- point: four-harness-properties | The harness adds four properties of its own. | terms: agentic-harness, injection-point -->
The harness adds four of its own. A raw model only recommends; the harness turns
recommendation into action, and every part of that turn is a property the engineer
can use:

- **Tool-mediated action.** The agent touches the world only through identifiable
  interfaces — a file edit, a command, an API call. There are no bare hands.
- **Interposition.** Because every action crosses an interface, a hook or wrapper
  can inspect it, constrain it, redirect it, or deny it at the moment it happens.
- **Lifecycle visibility.** Dispatch, tool calls, compaction, completion, merge,
  deploy — the work's transitions are machine-observable events, not private states
  in a worker's head.
- **Parallel execution.** Many independent reasoning loops run at once, on one
  codebase, around the clock.

<!-- point: the-substrate-framing-is-an-emerging-consensus | The model-plus-harness reading is now a shared one. | terms: engineering-substrate, agentic-harness -->
This reading is not idiosyncratic. A 2026 literature on *harness engineering* has
converged on the same decomposition: autonomous capability is a property of a
model–harness–environment system, not of the model alone [cite: zhong-zhu2026]; the
harness is a runtime an engineer designs and tunes, and its craft is as much removal
as construction, since every component encodes an assumption about what the model
cannot yet do — an assumption that expires as models improve [cite: young-anthropic2025];
what lasts is the durable substrate under the deletable scaffolding
[cite: tan-thinharness2026]. That is corroboration, not the argument. The argument is
what these properties do to the work.

## Not a faster programmer

<!-- section-terms: engineering-substrate, whose-fault -->

<!-- point: why-not-a-faster-programmer | The substrate is not a faster programmer; its properties are different in kind. | terms: engineering-substrate, foundation-model -->
Now the question the substrate answers. Why is this not simply software engineering
with a faster programmer? **Because the material has different properties.** A faster
programmer would still carry a model of your system in their head between tasks, and
would still stand behind their own work as correct. This reasoner does neither: it
reconstructs its picture from cold each time, and its confidence in a result is not an
account of that result. Speed is the least of the changes. The two properties that
bite — a finite, reconstructed working state and a probabilistic output that cannot
vouch for itself — are the ones no amount of raw capacity removes.

<!-- point: representation-and-authority-become-first-class | Those two properties make representation and authority first-class engineering problems. | terms: engineering-substrate -->
So the reinforced-concrete reading lands somewhere specific. Read the substrate's
properties through to the method they force, and two concerns that used to sit at the
margin move to the center. If the reasoner rebuilds its picture every task, then **how
the system is represented** to it stops being a convenience and becomes a first-class
engineering problem. If the reasoner cannot vouch for its own work, then **where
engineering intent gets authority** over that work becomes the other. Representation
and authority: the machine makes both of them structural.

## The loop is observable

<!-- section-terms: agentic-harness, injection-point -->

<!-- point: agentic-work-occurs-in-an-observable-loop | Agentic work occurs in an observable loop, so control need not reach the hidden reasoning. | terms: agentic-harness, injection-point -->
One property of the harness carries more than the others, and it sets up the second
problem. Agentic work occurs in an **observable loop** rather than in one indivisible
act. The model may reason internally, but its state inputs, actions, outputs, and
lifecycle transitions meet an external engineering environment. That matters because
the engineer need not control hidden reasoning in order to control the conditions
under which its work enters the system. The loop is where authority can act — not
inside the model's private thought, but at the seams its work has to cross.

## Two questions, not two answers

<!-- section-terms: engineering-substrate, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: part-one-poses-the-representation-problem | Problem A, representation, follows from broad reasoning over a finite reconstructed state. | terms: engineering-substrate, reasoning-horizon -->
Cross the substrate's properties and two engineering problems fall out. State them as
questions and stop there; the rest of the book is the work of answering them.

**The representation problem.** A broad reasoner can operate on abstractions, but its
active reasoning state is finite and reconstructed from cold. So:

> How should a large evolving system be represented so a finite reasoner can work
> coherently over it without continually rediscovering the implementation?

That is Problem A. That answer is Part II's to earn.

<!-- point: part-one-poses-the-authority-problem | Problem B, authority, follows from probabilistic output meeting an observable loop. | terms: engineering-substrate, thesis-alignment, injection-point -->
**The authority problem.** A probabilistic reasoner can produce plausible work but
cannot be the final authority on whether that work satisfies an engineering
obligation. The harness, meanwhile, exposes action boundaries and lifecycle
transitions where an environment could hold it to account. So:

> How should engineering intent be given authority over probabilistic work so
> correctness does not depend on the agent remembering, agreeing, or self-certifying?

That is Problem B. That answer is Part III's.

<!-- point: the-two-problems-signpost-the-composition | The two problems map onto the next three Parts. | terms: engineering-substrate -->
That is the right amount of navigation. **Part II develops the representation problem.
Part III develops the authority problem. Part IV shows how the two responses compose
into a working engineering method.** Part I's job is to make the reader believe both
problems are real; it does not owe their solutions.

<!-- point: commodity-intelligence-removes-implementation-not-the-decisions | Commodity intelligence removes implementation effort from the decisions, not the decisions themselves. | terms: engineering-substrate, judgment-is-the-scarce-resource -->
Commodity intelligence removes implementation effort from many of the decisions that once
consumed it. It does not remove the decisions themselves. Someone still has to determine what
the system should do, which properties matter, what tradeoffs are acceptable, and what evidence
is sufficient to trust a change. The new substrate changes how those judgments are exercised:
less through direct authorship of every implementation, and more through the representations and
engineering environment that shape autonomous work.

<!-- point: representation-and-authority-related-not-the-same | The two problems are related but not the same; the next Parts develop them separately. | terms: engineering-substrate, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
These two problems are related but not the same. Representation determines what the reasoner can
know and work through; authority determines what the resulting work must satisfy before it is
accepted. The next two Parts develop those responses separately — Part II asks what a finite
reasoner should reason through, Part III asks how engineering obligations acquire authority over
probabilistic work — and Part IV shows what happens when the two are composed into a method.
