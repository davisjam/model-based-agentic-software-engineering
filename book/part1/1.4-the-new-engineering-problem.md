# The New Engineering Problem

<!-- point: substrate-changes-in-two-ways | Commodity intelligence changes the economics of implementation without abolishing scale, uncertainty, and assurance; the substrate makes two old problems especially consequential — representation for a finite reasoner, and authority outside the producing run. | terms: engineering-substrate, foundation-model, agentic-harness -->
Commodity intelligence changes the economics of implementation without abolishing the old
problems of scale, uncertainty, and assurance. The agentic substrate makes two of those
problems especially consequential. A finite reasoner needs representations that keep the
relevant state tractable as systems and tasks grow. Fast probabilistic implementation needs
authority outside the producing run so that important obligations do not depend on memory,
confidence, or repeated human inspection.

State them as questions.

## The representation problem

<!-- point: part-one-poses-the-representation-problem | Problem A, representation: how should a large evolving system be represented so a finite reasoner works coherently over the properties that matter without reconstructing them from implementation? | terms: engineering-substrate, reasoning-horizon, thesis-modeling -->
A broad reasoner can operate on abstractions, but its active reasoning state is finite and
large systems contain far more detail than any one engineering question needs. **How should a
large, evolving system be represented so that a finite reasoner can work coherently over the
properties that matter without continually reconstructing them from implementation?**

That is Problem A. Part II develops the Modeling Thesis.

## The authority problem

<!-- point: part-one-poses-the-authority-problem | Problem B, authority: autonomous work can be locally constrained and checked without a comprehensive model, but probabilistic implementation cannot be its own final authority, and richer obligations need semantics the environment must obtain somewhere. | terms: engineering-substrate, thesis-alignment, injection-point -->
Autonomous work can be locally constrained and checked without a comprehensive model, but
probabilistic implementation cannot be its own final authority, and richer system-level
obligations require semantics the environment must obtain somewhere. **How should engineering
obligations be given authority over autonomous work so that correctness does not depend on the
producing agent remembering, agreeing, or self-certifying—and so that deterministically
checkable questions do not require a human to reconstruct the answer each time?**

That is Problem B. Part III develops the Alignment Thesis.

## Related, but not the same

<!-- point: representation-and-authority-related-not-the-same | The two problems are related but neither reducible to the other; MAGE teaches Modeling first as a methodological choice, not because Alignment is impossible without it — local controls can already be authoritative, but better abstractions enlarge what can be stated, reasoned over, and checked without recurring human reconstruction. Commodity intelligence removes implementation, not the decisions. | terms: engineering-substrate, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
The two problems are related but neither is reducible to the other. Representation determines
the scale and semantics of what humans, agents, and tools can reason over; authority determines
what the resulting work must satisfy before it is accepted. MAGE teaches Modeling first as a
methodological choice. Local controls can already be authoritative, and better abstractions
enlarge the set of consequential properties that can be stated, reasoned over, and checked
without recurring human reconstruction. Part II
builds those abstractions, Part III develops the mechanisms of authority, and Part IV composes
the two into a working method. Commodity intelligence removes implementation effort from many
decisions that once consumed it; it does not remove the decisions themselves.
