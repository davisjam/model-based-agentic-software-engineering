# MAGE by Example

<!-- point: teaser-grounds-the-abstractions | You will meet DocAble in every chapter; here is just enough to hold it. | terms: running-example -->
DocAble provides the book's deepest worked example: a production accessibility system built largely through
coding agents. Before examining its models and controls, a few facts about the system are enough.

## Two birds, one stone

<!-- section-terms: document-accessibility, supervised-autonomy -->

<!-- point: explicit-problem-inaccessible-documents-at-scale | The explicit problem: inaccessible university documents at scale. | terms: document-accessibility, remediation-cost -->
The explicit problem is a mountain of inaccessible documents. In 2024 the Department of
Justice gave public universities a deadline: the slide decks, PDFs, and spreadsheets they
distribute must meet a real accessibility standard, so that a student using a screen reader
gets the same course as everyone else. A typical deck fails that standard dozens of ways —
images with no descriptions, missing titles, a reading order no machine can follow.
Remediating one course's materials by hand costs roughly {{workweeks_per_course}} full work
weeks, and a university offers thousands of courses. The mandate is just; the arithmetic is
crushing. [The ADA Context](5.1-the-problem-and-the-bar.html) walks the full bind.

<!-- point: two-birds-one-stone-motive | One project answered both problems at once. | terms: document-accessibility, running-example -->
The implicit problem was mine. In early 2026 coding agents got good enough that I, a professor of
software engineering, no longer knew what my discipline's daily craft was about to become — and the
only way to find out was to build something real with them and watch what the work demanded.
Accessibility remediation was a consequential problem whose technical core had suddenly come within
reach, so one project could answer both at once: build the remediation system, and let the building
teach the method. Two birds, one stone. The system is [DocAble]({{product_url}}); the method is MAGE, and Part V tells
the origin in full.

## What the user sees

<!-- section-terms: governance-target-product, document-accessibility -->

<!-- point: upload-in-remediated-file-plus-evidence-out | A user uploads a document; a checked file and its evidence return. | terms: governance-target-product -->
<!-- index-def: fidelity-validator -->
<!-- index-def: provenance-layer -->
From the outside the system is almost boring, by design. A professor drops a slide deck onto a web
page; a few minutes later a remediated version comes back — figures described, slides titled, reading
order set, contrast fixed — together with a record of every change and the clause of the standard each
remaining finding answers to. The evidence travels with the file, because a university needs not just
fixed documents but proof of what was fixed, and the ability to reverse any change that got the meaning
wrong. [ref:docable-glimpse] shows only that: what goes in, and what comes back.

## The grounding problem

<!-- section-terms: running-example -->

<!-- point: building-well-is-several-problems | Building DocAble well is not one problem; different questions want different pictures. | terms: running-example -->
Building DocAble well is not one problem. It is several, and they do not answer to the same picture.

<!-- point: five-questions-five-pictures | Structure, behavior, policy, measurement, and provenance are five questions, each wanting its own reduction. | terms: running-example -->
Ask what the system is made of, and you want a map of its parts and the lines that may run between
them. Ask what happens to a document after it is uploaded, and that map goes quiet; now you want the
sequence of states a job moves through, and which moves are legal. Ask which piece of the system may
call which other piece, and you want neither a parts-list nor a timeline but a policy — a set of
permitted relationships. Ask whether the system is fast enough and cheap enough, and you want measured
quantities and the ceilings they must stay under. Ask whether the record handed back to the university
is honest, and you want a fifth view: the provenance of every change, kept in step with what the code
actually did.

<!-- point: no-single-model-of-docable | No one picture is the model of DocAble; each is a purposeful reduction that makes one question's facts cheap to see — comprehensiveness is not the goal; choosing the reduction the question demands is the skill. | terms: running-example -->
Five questions, five pictures. The system did not change between them; the engineering question did.
No one of these pictures is *the* model of DocAble. Each is a purposeful reduction, built to answer a
particular question and expose a particular property. A more comprehensive representation could contain
more of the system, but comprehensiveness is not the goal: useful engineering abstractions make the
facts relevant to one question cheap to see while suppressing detail that does not matter. The skill is
choosing the reduction the question demands. Part II turns that choice into a discipline.

## A few landmarks

<!-- section-terms: running-example -->

<!-- point: landmarks-are-stable-nouns | A handful of plain names that stay put across every later chapter. | terms: running-example -->
To follow those questions you need a handful of names that stay put. Here they are, kept deliberately
plain — not architecture, just enough territory that later chapters can redraw the map without laying
the ground again each time.

<!-- index-def: remediation-job -->
<!-- index-def: worker -->
<!-- index-def: chunk -->
<!-- index-def: remediation-service -->
<!-- index-def: work-queue -->
<!-- index-def: evidence-record -->
<!-- index-def: accessibility-obligation -->
- A **job** is one document's trip through the system, from upload to delivered result.
- A **worker** is the process that does the remediation work on one piece of a job.
- A **chunk** is that piece: a job split into parts so several workers can run at once.
- A **service** is a specialized helper a worker calls for one narrow task: describing an image,
  converting a format, rendering a page.
- **Queues and infrastructure** are the plumbing that holds work between hand-offs: where a job waits,
  how a worker is handed its next chunk.
- The **remediated document and its evidence record** are what comes back — the fixed file, plus the
  changelog of every edit and the map from each remaining finding to the clause it answers.
- The **accessibility obligations** are the standard the document must meet: the specific, checkable
  requirements a screen-reader user depends on.

<!-- point: one-worker-five-views | A worker is one thing in the system and five things to an engineer, depending on the question. | terms: running-example, model-selection -->
**A worker can be viewed as
a deployable component, an actor in a job's lifecycle, an owner of a resource, a subject of an access
policy, or a source of measurements.** One worker, five true pictures. Each names the same worker;
none of them *is* the worker; each makes a different fact cheap to see. The same piece of reality supports several
models, and the question decides which you build. Hold the worker in mind. You will watch it change
shape five times.

## Why this system

<!-- section-terms: running-example -->

<!-- point: why-one-system-opened-deep | One example, opened as far as the question needs, teaches more than a shallow survey. | terms: running-example -->
Why hang a whole book on one system? Because a single example, opened as far as the question needs,
teaches more than a survey of many kept shallow. DocAble is real, it shipped, and its insides can be
shown at the resolution an engineer would actually work at. It is a single case, with the corresponding
limits on generalization, but one that can be inspected at engineering depth.

<!-- point: synchronic-here-diachronic-in-part-v | Parts II/III meet DocAble held still: Part II asks which representations make its properties tractable, Part III how selected obligations acquire authority; Part V tells how it came to be and what broke. | terms: running-example -->
The book uses it two ways and keeps them apart on purpose. Parts II and III meet DocAble as it stands:
a working system, held still while the engineering question varies. What is it made of? What states can
a job occupy? What may call what? Part II asks which representations make those properties tractable;
Part III asks how selected obligations acquire authority over the work. Part V tells the other story —
how the system came to be, what broke along the way, and which failures hardened into the machinery the
earlier Parts describe. **Same system, two tenses: the present tense here, the past tense later.**

<!-- point: enough-for-now-bridge-to-part-two | Enough of DocAble for now; the landmarks and the governed system are in place — before deciding what representations or controls it needs, inspect the productive substrate doing the work. | terms: running-example -->
The landmarks are in place, along with the system the book will
repeatedly redraw and govern. Before deciding what representations or controls it needs, we have to
inspect the productive substrate doing the work.
