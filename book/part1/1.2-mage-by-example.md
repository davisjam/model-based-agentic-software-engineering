# MAGE by Example: Summary of the DocAble Case

<!-- point: teaser-grounds-the-abstractions | You will meet DocAble in every chapter; here is just enough to hold it. | terms: running-example -->
[DocAble]({{product_url}}) provides the book's deepest worked example: a production accessibility system built largely through coding agents. Part V presents the full case study; before examining its models and Alignment mechanisms here, a few facts about the system are in order.

## Accessibility and the Engineering Experiment

<!-- section-terms: document-accessibility, supervised-autonomy -->

<!-- point: explicit-problem-inaccessible-documents-at-scale | The explicit problem: inaccessible university documents at scale. | terms: document-accessibility, remediation-cost -->
In 2024, the U.S. Department of Justice gave public universities a deadline to make their digital content accessible. A typical slide deck can fail the standard in dozens of ways: images without descriptions, missing titles, or a reading order that a screen reader cannot follow. I estimated that remediating one course's materials by hand would require roughly {{workweeks_per_course}} full work weeks; a university offers thousands of courses. I personally maintain two courses and had no desire to spend a full semester remediating them—at least not manually. I was, however, perfectly willing to spend a full semester attempting to automate the work.

<!-- point: two-birds-one-stone-motive | One project answered both problems: build the production system, and use the engineering work to discover what sustained agentic development demanded. | terms: document-accessibility, running-example -->
In early 2026, coding agents had also become capable enough to make a different experiment possible. I wanted to know what software engineering would look like if I stopped writing most of the implementation myself, so I used accessibility remediation as a real production problem on which to find out. The system became DocAble; the resulting method became MAGE. MAGE makes the judgment of one engineer capable of governing the implementation capacity of a software team. Part V returns to the build and examines how that capability emerged.

## What the User Sees

<!-- point: upload-in-remediated-file-plus-evidence-out | From the user's view DocAble is simple: an inaccessible document goes in; a remediated document and an evidence record come back, with consequential changes recorded so the result can be inspected and, where necessary, reversed. | terms: governance-target-product -->

<!-- index-def: provenance-layer -->
From the user's perspective, DocAble is simple: upload an inaccessible document; receive a remediated
document and an evidence record. It works across common document formats: Word, PowerPoint, and Excel;
their LibreOffice equivalents; PDF; and text-based formats including LaTeX and Typst. Figures are described,
reading order is repaired, structural accessibility is restored where possible, and consequential changes
are recorded so that the result can be inspected and, where necessary, reversed.

## One System, Several Engineering Questions

<!-- section-terms: running-example -->

<!-- point: building-well-is-several-problems | Building DocAble well is not one engineering problem; different questions require different representations. | terms: running-example -->
Building DocAble well is not one engineering problem. Different questions require different representations.

<!-- point: five-questions-five-representations | Structure, behavior, decisions, measurement, and provenance are different questions, each wanting its own representation of the same system. | terms: running-example -->
Ask what the system contains and how its parts relate, and you want a structural view. Ask what happens to
a document after upload, and you want behavior: the states a job moves through and the transitions that are
legal. Ask which component may call another, and you want a model of permitted
relationships. Ask whether the system is fast and cheap enough, and you want measurements and their
allowable envelopes. Ask whether the evidence returned with a document faithfully describes what happened,
and you want provenance.

<!-- point: no-single-model-of-docable | The system has not changed between these questions; the representation has. No single model is best for all of them — each is a purposeful reduction that preserves the facts one question needs and suppresses detail it does not. | terms: running-example, model-selection -->
The system has not changed between these questions. The representation has. There is no single model of
DocAble that is best for all of them. Each model is a purposeful reduction that preserves the facts needed
for an engineering question and suppresses detail that question does not need.

<!-- point: the-territory-vocabulary | A job is one document's trip; a chunk is a portion processed independently; workers claim and process chunks, calling specialized services; queues coordinate the handoffs; the outputs are the remediated document and its evidence. | terms: running-example -->
A job is one document's trip through the system. A chunk is a portion of that job that can be processed
independently. Workers claim and process chunks, calling specialized services for capabilities such as
image description, rendering, conversion, or OCR. Queues and other infrastructure coordinate those
handoffs. The final outputs are the remediated document and evidence describing consequential changes and
remaining findings.

<!-- point: one-worker-several-models | The same worker appears differently in later models — a deployable component, an actor in a lifecycle, an owner of a resource, a subject of an access policy, or a source of measurements; not competing descriptions, but different properties of the same system. | terms: running-example, model-selection -->
The same worker will appear differently in later models. It may be a deployable component, an actor in a
lifecycle, an owner of a resource, a subject of an access policy, or a source of measurements. These are
not competing descriptions. Each preserves different properties of the same system for a different
engineering question: structure, behavior, ownership, decisions, measurements, or provenance.
