# The Printer

<!-- point: agents-made-implementation-cheap-not-reliable | Coding agents make implementation cheap enough to change the engineering problem; a sound spec, a coherent model, and evidence do not arrive with that capacity — implementation speed amplifies whatever engineering surrounds it. | terms: code-fabricator -->
Coding agents make implementation cheap enough to change the engineering problem. Describe a change and a
capable agent can often build it quickly, at a scale that once required substantial human effort. What does
not arrive automatically with that capacity is a sound specification, a coherent system model, or evidence
that the result is acceptable. Implementation speed amplifies whatever engineering surrounds it.

<!-- point: the-agent-is-a-printer-so-a-bad-build-indicts-the-instructions | Treat the agent first as a printer, not the engineer of the whole system; when a build is wrong, inspect the engineered setup before inferring a fixed limit in the machine. | terms: printer-metaphor, whose-fault -->
<!-- slogan: printer-posture -->
<!-- slogan: failure-diagnosis -->
That changes how to read a bad build. Treat the agent first as a printer, not as the engineer responsible
for the whole system. A printer realizes a description; when the result is wrong, inspect the engineered
setup before inferring a fixed limit in the machine. Was the intent clear? Was the relevant system state
represented? Was the task bounded sensibly? Did the environment expose the right tools and evidence?
Sometimes the model simply cannot do the work. But failed output alone does not distinguish that case from
a badly engineered task.

<!-- point: printer-is-commodity-intelligence-made-local | This is the Preface's question from the engineer's seat: as implementation becomes abundant, engineering leverage moves toward the instructions, representations, constraints, evidence, and infrastructure that shape autonomous work. | terms: commodity-intelligence, printer-metaphor -->
This is the Preface's question—*how do we safely grant autonomy to commodity intelligence?*—from the
engineer's seat. As implementation becomes abundant, engineering leverage moves toward the instructions,
representations, constraints, evidence, and infrastructure that shape autonomous work. [ref:new-engineering-problem] fixes that shift before the details begin.

## What this book is about (and what it is not)

<!-- point: the-agent-is-not-the-hard-part-of-agentic-engineering | Agent capabilities matter but are not the engineering object this book stabilizes; the durable problem begins when an agent works autonomously where "it usually works" is not an acceptable standard. | terms: commodity-intelligence -->
If you opened a book about agentic software engineering expecting a tour of agents, you may be
surprised by what comes first. That is deliberate. Agent capabilities matter, but they are not the
engineering object this book tries to stabilize; models and vendors will continue to change. The durable
problem begins when an agent works autonomously on a system large enough, important enough, or long-lived
enough that "it usually works" is not an acceptable engineering standard.

So this Part starts one level above the agent. What changed when implementation became cheap? Which old
engineering constraints remain? What properties of the agentic substrate create new opportunities? And
what should the human engineer build around commodity intelligence so that implementation speed becomes
trustworthy production rather than churn? We will get to agents, tools, hooks, skills, and workflows.
MAGE's argument is that the discipline lies in the representations the work reasons through, the
obligations the environment can make authoritative, and the judgment that decides what should become
durable structure.

## The printer

<!-- section-terms: printer-metaphor, open-ended-technology, genre -->

<!-- point: printer-is-the-governing-metaphor | The 3D printer is the book's governing metaphor. | terms: printer-metaphor -->
<!-- index-def: printer-metaphor -->
Think of a 3D printer. It will build an extraordinary range of things,[note: Open-ended technologies repeatedly create this problem. Writing began as a means of recording particular things; its users later invented genres such as poetry, contracts, and textbooks. Printing made reproduction cheap and enabled still more forms. Generative AI has the same open-ended quality: the machine supplies productive capacity, while people still have to discover what is worth producing and how a good instance should be specified.] but it does not
decide what should be built or whether the specification is sound. Its power lies in turning an
authored representation into an artifact. Coding agents increasingly make software implementation
resemble the realization of an upstream engineering description.

<!-- point: leverage-moves-to-the-description | When realization is cheap, leverage moves to the description. | terms: printer-metaphor -->
That is why the metaphor matters. When realizing and revising software required scarce engineering
labor, every design decision carried a substantial implementation-and-feedback cost. As that cost
falls, engineering leverage moves toward what is being realized: the representations through which the
system is understood, the constraints that bind the work, and the evidence by which the result is
accepted.

## A picture is not enough

<!-- section-terms: picture-vs-model -->

<!-- point: everyone-can-sketch-software-but-a-picture-wont-print | Anyone can picture software, but a picture will not print. | terms: picture-vs-model -->
Everyone can sketch a piece of software. We have all used Google Maps, spreadsheets,
a document editor — programs that take in data and do something useful with it. The
concept is easy to picture. But picturing the thing is not the same as knowing how to
build it, and a picture is not a thing you can print.

<!-- point: printer-needs-a-model-not-a-photo | A printer needs a structural model plus print notes. | terms: picture-vs-model, cad-model -->
<!-- index-def: picture-vs-model -->
Hand a 3D printer a photograph of the object you want and nothing comes out. The
printer needs instructions: a CAD model that shows not just the outer shape but the
internal structure — how the pieces fit, where the load is carried — and then a set
of print notes on top of that.

<!-- point: agents-need-the-instructions-not-just-the-picture | A picture yields a plausible instance, not a specification of what must hold. | terms: picture-vs-model, build-instructions -->
<!-- index-example: picture-vs-model -->
Software agents are not quite the same. A capable multimodal agent can take a screenshot
and infer a great deal, so the durable point is not input modality — it is
underspecification. A picture may be enough to generate a plausible instance. It is not
enough to state which properties must hold, which tradeoffs are acceptable, or what
evidence establishes correctness. Those you must supply, or work out how to articulate.
Good instructions raise your odds; they do not guarantee a correct result, and a capable
model will sometimes rescue a poor one. The Printer fixes responsibility, not fidelity:
authoring what the build answers to is the engineer's work.

## Suspect the instructions first {#whose-fault-is-it}

<!-- section-terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->

<!-- point: suspect-the-engineered-setup-before-a-machine-limit | Suspect the engineered setup before inferring a machine limit. | terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->
<!-- index-def: whose-fault -->
<!-- index-example: whose-fault -->
Suspect the engineered setup before you infer a machine limit. When an agent produces
the wrong result, inspect first what you controlled: the instructions, the representation
of the problem, the task boundary, the tools it could use, and the evidence it was asked
to satisfy. Sometimes the model simply cannot do the work. But a failed task does not
distinguish that case from a badly engineered one.

<!-- point: danger-is-velocity-without-judgment-not-the-vendor | Capable models execute a bad direction at speed; velocity is neutral — it amplifies sound and bad engineering alike — so abundant implementation increases the value of getting the upstream engineering right. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger, build-instructions -->
<!-- slogan: danger-in-instructions -->
That distinction matters because capable models can execute a bad direction with extraordinary speed.
Velocity is neutral: it amplifies sound engineering and bad engineering alike. Give commodity intelligence
a doomed direction and it can elaborate that direction much faster than a human team once could. Abundant
implementation therefore increases the value of getting the upstream engineering right.[note: My field notes on this point were less measured: "Claude is the fastest road to hell."]
