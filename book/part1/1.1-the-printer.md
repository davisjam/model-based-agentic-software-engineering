# The Printer

<!-- point: agents-made-implementation-cheap-not-reliable | Coding agents make implementation cheap enough to change the engineering problem; a sound spec, a useful representation, and evidence do not arrive with that capacity — implementation speed amplifies the engineering around it. | terms: code-fabricator -->

Coding agents make implementation cheap enough to change the engineering problem. Describe a change and a capable agent can often build it quickly, at a scale that once required substantial human effort. But the agent may not build what you wanted. Reliable production still requires a sound specification, useful representations, and evidence that the result is acceptable.

<!-- point: the-3d-printer-is-open-ended | A printer is fabricative (open-ended) productive machinery: 2D printers realize documents from descriptions of their contents, 3D printers physical artifacts from descriptions of their geometry. | terms: printer-metaphor -->

<!-- index-def: printer-metaphor -->

For an analogy, let us begin with office supplies. Consider first a stapler and a hole-punch. The purpose and limitations are in the names. Need two sheets joined together, or a hole punched? Look no further. These tools perform narrow operations on artifacts that already exist.

But is the same true about printers? A 2D printer can produce pages of text, images, or a combination of the two. A 3D printer builds physical objects from descriptions of their shapes. In each case, the same machine can produce an enormous variety of artifacts. Both are fabricative: their productive capacity is open-ended.[^fabricative]

<!-- point: open-ended-capacity-must-be-engineered | People make fabricative capacity useful by developing forms and ways of working. | terms: printer-metaphor, commodity-intelligence -->

People make fabricative capacity useful by developing forms and ways of working. For 2D printers, familiar forms include newspapers, posters, and maps, each with conventions for layout and legibility. For a 3D printer, you might be trying to make a mounting bracket, a protective enclosure, or an adapter. These have different purposes and recurring requirements for strength, clearance, and fit. Templates, reusable designs, and established production practices give people starting points for making such artifacts. People develop and adapt this knowledge as they discover what the machinery can usefully make.

<!-- point: agent-inference-needs-engineered-boundaries | Agents can supply unspecified implementation choices; the forms and ways of working around them should preserve freedom among acceptable choices and constrain it where those choices are unacceptable. | terms: printer-metaphor, commodity-intelligence -->

Software agents likewise supply broad realization capacity. Agents can infer much more from an incomplete description than a physical printer can: an algorithm, a data structure, or a decomposition may never have been specified, and yet an agent can choose them and produce an implementation. That inferential capacity is precisely what makes the machinery powerful. The forms and ways of working we develop around agents should preserve this freedom where different choices are acceptable, and constrain it where they are not.

Realization is the act of producing the concrete artifact or action called for by engineering intent. In the software-engineering case developed in this book, that work is conventionally called implementation, so I use implementation from here forward. Implementation here includes the broader work of realizing a software change, not only writing source code. Agents may implement requirements through code, but they may also realize engineering work in documentation, system models, configuration, deployment artifacts, support materials, and operational procedures.

<!-- point: delegating-judgment-is-an-opportunity-not-a-requirement | Delegation can leave useful judgment to the agent, but the engineer decides which decisions to make explicit and which to delegate. -->

When we work with agents, we delegate some judgment as well as production. We do not have to, of course. We could write the entire implementation, hand it to an agent, and ask it to reproduce it. That would be an unusually expensive way to copy a file. Much of the opportunity lies in letting the agent work out things we have not already worked out ourselves. The engineering question is which decisions we should make explicit and which we can responsibly leave to the agent.

<!-- point: a-simple-specification-leaves-enormous-freedom | Even a simple software specification leaves enormous freedom — ten lines or ten thousand, a monolith or microservices — so engineers can specify the properties that matter and leave other decisions to the intelligence performing the work; which choices can safely remain open is itself an engineering decision. -->

Consider how much freedom even a simple software specification leaves. A function could be implemented in ten lines or ten thousand. A service could be built as a monolith or decomposed into microservices. Different algorithms, data structures, and architectures might all satisfy the same requirements. Some choices are better than others, but engineers need not make every choice in advance. They can specify the properties that matter and leave other decisions to the intelligence performing the work. Which choices can safely remain open is itself an engineering decision.

<!-- point: leverage-moves-into-the-environment | As implementation becomes abundant, engineering leverage moves toward the instructions, representations, constraints, evidence, and infrastructure that shape autonomous work. | terms: printer-metaphor, commodity-intelligence -->

When agents perform the implementation, more of the engineer's control acts through the environment in which that work occurs. This is part of what I meant in the Preface by “a level of indirection.” Engineers can express decisions in the representations, constraints, and checks surrounding autonomous work. These structures shape what information is available to the agent, what actions it can take, and what results the environment accepts. Other implementation choices can remain open. [ref:engineering-leverage] shows the shift.

<!-- point: reusable-forms-carry-design-decisions-forward | A layout or physical design preserves decisions made once across many later artifacts while leaving room for variation. | terms: printer-metaphor -->

To illustrate this idea, let's return to the familiar forms developed around printing. An event-poster template might reserve the top for the event name and the bottom for sponsor logos, leaving room between them for the designer's composition. Once defined, that layout can serve an entire series of posters: the event details and artwork change, but the basic organization carries forward. A newspaper can similarly reuse its headline styles and page layout from one edition to the next. For a 3D printer, an enclosure design might fix mounting-hole positions and reserve space for the parts it will contain. Later versions can reuse those dimensions while varying other features. In both cases, the leverage comes from making some design decisions once and putting them into a form that can guide many subsequent artifacts.

An engineering environment is the surrounding structure through which engineering work occurs: its representations, tools, processes, permissions, evidence, and mechanisms. It becomes a governed engineering environment when that structure deliberately carries engineering decisions forward—making consequential knowledge available, enforcing selected obligations, and preserving evidence needed for engineering control. Governed Engineering Environment (GEE) names the resulting MAGE construct.

## The Engineering Object

<!-- point: the-agent-is-not-the-engineering-object | Foundation models and agent products keep changing; the durable engineering problem begins when an agent works autonomously where "it usually works" is not an acceptable standard. | terms: commodity-intelligence -->

Agent products and foundation models will continue to change.[note: Engineering predates machine learning in its use of models as purposeful representations of systems. Machine learning later adopted the same word for a learned approximation of a function—also reasonably a model, but a different kind. This book gives the engineering usage precedence: unqualified model means a purposeful representation of an engineered system or some property of it. I therefore use foundation model for the trained machine-learning system and agent for the acting system built around one through a harness, tools, and working state. Where the distinction matters, I will not ask the reader to infer which "model" I mean.] The durable engineering problem begins when an agent works autonomously on a system large enough, important enough, or long-lived enough that "it usually works" is not an acceptable standard.

<!-- point: mage-is-about-that-environment | MAGE is principally about that environment; the method lies in the representations agents and engineers reason through, the mechanisms that enforce obligations, and the judgment that decides what should become durable engineering structure. | terms: commodity-intelligence -->

MAGE is principally about that environment. We will get to foundation models, harnesses, tools, hooks, skills, and workflows because their properties determine what can be engineered around them. But the method lies in how engineers represent what matters, how they enforce obligations, and how they decide which recurring judgments should become durable engineering structure.

<!-- point: modeling-and-alignment-supply-the-instruction-package | Modeling and Alignment supply the printer's missing instruction halves. | terms: printer-metaphor -->

Chapters {{chapter:modeling|num}} and {{chapter:alignment|num}} develop these ideas. Modeling makes consequential properties explicit; Alignment enforces selected obligations during implementation.

That environment need not simply reproduce the one built for human developers. Agents can consume and maintain representations at scales that would be impractical for people, and automated analyses can check properties that human review would otherwise have to reconstruct from implementation. The design question is therefore two-sided: what existing engineering machinery should agents inherit, and what stronger machinery becomes practical because the workers are now machines?

MAGE is not a general software-engineering method. It does not attempt to teach engineers which programming language to choose, which architectural or design pattern fits a system, or which verification technique is appropriate. Those questions draw on decades of accumulated software-engineering theory and practice. MAGE asks a different question: when such engineering knowledge matters to autonomous implementation, how should it be represented so that the machinery can use it, and which resulting obligations should constrain what the machinery may produce?

Commodity intelligence can also change which traditional engineering choices remain consequential. Consider the choice of a programming language. A human team might once have chosen Java over another suitable language simply because its developers already knew Java. If an agent can implement competently in either language, that constraint largely disappears: some language still has to be chosen, but the particular choice may no longer deserve much engineering attention. Other language choices remain consequential. A deployment target may require a particular toolchain, or a safety requirement may favor a language that provides a needed property. The relevant question is therefore not whether engineers have traditionally made a choice, but what property made the choice consequential.

The same distinction applies at larger scales. Suppose a service needs persistent storage. If either a relational or nonrelational database can satisfy every property that matters, choosing between them may simply be a degree of freedom left to implementation. But if the system requires transactions across related records, a particular consistency model, or another property that distinguishes the alternatives, the choice becomes consequential. MAGE does not supply the database theory needed to make that judgment. It asks that the consequential property be represented—and, where necessary, enforced—rather than relying on each implementing agent to rediscover why the choice mattered.

Notice the distinction between unmodeled and free. A choice is unmodeled when the engineering representations do not state it; it is free only when engineering permits the alternatives. Suppose the model says nothing about which database to use. If either database really can satisfy every consequential property, the omission represents a genuine degree of freedom. But if one alternative would violate a security, consistency, or performance obligation, the choice was never free. The model simply failed to make the relevant obligation explicit.

<!-- point: mage-accepts-the-lesson-changes-the-level | External representation, memory, and tools have long improved machine reasoning; MAGE shifts the primary engineering focus to the engineered environment through which autonomous work proceeds over time. -->

That emphasis has important antecedents. AI research has long improved machine reasoning by giving systems access to external representations,[cite: besta2024got] managed memory,[cite: packer2023memgpt] and specialized solvers.[cite: liu2023llmp] Recent agentic software-engineering research extends this approach through repository retrieval,[cite: zhang2023repocoder] program analysis,[cite: zhang2024autocoderover] and interfaces designed for autonomous work.[cite: yang2024sweagent] The ambition predates foundation models.[cite: rich-waters] {{chapter:profession}} returns to these traditions and to what commodity intelligence changes. The distinction lies in the engineering objective: MAGE shifts the primary engineering focus from individual implementation tasks to the engineered environment through which autonomous software work proceeds over time, including the representations and controls that govern its results.

## Examine the Environment First {#whose-fault-is-it}

<!-- section-terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->

<!-- point: suspect-the-engineered-setup-before-a-machine-limit | When an agent fails, examine its engineering environment before concluding that the foundation model lacks the necessary capability. | terms: whose-fault -->

When an agent fails, examine its engineering environment before concluding that the foundation model lacks the necessary capability. Observed performance is a property of the whole arrangement, not just the productive machinery.

<!-- point: a-failed-part-has-several-diagnoses | A failed part can reveal several different problems. | terms: whose-fault -->

A 3D printer separates productive capability from the instructions that condition it. A photograph may show what an object should look like, but appearance does not specify internal structure, material, tolerances, load paths, or fabrication constraints. A failed part can therefore reveal several different problems: the machine lacked capability; the model omitted a consequential property; the chosen material could not satisfy it; or the fabrication instructions permitted a realization that should have been ruled out.

<!-- point: inferred-completion-is-not-explicit | Inferred completion is not an explicit specification. | terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->

Software agents blur this distinction because they can infer missing structure. A capable agent may produce an impressive result from a screenshot, prose request, or incomplete specification. But inferred completion is not an explicit statement of which properties must hold, which tradeoffs are acceptable, or what evidence establishes acceptance.

<!-- point: inspect-what-the-engineer-controlled-first | When an agent produces the wrong result, inspect first what the engineer controlled — instructions, representation, task boundary, tools, evidence; failed output alone does not establish that the foundation model lacks the capability. | terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->

When an agent produces the wrong result, inspect first what the engineer controlled: the instructions, the representation of the problem, the task boundary, the available tools, and the evidence the work was required to satisfy. Sometimes the foundation model simply lacks the necessary capability. Failed output alone does not establish that diagnosis.

<!-- point: velocity-amplifies-sound-and-bad-engineering-alike | Abundant implementation raises the stakes: capable agents execute a bad direction at extraordinary speed, and velocity amplifies sound engineering and bad engineering alike. | terms: build-instructions -->

<!-- slogan: danger-in-instructions -->

Abundant implementation raises the stakes. Capable agents can execute a bad direction with extraordinary speed. Velocity amplifies sound engineering and bad engineering alike.[note: My field notes on this point were less measured: "Claude is the fastest road to hell."]

[^fabricative]: I use *fabricative* (open-ended) for a productive technology whose useful output classes are not fixed by the machine itself. The capability arrives before the engineering conventions for exploiting it. New artifact types, representations, interfaces, constraints, and operating practices are discovered as people learn what the technology is useful for. Writing and general-purpose computation offer earlier examples; commodity generative intelligence adds a similarly broad realization substrate to engineering.
