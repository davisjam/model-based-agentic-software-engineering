# The Printer

<!-- point: agents-made-implementation-cheap-not-reliable | Agents made implementation cheap; reliability did not come with it. | terms: code-fabricator -->
Coding agents have made writing software cheap. Describe what you want and a capable
model builds it, fast, at a scale no team could match. Implementation is abundant now. What
has not come with it is reliability: the same machine builds the wrong thing as readily as
the right one, and just as fast.

<!-- point: the-agent-is-a-printer-so-a-bad-build-indicts-the-instructions | An agent is a printer; a bad build indicts instructions. | terms: printer-metaphor, whose-fault -->
<!-- slogan: printer-posture -->
<!-- slogan: failure-diagnosis -->
So the chapter opens on a question: when an agent builds the wrong
thing, where does the fault lie? Treat the agent as a *printer*, not a coder. A printer builds
within the description you provide, so read a bad build first as evidence about the
instructions, the representation, the task boundary, and the environment that produced
them — not, by reflex, as a fixed ceiling in the machine. If implementation is cheap and unreliable, the
engineering moves to authoring what the printer reads.

<!-- point: printer-is-commodity-intelligence-made-local | The printer is the engineer's-seat face of commodity intelligence. | terms: commodity-intelligence, printer-metaphor -->
This is what the Preface's question — *how do we safely grant autonomy to commodity intelligence?* — looks
like from the engineer's seat. The printer is commodity intelligence made local: implementation so
abundant that it will build almost anything you describe. The scarcity has moved upstream. What matters
now is the engineering of the instructions, representations, constraints, and evidence that shape what
gets built.

One picture fixes the shift before the details begin. [ref:new-engineering-problem] contrasts the classical pipeline, where an engineer's skill largely sets a program's quality, with the agentic one, where the environment the work passes through does.

## What this book is about (and what it is not)

<!-- point: the-agent-is-not-the-hard-part-of-agentic-engineering | You came for agents; the agent itself is not the hard part. | terms: commodity-intelligence -->
If you opened a book about agentic software engineering expecting a tour of agents, you may be
surprised by what comes first. That is deliberate. The agent itself is not the hard part of agentic
software engineering. Agents are becoming abundant, capable, and increasingly interchangeable. The
engineering problem begins when you ask one to work autonomously on a system large enough, important
enough, or long-lived enough that "it usually works" is not good enough.

So this Part starts one level above the agent. What changed when implementation became cheap? What now
limits engineering? And what must the human engineer build around commodity intelligence so that its
speed becomes trustworthy production rather than churn?

We will get to agents, tools, hooks, skills, and workflows. But MAGE's claim is that those are
machinery. The discipline lives in the models the machinery reasons through, the obligations the
environment enforces, and the judgment that decides what should become permanent structure.

## The printer

<!-- section-terms: printer-metaphor, open-ended-technology, genre -->

<!-- point: printer-is-the-governing-metaphor | The 3D printer is the book's governing metaphor. | terms: printer-metaphor -->
<!-- index-def: printer-metaphor -->
Think of a 3D printer. It will build an extraordinary range of things,[note: Open-ended technologies repeatedly create this problem. Writing began as a means of recording particular things; its users later invented genres such as poetry, contracts, and textbooks. Printing made reproduction cheap and enabled still more forms. Generative AI has the same open-ended quality: the machine supplies productive capacity, while people still have to discover what is worth producing and how a good instance should be specified.] but it does not
decide what should be built or whether the specification is sound. Its power lies in turning an
authored representation into an artifact. Coding agents increasingly occupy the same role in
software: implementation is becoming the execution of an upstream description.

<!-- point: leverage-moves-to-the-description | When realization is cheap, leverage moves to the description. | terms: printer-metaphor -->
That is why the metaphor matters. When implementation was expensive, much of engineering effort was
spent realizing the design. When realization becomes cheap, leverage moves toward the description
itself: what is represented, what is left implicit, what constraints bind the result, and what
evidence says it is acceptable.

## A picture is not enough

<!-- section-terms: picture-vs-model -->

<!-- point: everyone-can-sketch-software-but-a-picture-wont-print | Anyone can picture software, but a picture will not print. | terms: picture-vs-model -->
Everyone can sketch a piece of software. We have all used Google Maps, spreadsheets,
a document editor — programs that take in data and do something useful with it. The
concept is easy to picture. But picturing the thing is not the same as knowing how to
build it, and a picture is not a thing you can print.

<!-- point: printer-needs-a-model-not-a-photo | A printer needs a structural model plus print notes. | terms: picture-vs-model, cad-model -->
<!-- index-def: picture-vs-model -->
Hand a 3D printer a photograph of the object you want and nothing comes out. The
printer needs instructions: a CAD model that shows not just the outer shape but the
internal structure — how the pieces fit, where the load is carried — and then a set
of print notes on top of that.

<!-- point: agents-need-the-instructions-not-just-the-picture | A picture yields a plausible instance, not a specification of what must hold. | terms: picture-vs-model, build-instructions -->
<!-- index-example: picture-vs-model -->
Software agents are not quite the same. A capable multimodal agent can take a screenshot
and infer a great deal, so the durable point is not input modality — it is
underspecification. A picture may be enough to generate a plausible instance. It is not
enough to state which properties must hold, which tradeoffs are acceptable, or what
evidence establishes correctness. Those you must supply, or work out how to articulate.
Good instructions raise your odds; they do not guarantee a correct result, and a capable
model will sometimes rescue a poor one. The Printer fixes responsibility, not fidelity:
authoring what the build answers to is the engineer's work.

## Suspect the instructions first {#whose-fault-is-it}

<!-- section-terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->

<!-- point: suspect-the-engineered-setup-before-a-machine-limit | Suspect the engineered setup before inferring a machine limit. | terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->
<!-- index-def: whose-fault -->
<!-- index-example: whose-fault -->
Suspect the engineered setup before you infer a machine limit. When an agent produces
the wrong result, inspect first what you controlled: the instructions, the representation
of the problem, the task boundary, the tools it could use, and the evidence it was asked
to satisfy. Sometimes the model simply cannot do the work. But a failed task does not
distinguish that case from a badly engineered one.

<!-- point: capable-models-execute-a-bad-spec-at-speed | Capable models execute a bad specification at full speed. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger, build-instructions -->
That distinction matters because capable models will execute a bad specification with
extraordinary speed. They do not naturally stop to ask whether the path you chose was
wise.

> Claude is the fastest road to hell.

<!-- point: danger-is-velocity-without-judgment-not-the-vendor | The danger is velocity without judgment, not the vendor. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger, build-instructions -->
<!-- slogan: danger-in-instructions -->
The line is intentionally unfair to Claude. The danger is not the vendor; it is velocity
without judgment. Give commodity intelligence a doomed direction and it can elaborate
that direction much faster than a human team ever could. Abundant implementation amplifies
the quality of the engineering upstream — good or bad.
