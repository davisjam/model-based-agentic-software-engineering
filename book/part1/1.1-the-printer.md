# The Printer

<!-- point: agents-made-implementation-cheap-not-reliable | Agents made implementation cheap; reliability did not come with it. | terms: code-fabricator -->
Coding agents have made writing software cheap. Describe what you want and a capable
model builds it, fast, at a scale no team could match. Implementation is abundant now. What
has not come with it is reliability: the same machine builds the wrong thing as readily as
the right one, and just as fast.

<!-- point: the-agent-is-a-printer-so-a-bad-build-indicts-the-instructions | An agent is a printer; a bad build indicts instructions. | terms: printer-metaphor, whose-fault -->
<!-- slogan: printer-posture -->
<!-- slogan: failure-diagnosis -->
So the chapter opens on a question: when an agent builds the wrong
thing, where does the fault lie? Treat the agent as a *printer*, not a coder. A printer builds
within the description you provide, so read a bad build first as evidence about the
instructions, the representation, the task boundary, and the environment that produced
them — not, by reflex, as a fixed ceiling in the machine. If implementation is cheap and unreliable, the
engineering moves to authoring what the printer reads.

<!-- point: printer-is-commodity-intelligence-made-local | The printer is the engineer's-seat face of commodity intelligence. | terms: commodity-intelligence, printer-metaphor -->
This is what the Preface's question — *how do we safely grant autonomy to commodity intelligence?* — looks
like from the engineer's seat. The printer *is* commodity intelligence made local: implementation so
abundant it will build almost anything you describe, and only what you describe. When intelligence is the
commodity, the scarce thing is the authoring — the instructions, representations, and boundaries the
printer reads.

Two pictures fix the shift before the details begin. [ref:new-engineering-problem] contrasts the classical pipeline, where an engineer's skill largely sets a program's quality, with the agentic one, where the environment the work passes through does. [ref:environment-as-object] names the consequence: where the common move is to engineer the agent, MAGE engineers the environment the agent works in.

## What this book is about (and what it is not)

<!-- section-terms: task-mode-decision, supervised-autonomy -->

<!-- point: the-agent-is-not-the-hard-part-of-agentic-engineering | You came for agents; the agent itself is not the hard part. | terms: commodity-intelligence -->
If you opened a book about agentic software engineering expecting a tour of agents, you may be
surprised by what comes first. That is deliberate. The agent itself is not the hard part of agentic
software engineering. Agents are becoming abundant, capable, and increasingly interchangeable. The
engineering problem begins when you ask one to work autonomously on a system large enough, important
enough, or long-lived enough that "it usually works" is not good enough.

So this Part starts one level above the agent. What changed when implementation became cheap? What now
limits engineering? And what must the human engineer build around commodity intelligence so that its
speed becomes trustworthy production rather than churn?

We will get to agents, tools, hooks, skills, and workflows. But MAGE's claim is that those are
machinery. The discipline lives in the models the machinery reasons through, the obligations the
environment enforces, and the judgment that decides what should become permanent structure.

<!-- point: task-mode-is-first-judgment | The first judgment is whether a task needs governed autonomy. | terms: task-mode-decision, one-shot-scripting -->
Of course, not every programming task needs the machinery this book describes. Some tasks a
capable model can simply do. You describe the input and the output, you push the button,
and a working program comes back on the first pass. No iteration, no governance, no loop management.
Knowing which tasks are like that, and
which are not, is the first judgment an agentic engineer has to make. [ref:task-mode-decision]
lays out that judgment as a flowchart.

<!-- label: task-mode-decision -->
<!-- figure: assets/one-shot-vs-supervised-autonomy.svg | *The Task-Mode Heuristic.* Specify a task's input/output behavior, then split on size and difficulty: a simple no-reasoning task goes to one-shot scripting, a hard or reasoning-heavy one to supervised autonomy. If unsure, try to one-shot it, but switch paths rather than throw good tokens after bad. -->

### The two modes

<!-- section-terms: one-shot-scripting, supervised-autonomy -->

<!-- point: two-modes-fork-on-size-and-difficulty | Mode forks on the task's reasoning horizon. | terms: task-mode-decision, reasoning-horizon, long-horizon-task -->
<!-- slogan: reasoning-limit -->
<!-- index-def: reasoning-horizon -->
<!-- index-def: long-horizon-task -->
Start at the decision on the left. You have a computational task and you can say
what it takes in and what it must produce. From there the path forks on how big and
how hard the work is. More formally, what makes a task hard is often its **reasoning
horizon** — the amount of intermediate reasoning state you must hold in mind at once
to get it right — and a **long-horizon task** is one whose reasoning state will not
fit losslessly within the working memory available to hold it.

<!-- point: one-shot-for-small-unsubtle-tasks | One-shot scripting fits a small, short-horizon task. | terms: one-shot-scripting, reasoning-horizon -->
<!-- index-def: one-shot-scripting -->
**One-shot scripting** is the righthand branch. A task belongs here when its relevant
reasoning state fits comfortably in one pass and the result is cheap to inspect — a few
thousand lines is a rough indicator, not the rule. Think of a CSV-to-HTML report script. You specify the behavior
once, the agent writes it in a single pass, and you read the result. Its reasoning
horizon is short — the whole transaction fits in one context window and one sitting.
When a task really is this size, the full apparatus of governed autonomy is wasted
effort. Send it one-shot and move on.

<!-- point: supervised-autonomy-supplies-four-things | Supervised autonomy supplies four things in two groups. | terms: supervised-autonomy, correctness-conditions, long-horizon-task -->
<!-- index-def: supervised-autonomy -->
**Supervised autonomy** is the downward branch — the method for a long-horizon task,
and where most of this book lives. You do not hand the agent the whole job and hope:
its reasoning state is too large to hold in a single context window, or to trust to a
single pass. So you supply four things, in two groups. The system
side shapes what gets built:

- **Correctness conditions** — what a right answer looks like.
- **Sub-tasks** — the breakdown you have proposed to divide the work.

The development side shapes how the building runs:

- **Tools that determinize recurring processes**, so the same operation runs the
  same way every time.
- **Monitoring and intervention**, so you can watch the work and step in when it
  drifts.

<!-- point: book-structure-fills-the-four | Each later part develops one of the four elements. | terms: supervised-autonomy -->
Those four are the skeleton of supervised autonomy, and each later part of the book
fills one of them out.

### Where the emphasis falls

<!-- section-terms: supervised-autonomy, sizing-the-leap, reasoning-horizon -->

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | Where the emphasis falls — the section names the reasoning-horizon axis and the modularity bound the whole book turns on; six short paragraphs, no wall of text -->

<!-- point: reasoning-horizon-sorts-tasks | Tasks sort along the reasoning horizon, from short to long. | terms: reasoning-horizon, supervised-autonomy -->
That fork runs along the reasoning horizon. Writing a shell script, proving a theorem,
debugging a deadlock, and re-architecting a subsystem sit at very different points on
it, and the horizon is a property of the task, not of who does the reasoning — a
human, an agent, or some later machine. The longer the horizon, the more the work has
to be supervised rather than one-shot.

<!-- point: mode-misjudgment-is-costly-both-ways | Misjudging the mode either way is expensive. | terms: one-shot-scripting, supervised-autonomy -->
This book covers both modes. It has to, because the first skill is telling
them apart, and getting that wrong in either direction is expensive. Treat a
one-shot task as if it needed supervised autonomy and you drown a five-minute
script in ceremony. Treat a supervised-autonomy task as if it were one-shot and you
get confident, plausible, subtly wrong output at a scale no human can read.

<!-- point: leash-length-from-data-density-and-capability | Leash length follows training-data density and model capability. | terms: sizing-the-leap, training-data-bias -->
So the book spends real pages on the sizing question. How long a leash a task can
run on comes down to two things: how densely the model's training data covers the
kind of work you are asking for, and how capable the model is at that work in the
first place. The denser the coverage and the stronger the model, the more briefly
a task can be described and the farther it can be trusted; the nearer the task sits
to the edge of what the model has seen, the more the reasoning must be broken into
smaller, checked steps. Judging that distance tells you whether a job can go
one-shot or must be supervised, and the book gives you the means to judge it.

<!-- point: modularity-lowers-the-horizon-not-removes-it | Modularity lowers the reasoning horizon; it does not remove it. | terms: reasoning-horizon -->
Breaking the work into smaller, checked steps is how you pull a long horizon back
within reach: each step holds less reasoning state at once. But **modularity reduces
the reasoning horizon; it does not eliminate it.** The pieces still have to compose,
and holding how they compose is itself reasoning state. The craft is in cutting the
task so the state any one step carries stays inside what the reasoner can hold.

<!-- point: books-weight-is-supervised-autonomy | The book's weight falls on supervised autonomy. | terms: supervised-autonomy, context-window -->
But the weight of the book is on the downward branch. The interesting engineering, the
part that did not exist a few years ago and is not yet written down, is
**supervised autonomy**: building larger-scale products whose quality needs exceed
what a model can be trusted to do alone, and whose size exceeds current and
foreseeable context windows. That is the regime where you stop pressing go on a
script and start running a printer, framing the goal, modeling the world for the
machine, and putting every change on rails from dispatch to production. Everything
after this chapter is about how to do that well.

<!-- point: code-is-cheap-now-productionize-it | Code is cheap; turn the fabricator into a production line. | terms: code-fabricator -->
So the premise of the book is short: we have a fast code-fabricator, and the work is
turning it into a production line.

## The printer

<!-- section-terms: printer-metaphor -->

<!-- point: printer-is-the-governing-metaphor | The 3D printer is the book's governing metaphor. | terms: printer-metaphor -->
<!-- index-def: printer-metaphor -->
Think of a 3D printer. It will build almost anything you can imagine — a replacement gear, a
prosthetic hand, a scale model of a building — out of nothing but spooled filament. What is
magic about it is that it is unbounded, except by your own creativity. That machine is the
metaphor that drives this entire book, because building software with a coding agent
is far more like running a 3D printer than most people expect — a difference the rest
of this chapter unpacks.

## An open-ended technology

<!-- section-terms: open-ended-technology, genre -->

<!-- point: open-ended-technology-mints-genres | An open-ended technology arrives with no list; people invent genres. | terms: open-ended-technology, genre -->
A 3D printer belongs to a rare class of technology — the *open-ended* kind — alongside the
invention of writing and the printing press. What those three share is
that none of them arrives with a list of what to make. People had to invent that. Writing began
as marks for counting cattle; only later did someone work out the poem, the textbook, the
contract — each a *genre* somebody had to discover. The printing press inherited them all, then,
by making production cheap, minted genres no scribe could
ever have justified: the poster, the pamphlet, the newspaper.

<!-- point: genai-is-open-ended-book-takes-one-genre | Generative AI is open-ended; this book takes one genre. | terms: open-ended-technology, genre, production-software -->
Generative AI belongs to this class. It is open-ended in the same way — it will make almost
anything, and it hands you no list. So running the machine is the smaller half of the work;
the larger half is working out which genres are worth producing, and the method for producing
each one well. This book takes one
genre and gives it a method: **production software** — code whose quality metrics you actually
care about, and whose every change you can trace back to the metric it moves.

<!-- gloss: Quality metrics | The properties of the code you actually care about — correctness, performance, security, maintainability — named explicitly, and traceable: for any change, you can point to the metric it moves. Production software is code whose quality metrics are named and whose changes map back to them. -->

<!-- point: quality-floor-was-always-the-price-now-cheap | The quality floor was the price; agents make it cheap. | terms: quality-floor, production-software -->
> **The quality floor, made cheap.** The apparatus in this book — the
> models, the gates, the traceability — is the price of
> *any* software you can trust: name the properties that must hold, check them, and
> keep every change tied to the property it moves. That price was always the same. What
> changed is who can afford it. For decades this level of assurance was reachable only by
> the shops that could justify the cost — the Rolls-Royces and the Boeings, where a
> mistake is catastrophic and a modeling program pays for itself. Agents drop that cost
> to almost nothing: they write the checks, maintain the models, and hold the line, so
> the quality once affordable only to a regulated few is now within reach of almost
> anyone. Agents sharply reduce the implementation cost of that assurance, bringing it
> within reach of ordinary production software. [data: nearly-free]

## A picture is not enough

<!-- section-terms: picture-vs-model -->

<!-- point: everyone-can-sketch-software-but-a-picture-wont-print | Anyone can picture software, but a picture will not print. | terms: picture-vs-model -->
Everyone can sketch a piece of software. We have all used Google Maps, spreadsheets,
a document editor — programs that take in data and do something useful with it. The
concept is easy to picture. But picturing the thing is not the same as knowing how to
build it, and a picture is not a thing you can print.

<!-- point: printer-needs-a-model-not-a-photo | A printer needs a structural model plus print notes. | terms: picture-vs-model, cad-model -->
<!-- index-def: picture-vs-model -->
Hand a 3D printer a photograph of the object you want and nothing comes out. The
printer needs instructions: a CAD model that shows not just the outer shape but the
internal structure — how the pieces fit, where the load is carried — and then a set
of print notes on top of that. You slice the model into layers, and you think about
how those layers compose as the machine lays them down. Different materials have
different minimum thicknesses, different strengths, different joints that hold and
joints that fail. There are a hundred ways to misprint a part that looked fine as a
picture.

<!-- point: agents-need-the-instructions-not-just-the-picture | Agents need the build instructions, not just the picture. | terms: picture-vs-model, build-instructions -->
<!-- index-example: picture-vs-model -->
Software agents are the same. You cannot hand an agent only the picture of what you
want. You must also give it — or work out how to articulate — the detailed instructions
for how to build it. Good instructions, and it works beautifully. Bad instructions,
and you have not got a hope.

## Suspect the instructions first {#whose-fault-is-it}

<!-- section-terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->

<!-- point: agent-is-a-printer-not-a-stapler-so-suspect-the-instructions | An agent is a printer, not a stapler; suspect instructions. | terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->
<!-- index-def: whose-fault -->
Here is the shift in posture. When you use a stapler and the
staple jams, that is the stapler's fault, not yours. A stapler is supposed to just
work — it is so foolproof my three-year-old runs one, insert the paper, ka-chunk, no
problem. That is exactly what an agent is not. An agent is not a stapler — it is a
printer, and a printer only ever builds what its instructions describe. So when the
agent produces the wrong thing, suspect the instructions first.

<!-- point: models-can-build-anything-you-can-explain | Models build anything you can explain; explaining is the discipline. | terms: whose-fault, build-instructions -->
<!-- index-example: whose-fault -->
In my own experience, when an agent has failed to produce what I wanted, the fault has
almost always been mine — the instructions, the way I represented the problem, where I
drew the task boundary. Look there first, before you conclude the model simply cannot do
the work. The strong models build most of what we can envision in software — *so long as
you can explain it precisely enough to build*. And the discipline is older than it
looks: an engineer has always been accountable for the instructions handed to a machine,
even the ones not hand-crafted — the back matter returns to what that responsibility
becomes when the thing you author is the model, not the code.

<!-- point: the-promise-is-also-the-danger | Building anything you describe is both promise and danger. | terms: printer-metaphor, velocity-exposes-the-danger -->
The printer builds anything you can describe. That is the promise. But it is also the
danger, because a printer with no judgment behind it builds
a wrong thing as fast and as willingly as a right one, and carries you at full speed
exactly where your instructions point.

> Claude is the fastest road to hell.

<!-- point: speed-exposes-the-danger-not-causes-it | Speed does not cause the danger; it exposes it. | terms: velocity-exposes-the-danger, build-instructions -->
<!-- slogan: danger-in-instructions -->
The danger was already in the instructions; the speed just gets you there first. Hand
the machine a doomed idea and it does not slow down to warn you; it makes the doomed
idea better and better, an afternoon and ten thousand plausible lines deep into a dead
end you picked. The printer that builds anything will build your mistake with the same
tireless fidelity it builds your success. Which one you get is down to your instructions.

<!-- point: running-example-is-a-real-shipped-system | The running example is a shipped system, not a toy. | terms: running-example -->
A word on the example this book builds on. The running example is a real shipped system:
DocAble, the production accessibility-remediation tool — its own pipeline,
its own agent fleet, its own scars — described in full at the back, in
[The Built System](5.3-the-built-system.html). Every later chapter returns to it, so what you watch getting printed
correctly, chapter by chapter, from model through mechanism and architecture to migration,
is a system that shipped. The next chapter gives you a first concrete look.
