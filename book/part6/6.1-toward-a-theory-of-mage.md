# A Theory of MAGE

<!-- point: theory-explains-both-part-v-views | This section proposes one explanation for both evidence-chapter views: the deep case and the broad variation. -->
{{chapter:software-factories}} ended with two views of agentic engineering: one system studied deeply enough to
reconstruct mechanisms and sequence, and independent systems studied broadly enough to expose variation.
This section proposes an explanation for both.

<!-- point: theory-is-directional-not-numerical | The theory is directional, not numerical: it names the main parts, how they interact, and the relationships that should hold under stated conditions. -->
The theory is directional rather than numerical. It names the main parts, how they interact, and the
relationships that should hold under stated conditions. {{sec:what-the-theory-predicts}} turns those claims into testable predictions.

<!-- point: central-claim-capacity-acts-through-the-environment | The central claim: agentic capacity acts through the engineered environment, which turns it into progress or churn. -->
The central claim is simple. **Agentic capacity acts through the engineered environment that surrounds
it.** A strong environment turns more implementation capacity into durable progress; a weak or
incoherent one turns the same capacity into churn, escaped defects, repeated intervention, and control
friction. Those outcomes change what later work inherits. Poor realizations can degrade architecture,
representations, or conventions, making later work harder. Engineers can instead diagnose
recurring problems and change the environment so that later work inherits better structure. As the
environment improves, larger tasks and greater autonomy become feasible, exposing the next frontier.

<!-- box-family: canonical -->
> ### Theory of MAGE — informal
>
> Agentic capacity does not by itself produce engineering progress. It magnifies the fit—or misfit—between
> the work attempted and the engineered environment that surrounds it.

Engineering progress is intentionally domain-specific. Software engineering supplied the evidence for the
theory and gives its quantities concrete forms: landed changes, defects, review, and rework. What may
transfer is the causal structure. Agentic capacity acts through an environment, and the fit between that
environment and the work shapes useful output, escaped failure, and human attention.
{{sec:scope-conditions}} asks how far that structure transfers beyond software.

<!-- point: the-pieces-this-chapter-assembles | Chapter 1 posed the reliability problem of delegation; Modeling and Alignment supplied two engineering responses; Chapter 5 placed those mechanisms inside a production system — the pieces this chapter puts together. -->
Chapters {{chapter:problem|num}}–{{chapter:alignment|num}} developed the reliability problem and two
engineering responses. Modeling changes the representation over which reasoning occurs. Alignment gives
selected obligations evidence and enforcement independent of the producing reasoner.
{{chapter:software-factories}} placed both inside a production system.

<!-- point: the-starting-point-is-the-master-equation | The starting point is the Master Equation: a decomposition of the probability that delegated reasoning produces an acceptable realization, describing one episode so we can ask where reliability comes from and where it can fail. -->
The starting point is the **Master Equation**: a decomposition of the probability that delegated reasoning
produces an acceptable realization. For work realized through a representation \(R\), it factors that
probability into the three stages a delegation must survive — correct encoding of consequential intent,
correct interpretation of that encoding, and correct realization of the interpreted intent:

<!-- eq: p_R = P(E \mid R)\, P(I \mid E, R)\, P(L \mid I, E, R) -->

The Master Equation describes one realization episode. That narrow view lets us ask precisely
where reliability comes from and where it can fail.

<!-- point: the-chapter-progressively-widens-the-view | The chapter widens the view in four steps: the cost of attempts and retries, independent assurance against unacceptable outcomes, an environment modified by the work itself, and the economics of maintaining it across a stream of changes. -->
We progressively widen that view. First, we add the cost of attempts and retries. Next, we
separate successful production from independent assurance against unacceptable outcomes. We then allow
engineering work to modify the environment inherited by later work. Finally, we account for the cost and
value of maintaining that environment across a stream of changes.

<!-- point: direction-from-one-realization-to-an-evolving-system | The direction matters: what begins as a model of one probabilistic realization becomes a model of an evolving engineering system. -->
The direction matters. What begins as a model of one probabilistic realization becomes a model of an
evolving engineering system.

<!-- point: the-equations-are-enlargements-not-rivals | The chapter's equations are enlargements, not rivals: each adds something the previous one deliberately omitted, widening the unit of analysis step by step. -->
The chapter therefore contains several equations at different scales, and they are not competing
formulations. Each adds something the previous one deliberately omitted ([ref:equation-hierarchy]).

| Model | Question it answers | What it adds |
|---|---|---|

| Retry economics | What does success cost? | \(\bar C_{\text{realize}} = \frac{c}{p}\) |

| Dynamic environment | What does this episode leave behind? | \(p_i = p(T_i, M, R_i, H, \mathcal{G}_i)\), \(\mathcal{G}_{i+1} = f(\mathcal{G}_i, a_i)\) |

<!-- point: p-and-q-are-different-quantities | Foreshadowing the distinction the hierarchy turns on: a stronger model may raise p, while an independent gate can leave p unchanged and lower q — q is not a component of the Master Equation but the residual probability that an unacceptable candidate escapes independent assurance. -->
The hierarchy distinguishes production probability \(p\) from escape probability \(q\). They are different
quantities, moved by different levers. A stronger model may
increase \(p\). An independent gate may leave the producer's \(p\) unchanged while decreasing \(q\). And
\(q\) is not another component of the Master Equation: it is the residual probability that an unacceptable
candidate escapes independent assurance. Keeping the two apart is what allows production and assurance to
be engineered separately.

## The Dynamic Model

<!-- point: dynamic-model-capacity-environment-produce-performance | Agentic capacity and the governed environment jointly shape realized performance: throughput, defect escape, attention consumed. -->
[ref:mage-dynamic-model] summarizes the theory. **Agentic capacity** supplies model capability,
concurrency, and raw implementation rate. The **governed engineering environment** supplies the
representations, constraints, evidence, and enforcement through which that capacity acts. Together they
shape three outcomes: durable throughput, defect escape, and human attention consumed per
unit of useful output.

<!-- label: mage-dynamic-model -->
<!-- figure: assets/mage-dynamic-model.svg | *The MAGE Dynamic Model.* Agentic capacity acts through a governed engineering environment. Mismatch creates governance pressure; diagnosis and adaptation can improve the environment inherited by later work. The balancing loop seeks fit, while capability amplification reopens the frontier as stronger environments support more ambitious work. Prior engineering knowledge seeds the system before failure occurs. -->

<!-- point: mismatch-shows-as-symptoms-that-need-diagnosis | Mismatch shows as churn, escaped defects, and friction; symptoms signal a problem but not which one. -->
When the environment does not fit the work, the mismatch becomes visible as churn, recurring failures,
escaped defects, repeated intervention, or friction from the controls themselves. These symptoms reveal a
mismatch, but not its cause. Engineers must still diagnose whether the problem is local or structural.
That diagnosis drives the change to the environment, which shapes the next round of work.

<!-- point: feedback-structure-knowledge-work-gaps-diagnose-improve | The feedback loop: prior knowledge seeds the environment; work exposes gaps; diagnosis improves it; ambition repeats. -->
Prior engineering knowledge seeds the environment. Work exposes gaps between what the task requires and
what the environment supports; diagnosis can turn those gaps into changes to the shared environment. As
that environment supports more ambitious work, new gaps appear.

<!-- point: cybernetic-heritage-good-regulator | The feedback structure has a cybernetic heritage; Conant and Ashby's good regulator theorem connects regulation to representation — the regulated object changed, but the model-regulation link remains. -->
This feedback structure has a precedent in cybernetics, which studied how regulators use information about a
system to keep its behavior within acceptable bounds. Conant and Ashby's good regulator theorem makes
representation central: under its assumptions, the simplest optimal regulator must embody a model of the
system it regulates.[cite: conant-ashby]

<!-- point: environment-represents-observes-adapts | The environment represents consequential properties, observes work, and changes when its representations or controls prove inadequate. -->
MAGE applies this regulatory view to software engineering. The environment represents consequential properties of
the system and its engineering process, observes what happens during the work, and changes when its
representations or controls prove inadequate. The recurring question is simple: what must the environment
know in order to govern the work?

<!-- point: two-routes-ex-ante-and-ex-post | Durable structure has two routes: before failure from known obligations, after experience from use (governance conversion). -->
Not all useful structure is discovered through failure. Existing architecture, standards, requirements,
operating history, and lessons imported from earlier systems can seed the environment before an agent
acts. Other needs become visible only in use. Durable structure can enter the environment **before
failure**, when engineers encode known obligations or representations, or **after experience** exposes
recurring costs or risks. Governance conversion names the latter route.

<!-- point: adaptation-needs-diagnosis-and-a-path-to-the-shared-environment | Adaptation reaches the shared environment only with structural diagnosis and a path with authority to change it. -->
Two conditions determine whether adaptation reaches the shared environment:

1. **Diagnosis.** Someone must recognize that the pressure is structural rather than local.
2. **Authority.** That diagnosis must have a path into the shared architecture, models, mechanisms, or infrastructure.

Without diagnosis, pressure produces motion without direction. Without authority, the lesson dies as a
local patch. {{sec:scope-conditions}} treats both as scope conditions.

## Where Engineering Can Apply Leverage

<!-- point: leverage-has-several-intervention-points | Engineering can intervene at model, environment, process, representation, or evidence. -->
The dynamic model suggests a broader question: where can engineering intervene to improve agentic work?
Engineers can intervene at five points:

1. the reasoning model;
2. the context, tools, memory, or harness through which it works;
3. the process that decomposes, coordinates, reviews, and approves work;
4. the representations over which consequential reasoning occurs; and
5. the evidence and controls that determine which results acquire consequence.

These interventions can substitute for or complement one another. A stronger reasoner may need less support from its environment; a better representation may make the same task tractable for a weaker reasoner; a stronger admission mechanism may tolerate a less reliable producer.

<!-- point: factory-constructs-map-into-the-theory | The factory of Chapter 5 and the constructs of the theory name the same production system: each modeled quantity has a factory reading, and the categories overlap because one mechanism can serve more than one engineering role. -->
The theory models these intervention points.
[ref:factory-theory-mapping] reads each of them back onto the factory
{{chapter:software-factories}} described.

<!-- point: one-school-locates-leverage-in-the-process | One school locates leverage in process design around agents. -->
One emerging school of thought puts substantial leverage in process design around the agents. Hassan's Agentic Software Engineering treats trustworthy agentic development as an end-to-end system spanning people, process, tools, and artifacts. Yegge's Gas Town approaches the problem from orchestration: persistent work state, specialized agent roles, handoffs, coordination, supervision, and merge machinery organize many fallible agents into a functioning software factory. Both make the organization of production an important control surface.[cite: hassan2026; yegge2026]

<!-- point: mage-weights-the-environment-and-representations | MAGE weights the representations and enforceable obligations that work inherits from the engineered environment; those mechanisms complement process design, so the theory treats capability, representation, harness, and enforcement as distinct variables. -->
MAGE places additional weight on the representations and enforceable obligations that work inherits from the engineered environment. Modeling changes the objects over which humans and agents reason; Alignment gives selected obligations evidence and enforcement independent of the producing reasoner; governance conversion lets later work inherit lessons learned by earlier work. These mechanisms complement process design. The theory therefore treats model capability, representation, harness and process, and enforcement as distinct variables whose effects may substitute for or compound one another; {{sec:research-agenda}} returns to that design space.

## What Makes an Environment Effective

<!-- point: more-machinery-is-not-a-better-environment | More machinery is not a better environment; what counts is fit to the work, not how much machinery exists. -->
More machinery does not mean a better environment. Ten thousand lines of stale checks are worse than one
hundred lines that govern the obligations that matter. What counts is not how much machinery exists, but how
well the environment fits the work.

<!-- point: four-properties-of-an-effective-environment | An effective environment needs useful representations, adequate coverage, coherence, and economy. -->
An effective environment needs four things:

1. **Useful representations.** The information needed for the work is explicit at an appropriate level of abstraction.
2. **Adequate coverage.** Important obligations have appropriate evidence and enforcement.
3. **Coherence.** Models and mechanisms reinforce one another rather than contradicting or needlessly duplicating.
4. **Economy.** Their future value justifies their maintenance, friction, and context cost.

<!-- point: four-properties-govern-the-probabilistic-surface | The four properties jointly determine how much consequential work still rests on fallible per-instance inference. -->
Together these determine how much consequential work still depends on fallible per-instance inference: the
probabilistic surface of {{chapter:problem}}. Representation makes consequential knowledge explicit and, where its structure permits, easier to reason about. Coverage supplies evidence and
enforcement. Coherence asks whether the parts work together. Economy asks whether avoiding repeated
judgment is worth the cost of carrying the machinery.

<!-- point: coverage-need-not-mechanize-every-obligation | Coverage need not mechanize every obligation; a strong environment names where judgment belongs and where paying repeatedly is wasteful. -->
Coverage does not require mechanizing every obligation. Some belong in a compiler rule or admission gate;
others remain statistical, semantic, or consequential enough to require human judgment. A strong
environment does not mechanize everything. It makes clear **where judgment belongs and where paying for the
same judgment repeatedly would be wasteful**.

<!-- point: not-a-maturity-model-improve-the-limiting-part | These are not maturity stages; improve whichever part of the environment is limiting the work. -->
These are not stages of a maturity model.[cite: bollinger1991critical] Improve the part of the environment that is limiting the work. If
engineers repeatedly reconstruct important state, improve the representation. If failures escape, strengthen
evidence or enforcement. If controls have become a tax, reconcile or remove them.

## Outcomes and Observables

<!-- point: distinguish-activity-from-progress-three-outcomes | The theory distinguishes activity from progress via three outcomes: durable throughput, defect escape, human-attention burden. -->
A theory of agentic engineering should distinguish activity from progress. MAGE therefore treats raw
implementation velocity as an input and tracks three outcomes: **durable throughput**, **defect
escape**, and **human-attention burden**. [ref:mage-outcomes] defines each outcome and identifies candidate
observables.

<!-- label: mage-outcomes -->
<!-- table: *Outcomes of the theory.* Raw implementation activity is an input. The theory is interested in how much survives as durable progress, what failures escape, and how much scarce human judgment that progress consumes. [short: The three MAGE outcomes and their observables] -->
| Outcome | What it measures | Candidate observables |
|---|---|---|
| **Durable throughput** | Raw implementation converted into lasting system progress | landed changes net of rework; churn share; the slope of sustained throughput |
| **Defect escape** | Relevant failures reaching later lifecycle stages or production | escaped-defect rate; reopen rate; silent-corruption escape |
| **Human-attention burden** | Human judgment required per unit of durable output | interventions per landed change; review time; conversion effort |

<!-- point: definitions-of-the-three-outcomes | Durable throughput survives rework; defect escape reaches later stages; attention burden is judgment consumed per unit. -->
Durable throughput is useful system progress that survives rework, integration, and operation rather than
merely landing in the repository. Defect escape records consequential failures that reach later lifecycle
stages or production. Human-attention burden measures the scarce engineering judgment consumed per unit of
durable progress.

<!-- box-family: inset -->
<!-- inset-domain: HUMAN FACTORS -->
> #### Inset — Human Attention Is Not a Free Control {#inset-human-attention-not-a-free-control}
>
> Moving work from human execution to machine execution does not eliminate the cost of human attention if people must inspect the resulting stream. Human-factors research has studied this problem for decades. Wolfe and colleagues found that rare targets in visual search were disproportionately missed as target prevalence fell.[cite: wolfe2005rare] Warm, Parasuraman, and Matthews synthesize a broader vigilance literature showing that sustained monitoring is not passive work: it consumes attentional resources, imposes substantial workload, and can produce performance decrements and stress.[cite: warm2008vigilance] These findings apply particularly to human-machine systems in which people monitor automated processes.
>
> Operational practice provides the corresponding field example. The Transportation Security Administration uses covert tests of real airport screening operations to identify vulnerabilities in passenger and checked-baggage screening. Government Accountability Office reviews document both the use of these tests and failures that prompted changes to testing and remediation.[cite: gao2019covert] The setting differs from software review, but the engineering lesson is general: consequential inspection performed by trained professionals remains a capacity-limited control. More automation upstream does not create more human attention downstream.
>
> This matters directly for agentic engineering. If implementation output grows while assurance continues to require proportional human inspection, human attention becomes a throughput and assurance bottleneck. MAGE therefore does not seek to remove humans from consequential judgment. It seeks to concentrate human judgment where judgment is actually required. Recurring obligations that can be represented, evidenced, or enforced adequately should increasingly be carried by the engineered environment; scarce human attention can then be spent on ambiguous evidence, novel failures, tradeoffs, exceptions, and decisions whose consequences genuinely require expert judgment.

<!-- point: human-attention-is-a-conserved-resource | Human attention is not an implementation detail of governance; it is one of the scarce resources the engineered environment is intended to conserve. -->
Human attention is therefore not merely an implementation detail of the governance process. It is one of
the scarce resources the engineered environment is intended to conserve.

<!-- point: activity-counts-are-not-outcome | Easy-to-count quantities (commits, lines, control files, support ratio) measure activity or investment, not outcome. -->
Several easy-to-count quantities measure activity or investment rather than outcome: commits,
lines changed, control files, model-sync events, and support ratio. They can help explain what an
environment is doing; they do not establish that it is producing a return.[^activity-data]

[^activity-data]: The activity-versus-outcome gap already shows up in industry data. A 2026 benchmark of 83,000 developers across 253 organizations[cite: linearb2026] reports large merge-rate gains among heavy AI users, yet finds yield falling among the heaviest even as merge rates roughly doubled; an industry analyst reads the same period as rising AI spend without proportionate shipping velocity, the difficulty having moved from comprehension to confidence.[cite: riggins2026] Activity is not the same as durable progress.

## Three Core Predictions

<!-- point: three-predictions-three-ways-environment-helps | Three core predictions concern three ways an engineered environment can help. -->
Three predictions form the core of the theory. Each describes a different way the engineered environment
can help.

First, the environment can make additional agentic capacity more productive rather than merely faster.
Second, it can give a reasoner a better surface over which to work, reducing how much of the system must be
reconstructed for each task. Third, it can preserve useful engineering judgment so that later work inherits
it rather than purchasing the same reasoning again.

<!-- point: the-three-effects-are-distinct | Environment fit, representation leverage, and engineering capital are related but distinct effects. -->
These effects are related, but they are not the same. Environment fit concerns whether capacity can be
productively absorbed. Representation leverage concerns what the reasoner must reconstruct. Engineering
capital concerns what later work gets to inherit.

<!-- point: environment-fit-moderates-velocity | Environment fit moderates the effect of velocity: added capacity yields throughput when the environment fits and churn when it does not; raw velocity may rise either way. -->
**Environment fit moderates the effect of velocity.** Increasing agentic capacity should produce more durable throughput
when the relevant engineered environment fits the work, and more churn, escaped failure, or repeated
intervention when it does not. In either case, raw implementation velocity may increase; what changes is how much of that
activity becomes durable progress. The effect of added velocity therefore depends on whether the environment supplies
the representations, evidence, enforcement, and coherence the work requires.

<!-- point: representation-creates-leverage | A trustworthy task-relevant model lowers the cost of acceptable realization by reducing reconstruction; it fails if upkeep outweighs reach. -->
**Representation creates leverage.** A task-relevant, trustworthy model should reduce the expected cost of
reaching an acceptable realization, or increase the scale of work completed at matched quality, by reducing
reconstruction and exposing consequential distinctions at a more tractable abstraction. The advantage should
matter most as the required reasoning state grows. The claim fails if models impose maintenance and
interpretation costs without extending reasoning reach or preserving quality.

<!-- point: engineering-capital-amortizes-judgment | Durable structure lets later work inherit judgment, but the return lasts only while the asset stays fit. -->
**Engineering capital amortizes judgment.** Where useful engineering knowledge or recurring judgment
becomes durable structure, later work over that surface should require less reconstruction, repeated
judgment, or rework, and should carry less risk. The prediction includes its own boundary: the return lasts
only while the asset remains fit. Stale models, obsolete validators, conflicting controls, and procedures
that outlive their problem should lose value and can eventually impose net cost.

<!-- point: each-core-claim-can-fail | Each core proposition can fail; they are testable propositions, not guarantees. -->
Each claim can fail. The environment may have little moderating effect; explicit models may add cost
without extending reasoning reach; accumulated structure may cost as much as the judgment it was meant to
retire. These are the theory's core claims. {{sec:what-the-theory-predicts}} derives more specific predictions from them.

### The reasoning-horizon proposition

Why should representation create leverage in the first place?

<!-- point: reconstruction-precedes-reasoning-about-the-question | Answering an engineering question first requires reconstructing enough of the system to know what it means. -->
Consider what happens when an engineer enters an unfamiliar system to answer a seemingly simple question:
*Can this service depend on that one?* The answer may be distributed across source files, configuration,
dependency injection, deployment descriptors, documentation, and conventions that exist only in the history
of the system. Before reasoning about the dependency, the engineer must first reconstruct enough of the
system to know what the dependency means.

<!-- point: context-postpones-but-a-model-changes-the-problem | Larger context and retrieval postpone the horizon; DocAble met the limit literally — roughly 590,000 tokens of reasoning representation against a 272,000-token context made windowing and whole-document compression the next representation problem. -->
An agent faces the same problem. A larger context window can postpone the limit, and better retrieval can
bring useful evidence closer, but neither changes the underlying fact that implementation contains far more
detail than most engineering questions require. DocAble encountered the limit literally: one document
produced roughly 590,000 tokens of reasoning representation against a 272,000-token context. That result
did not argue for a larger context window; it made windowing and whole-document compression the next
representation problem.

<!-- point: the-general-move-presents-consequential-structure | The general move: a useful model changes the problem by presenting the consequential structure directly — the basis for the reasoning-horizon proposition. -->
That is the general move: a useful model changes the problem by presenting the consequential structure
directly. This is the basis for the reasoning-horizon proposition.

> **Reasoning-Horizon Proposition.** Large software systems contain more potentially relevant state than
> any finite reasoner can keep active at once. Task-relevant models can extend the effective reasoning horizon by
> replacing implementation detail with semantically richer representations of the properties under
> consideration. The gain holds only while the representation is relevant, sufficiently faithful to the
> relation it claims, and cheaper to use than reconstructing the same knowledge from lower-level artifacts.

**General form.** Consequential work can require more potentially relevant state than a finite reasoner can
reliably keep active at once. Task-relevant representations can extend the effective reasoning horizon when
they preserve the distinctions needed for the work while suppressing irrelevant detail. The software
proposition is one specialization of this claim. Whether other domains admit representations with the same
leverage is an empirical question.

<!-- point: a-model-shortens-the-reasoning-path | A model shortens the reasoning path, not merely each step: reasoning already performed is preserved in the environment. -->
A useful representation can shorten the reasoning path, not merely make each reasoning step easier. Without
an explicit model, an agent may first have to reconstruct the relevant architecture, state, dependencies,
obligations, or other relationships from lower-level artifacts before it can reason about the engineering
question itself. When the environment already supplies a trustworthy, task-relevant model, the agent can
skip much of that reconstruction and reason directly over the consequential structure. In this sense, the
environment preserves some prior reasoning instead of forcing each attempt to reconstruct it.

<!-- point: map-analogy-preserve-relationships-suppress-detail | Like a map, a model's value is preserving the relationships a class of questions needs while suppressing detail. -->
The effect is analogous to using a map rather than repeatedly reconstructing a city from street-level
observations. The map does not contain everything in the city, nor should it. Its value comes from
preserving the relationships needed for a particular class of questions while suppressing detail that would
otherwise have to be rediscovered. An engineering model provides the same kind of leverage when it
preserves the distinctions relevant to the work.

<!-- point: agents-change-the-economics-of-an-old-scale-problem | Agents do not create the scale problem; they change its economics and frequency, raising the return on representations. -->
Agentic systems do not create this scale problem. Human engineers have always used abstractions because no
engineer can keep the implementation of a Linux-scale system in active view. Commodity intelligence changes
the economics and frequency of the problem: larger delegated tasks and greater implementation volume
increase the return on representations that preserve useful reasoning across episodes.

<!-- point: representations-put-consequential-structure-into-usable-form | Representations put consequential structure into a form the reasoner can use directly, not merely store facts. -->
These representations do more than store facts. State machines expose transitions, dependency graphs expose
relationships, contracts expose obligations, and assurance arguments relate claims to evidence. They put
consequential structure into a form the reasoner can use directly.
{{chapter:modeling}} offered six useful classes of such model, not a proof that they are necessary, sufficient, or
minimal. Because representation creates leverage and can expand what Alignment can govern, choosing,
reducing, and even learning representations is itself an engineering problem. The principle is simple: **a
representation is worthwhile when the reasoning it saves is worth more than the cost of building and carrying
it.**

<!-- point: leverage-can-originate-bottom-up-from-recovered-models | Leverage can originate bottom-up: implementation signals can recover a latent model the system already embodies. -->
Representation leverage can also work in the other direction. {{chapter:one-problem-many-models}} showed the practical version of this move: use implementation signals and commodity intelligence to help recover latent models from an existing system. Repeated structures can reveal a domain concept the implementation already embodies but has never named; bottom-up analysis can recover an abstraction that later humans, agents, and mechanisms use directly. The representation is new as an explicit engineering artifact, even when the structure it captures was already latent in the system.

<!-- point: representation-innovation-machines-may-propose-new-representations | Representation innovation: a reasoner may propose a decomposition or state space under which a question becomes tractable. -->
The stronger possibility is representation innovation. A reasoner need not be limited to recovering an abstraction that engineers already know how to name. By working across implementations, histories, measurements, failures, and existing models, commodity intelligence may propose a different decomposition, relation, state space, or other representation that makes an engineering question tractable. The innovation is not that the machine draws a novel diagram. It is that the representation exposes consequential structure that existing representations did not make practically available for reasoning or analysis.

<!-- point: representations-human-designed-induced-and-innovated | Representations can be human-designed, machine-induced, or machine-innovated, each extending the effective reasoning horizon. -->
This extends the reasoning-horizon proposition in both directions. Human-designed representations can compress engineering knowledge into forms that let machines reason beyond implementation detail. Machine-induced representations can make latent structure explicit. Machine-innovated representations may go further, exposing relationships that extend what humans and later machines can practically reason about.

<!-- point: discovery-does-not-confer-trust-a-representation-must-earn-it | Discovery does not confer trust: a machine-proposed representation still must earn trust in the relationship it claims. -->
Discovery does not confer trust. Neither induction nor innovation makes a representation trustworthy. Like any model in {{chapter:modeling}}, a machine-proposed representation claims a relationship to the system and must earn the trust placed in that claim. A recovered model can be checked against the implementation it purports to summarize; a genuinely novel abstraction poses the harder question of what evidence establishes that it preserves the distinctions required by its intended engineering use. The stronger the reasoning, assurance, or enforcement built on the representation, the stronger that evidence must be.

<!-- point: representation-engineering-is-bidirectional | Representation engineering is bidirectional: humans extend machine reasoning and machines may recover or invent abstractions. -->
Representation engineering can therefore work in both directions. Humans can construct abstractions that extend machine reasoning; machines can recover abstractions implicit in human-built systems; and machines may eventually help invent abstractions that extend the effective reasoning horizon of both. The research agenda returns to how representations can be selected, induced, invented, validated, learned, and reduced.

### The engineering-capital proposition

Representation leverage explains how structure can make one episode of reasoning easier. The next question
is what happens across episodes.

<!-- point: repeated-reconstruction-pays-for-the-same-reasoning | Repeatedly reconstructing the same fact or judgment pays for the same reasoning; some judgments can instead be preserved. -->
If engineers repeatedly reconstruct the same architectural fact, rediscover the same failure mode, or make
the same review judgment, the organization repeatedly pays for essentially the same reasoning. But some
judgments can be preserved. A dependency rule can become a validator. An architectural distinction can
become a model. An operational lesson can become a procedure. Once that happens, later work begins from a
different starting point.

<!-- point: engineering-capital-defined-inherited-productive-capacity | Engineering capital: prior engineering work leaves behind productive capacity that future work can inherit. -->
This is the sense in which MAGE uses the term engineering capital: prior engineering work leaves behind
productive capacity that future work can inherit.

> **Engineering-Capital Proposition.** Durable engineering structures become capital when future work
> inherits useful capacity from them: less reconstruction, less repeated judgment, earlier or stronger
> evidence, safer action, or cheaper recovery. Models, mechanisms, architectures, procedures, and other
> engineering assets can all qualify. Their returns are local to the surfaces where later work inherits that
> capacity, and last only while the assets remain fit.

<!-- point: capital-not-memory-must-return-and-can-depreciate | Capital, not memory: an asset becomes capital when it changes future cost or risk, and it can depreciate — while the investment threshold itself moves as agentic capacity lowers construction and maintenance costs. -->
The metaphor is capital rather than memory. A document that merely records an old decision may preserve
information; an engineering asset becomes capital when it changes the cost, capability, or risk of future
work. Like other capital, it must produce a return to justify carrying it, and it can depreciate when the
environment changes. What counts as a justifying return is not fixed either: as agentic capacity lowers
the cost of constructing and maintaining such assets, investments that were once uneconomical can clear
the threshold.

<!-- point: capital-transfers-judgment-beyond-its-originator | Capital transfers judgment beyond its originator: a later reasoner benefits without reconstructing the reasoning. -->
That inheritance need not depend on the continued presence of the engineer who supplied the original
judgment. When an architectural distinction becomes a trustworthy model, a review judgment becomes a
validator, or an operational lesson becomes a reliable procedure, some of the originating engineer's
productive capacity now lives in the environment. A later engineer or agent can benefit from the result
without reconstructing the reasoning that produced it.

<!-- point: capital-transfer-is-partial-judgment-needed-again-on-change | The transfer is partial: capital carries only what it represents, so change requires expert judgment again. -->
The transfer is necessarily partial. Durable structure carries only the judgment it successfully represents
or mechanizes. When requirements change, a new failure appears, or the representation itself becomes
inadequate, engineering judgment is needed again. Engineering capital therefore does not eliminate
expertise; it changes how often the organization must pay again for expert judgment on the same question.

<!-- point: debt-vs-capital-governance-includes-retirement | Debt makes change costly, capital makes it productive; neither is permanent, so governance includes retirement. -->
The analogy to technical debt is intentional. Debt makes future change more expensive; capital makes
future change more productive. Neither status is permanent. Capital depreciates as systems, obligations,
and tools change. A validator can become noise, a model can drift, and a once-useful constraint can begin
blocking legitimate work. Governance therefore includes maintenance, reconciliation, and retirement. **Accumulation is not the objective; productive capacity is.**

<!-- point: three-interacting-debts-technical-cognitive-intent | Debt has three interacting forms — technical, cognitive, intent — which GenAI can accelerate together. -->
The analogy also connects MAGE to an emerging account of debt in AI-assisted software. Storey argues that technical debt in the implementation is only one of three interacting forms of debt. Cognitive debt accumulates when a team's shared understanding of the system erodes; intent debt accumulates when goals, constraints, and rationale are poorly externalized or maintained. Generative AI can accelerate all three by increasing implementation faster than teams can understand it or preserve why it exists.[cite: storey2026]

<!-- point: gee-acts-on-technical-intent-and-cognitive-debt-differently | A GEE counters technical and intent debt with durable structure and preserves the understanding needed for cognitive debt. -->
A Governed Engineering Environment acts on each layer, although not in the same way. Architectural constraints, validators, models, and other durable structures can reduce technical debt by keeping later realizations inside selected structural boundaries. Explicit requirements, decisions, models, and obligations counter intent debt by preserving consequential knowledge outside any one person or reasoning episode. Cognitive debt is different: MAGE does not require every engineer to reconstruct every generated implementation in detail. It instead seeks to preserve the understanding needed for consequential engineering decisions in representations and evidence that humans and agents can inspect, challenge, and reuse.

<!-- point: capital-gives-knowledge-a-home-governance-keeps-it-trustworthy | Capital gives knowledge, intent, and judgment a durable home; governance decides which assets stay trustworthy. -->
This does not make the three debts disappear. A stale model can preserve obsolete intent; an incoherent collection of mechanisms can itself become technical burden; and no representation can substitute for understanding that was never captured in the first place. The point is narrower. Engineering capital gives selected knowledge, intent, and judgment a durable place to live, while governance determines which of those assets remain trustworthy enough for later work to inherit.

## Modeling and Alignment Are Distinct—and Compound

<!-- point: modeling-and-alignment-are-two-separable-effects | Durable structure combines two separable effects: helping the reasoner answer, and controlling whether a bad answer gains consequence. -->
The previous propositions explain why durable structure can help, but they combine two effects that MAGE
keeps separate. An environment can help the reasoner reach a satisfactory answer, and it can
independently control whether an unsatisfactory answer acquires consequence. MAGE calls the first **Modeling**
and the second **Alignment**.

<!-- point: modeling-changes-representation-alignment-enforces-obligations | Modeling changes the representation reasoned over; Alignment enforces selected obligations; either can help without the other. -->
Modeling and Alignment are therefore distinct engineering activities. Modeling changes the representation
through which engineers and agents reason; Alignment enforces selected obligations on realization.
Either can produce value without the other.

<!-- point: either-lever-works-without-the-other | A model can help without enforcing; a mechanism can govern even when the producer never sees the rule. -->
A model can reduce reconstruction and make satisfactory realization easier without enforcing anything.
Conversely, an Alignment mechanism can govern an obligation even when the producing agent never sees the
representation or rule from which the mechanism decides. A type can exclude an invalid state, a sandbox can
deny a capability, and a validator can reject a forbidden dependency even if the agent repeatedly proposes
one.

Consider a simple architectural obligation: service A may not depend directly on service B.

<!-- point: two-problems-help-the-agent-vs-block-the-forbidden | Two distinct problems: help the agent produce acceptable work, and ensure the forbidden result cannot enter regardless. -->
One engineering problem is helping an agent understand the relevant architectural structure well enough not to propose the
forbidden dependency. A useful structural model may make acceptable realizations easier to produce. A
different engineering problem is ensuring that the forbidden dependency cannot enter the system even when
the producer gets that relationship wrong. An independent admission check can provide that guarantee.

### Cost and failure, separately

We can make the distinction precise by considering cost and failure separately.

<!-- point: modeling-lowers-expected-cost-of-acceptable-realization | Under a simple retry model the expected realization cost names the expected production cost of obtaining one acceptable realization; one role of Modeling is to raise per-attempt success or lower per-attempt cost so that quantity falls. -->
The probability in question is already familiar. For a representation \(R\) available to a reasoner,
\(p_R\) is the aggregate realization probability whose interior the Master Equation exposes: the
probability that one attempt produces a realization satisfying a particular obligation. Let \(c_R\) be the
cost of producing that attempt. A better representation can increase \(p_R\), decrease \(c_R\), or both. Under a simple retry
model in which successive attempts have the same success probability \(p_R\) and expected cost \(c_R\),
the **expected realization cost** — the expected production cost required to obtain one acceptable
realization — is

<!-- eq: \bar C_{\text{realize}}(R) = \frac{c_R}{p_R} -->

This is one role of Modeling: make acceptable realization cheaper to find. Later sections relax the
fixed-attempt assumption when successive changes alter the engineering environment.

<!-- point: the-ratio-matters-more-than-either-term | The ratio exposes the interaction between cost and reliability: halving attempt cost and doubling attempt success move the expected realization cost equally, so a representation that raises attempt cost can still be preferable when its increase in p_R is large enough. -->
The ratio is useful because it exposes the interaction between cost and reliability. Halving the cost of
an attempt and doubling its probability of success have the same first-order effect on this quantity.
More generally, a more expensive attempt can be cheaper in expectation if its probability of
satisfactory realization rises enough. A structured representation may therefore cost more to construct,
maintain, or supply than an informal description and still reduce the expected realization cost. A
representation \(R_2\) is worth preferring to \(R_1\) whenever

<!-- eq: \bar C_{\text{realize}}(R_2) < \bar C_{\text{realize}}(R_1) -->

<!-- point: representation-trades-against-reasoning-capability | Cheap articulation can be expensive in expectation; a stronger reasoner raises p_R at inference cost, a better representation changes the problem — so Modeling trades representational investment against realization cost and reasoning capability. -->
Conversely, cheap natural-language articulation can be expensive in expectation when consequential
intent is incompletely expressed or must be repeatedly reconstructed by the reasoner. A better
reasoning model can compensate for a weak representation by raising \(p_R\), but at greater
inference cost; a better representation can instead change the problem the reasoner must solve.
Modeling can therefore trade representational investment against both realization cost and
reasoning capability — which is why commodity intelligence does not by itself retire the case for
representation. {{sec:research-agenda}} makes the trade concrete: a trustworthy dependency model can
turn work that appeared to require a frontier model into a graph problem a cheaper reasoner handles
reliably — a consequence of this inequality, not a separate observation.

<!-- point: representation-precedes-model-selection | Representation precedes model selection: ask what representation makes the problem available for reasoning before asking which reasoner should solve it — in DocAble, a practical cost rule: improve the representation before buying more inference. -->
**Representation precedes model selection.** Before asking which reasoner should solve a problem, ask
what representation would make the problem available for reasoning. In DocAble this became a practical
cost rule: improve the representation before buying more inference.

<!-- point: the-probabilities-belong-to-a-production-system | The probabilities belong to a production system: the factory of Chapter 5, redrawn with each step of the realization path carrying the probability that it succeeds and the encoded property also supplying the tolerance the result is evaluated against. | terms: tolerance -->
The probabilities in these expressions belong to a production system.
[ref:probability-in-the-software-factory] puts them on the factory {{chapter:software-factories}}
asked the reader to inspect.

<!-- label: probability-in-the-software-factory -->
<!-- figure: assets/probability-in-the-software-factory.svg | *Probability in the software factory.* Correct realization depends on correctly encoding consequential intent, correctly interpreting that encoding, and correctly realizing the interpreted intent. One representation R is held fixed throughout the figure, so each probability term is drawn in its short form; the text conditions each of them on R. Process design changes the conditions under which interpretation and realization occur. When a consequential property is represented as an independently evaluable tolerance, evidence and admission can govern that property without relying solely on the producing reasoner. [short: Probability in the software factory — the encode, interpret and realize steps of the production path, and the encoded property forking into the tolerance the realization is evaluated against] -->

<!-- point: fabricator-and-tolerance-act-differently-on-the-same-system | The distinction matters because improving the fabricator and improving the tolerance act differently on this system. | terms: tolerance -->
The distinction matters because improving the fabricator and improving the tolerance act
differently on this system.

<!-- point: p-r-factors-into-encode-interpret-realize | Every term is conditioned on the representation R: an analytical (not independence-claiming) factorization of p_R into encoding E, interpretation I, and realization L exposes the asymmetry — a stronger reasoner improves interpretation and realization but cannot recover intent that was never encoded. -->
The aggregate probability \(p_R\) hides several distinct opportunities for failure, and the Master
Equation that opened this chapter separates them. \(E\) denotes correct encoding of consequential intent,
\(I\) correct interpretation of that encoding, and \(L\) correct realization of the interpreted intent.
For a fixed representation \(R\), \(P(E \mid R)\) is the probability that consequential intent is
correctly encoded in that representation, and

<!-- eq: p_R = P(E \mid R)\, P(I \mid E, R)\, P(L \mid I, E, R) -->

The Master Equation is a chain decomposition, not an independence assumption: the stages will not in
general be statistically independent. Its value is that the three stages fail for different reasons:

- **Encoding** — did the human correctly externalize consequential intent into the available
  representation?
- **Interpretation** — did the reasoner correctly recover that intent from the representation?
- **Realization** — did the reasoner successfully realize what it understood?

<!-- point: the-encoding-term-is-also-a-human-channel | The first term is easy to overlook: a representation can improve P(E | R) by helping the engineer express the intended property correctly, even if it leaves P(I | E, R) unchanged — which is why Modeling is an engineering activity rather than a technique for supplying context to an agent. -->
The first term is easy to overlook in an agentic setting. Representation is not valuable only because a
machine may interpret a structured model more reliably than prose. It can also help the engineer express
the intended property correctly in the first place. Different representations make different distinctions
visible, force different decisions to be made explicitly, and make omissions easier or harder to notice.
A representation can therefore improve \(P(E \mid R)\) even if it leaves \(P(I \mid E, R)\) unchanged.
This is one reason Modeling is an engineering activity rather than merely a technique for supplying
context to an agent.

<!-- point: capable-agents-can-raise-the-encoding-term-through-feedback | Representation is not the only route to the encoding term: comprehension amplification returns evidence that a realization missed what the engineer meant, so capable agents can raise P(E | R) indirectly by making the encoding correctable. -->
Representation is not the only route to that term. Because a realization that misses the engineer's
meaning is evidence about the encoding, the comprehension amplification of
{{sec:the-dynamics-of-mage}} can raise \(P(E \mid R)\) indirectly: capable agents return that evidence
quickly enough for the engineer to encode the intent again.

<!-- point: the-two-factory-techniques-act-on-different-factors | The two factory techniques act on different factors: process design changes the conditions under which interpretation and realization occur, while explicit tolerances bear on encoding and make selected properties available for independent evaluation. | terms: tolerance -->
The {{chapter:software-factories}} distinction identifies where different factory interventions enter
this decomposition. Process design changes the conditions under which interpretation and realization
occur: context, tools, decomposition, routing, permissions, and other features of the harness can
change \(P(I \mid E, R)\), \(P(L \mid I, E, R)\), their cost, or several at once. A better agent raises the
same terms. Explicit tolerances act differently. They bear on \(P(E \mid R)\) — did we correctly represent
the property we actually care about? — and they make selected consequential properties available for
independent evaluation, so the factory need not rely solely on successful producer interpretation and
realization to determine whether those properties hold. The realization can instead be measured
against the represented tolerance and rejected.

<!-- point: interpretation-depends-on-how-much-inference-is-asked | The interpretation factor also depends on how much inference the representation asks the agent to supply, and that burden varies: where the intended solution follows well-represented patterns, a sparse specification can still interpret well. -->
The probability of correct interpretation also depends on how much inference the representation
asks the agent to supply. That burden is not constant across engineering tasks. A capable model has
encountered enormous numbers of ordinary database interactions, standard algorithms, common
framework idioms, and familiar architectural patterns. When the intended solution follows such
well-represented patterns, a sparse specification may still yield a high probability of correct
interpretation. The agent can supply much of the missing detail from what it already knows.

<!-- point: novelty-reduces-the-usefulness-of-the-agents-prior | Other things equal, novelty is one factor that reduces the usefulness of the agent's prior as a substitute for explicit representation: specification supplies explicit engineering intent where inference from prior patterns is least dependable. -->
The situation changes as the intended approach becomes less conventional. Novel algorithms, unusual
architectural constraints, domain-specific invariants, and decisions that deliberately depart from
common practice provide less familiar structure for the agent to recover. Other things equal, the
more novel the intended engineering decision, the less confidence we should place in correct
interpretation of what has been left implicit. Specification substitutes explicit engineering intent
for inference from prior patterns.

<!-- point: familiarity-solves-production-not-assurance | Familiarity solves the production problem but does not establish the property: unless the consequential property is represented, the environment cannot distinguish following the intended pattern from producing something that looks right. -->
But familiarity solves only the production problem. It does not establish the property. If an agent
usually interprets "connect this service to the database" in the conventional way, that prior may be
entirely adequate for a prototype. If the production system depends on a particular transaction
boundary, consistency property, authorization rule, or failure behavior, relying on the same prior
provides no independent basis for determining that the obligation was preserved. The agent may probably
do the familiar thing; unless the consequential property is represented, the engineering environment
cannot reliably distinguish following the intended pattern from merely producing something that looks
right.

<!-- point: learned-priors-do-not-supply-an-assurance-surface | Raising the interpretation factor does not retire explicit models or Alignment: learned priors reduce how much must be specified for realization without supplying an assurance surface for consequential properties. -->
**Familiarity is not assurance.** This is why increasing \(P(I \mid E, R)\) does not
eliminate the role of explicit models or Alignment. Learned priors can reduce how much must be specified
for realization. They do not by themselves provide an assurance surface for consequential properties.

<!-- point: process-design-cannot-discharge-a-natural-language-obligation | The same limit applies to process design: it improves how an agent interprets natural-language intent, but an obligation left in natural language may require another act of probabilistic interpretation to establish that the result satisfies it, so tolerance supplies an independently evaluable boundary instead. | terms: tolerance -->
The same limit applies to the production conditions that raise that factor. Better context,
decomposition, tools, examples, and review all improve how an agent interprets natural-language
intent. None of them changes what happens next. If the consequential obligation itself remains in
natural language, establishing that the result satisfies it may require another act of
probabilistic interpretation. Ambiguity is only part of this. The evaluation inherits the
fallibility of the production, so a factory that improves only its production conditions has
strengthened one probabilistic step and left the other standing. Tolerance changes the structure
of the problem by supplying an independently evaluable boundary around acceptable realizations.

<!-- point: interpretation-factor-is-conditional-on-familiarity | The interpretation factor is conditional not only on encoding quality and agent capability but also on the familiarity of the intended interpretation relative to what the agent already knows. -->
Thus \(P(I \mid E, R)\) depends not only on the quality of the encoding and the capability of the agent,
but also on the familiarity of the intended interpretation relative to what the agent already knows.

<!-- point: the-first-factor-sits-at-the-human-interface | Encoding failure belongs largely to the human/factory interface: failing to articulate a consequential distinction and failing to recover it are different failures, and the entitlement case shows a representation that could not express the intent at all. -->
The first factor sits largely at the human/factory interface, not in machine reasoning. Two
different failures hide there: the engineers may never have articulated a consequential
distinction, and the reasoner may have failed to recover it. The entitlement failure from
{{chapter:modeling}} is the worked instance. The scalar representation read each group's unlimited
flag and still returned a single finite sum: its output type had no place for the distinction, so
the distinction died before any reasoner saw it. No downstream capability could have recovered
what the representation could not express.

<!-- point: capability-cannot-recover-unencoded-intent | The asymmetry: capability can compensate for difficult interpretation but not for missing intent, so Modeling helps both before implementation begins — and can sometimes remove the judgment entirely. -->
A sufficiently capable reasoner cannot recover information that the engineering environment failed
to express. Capability compensates for difficult interpretation; it does not reliably compensate
for missing intent. Modeling can therefore improve realization
economics in two ways before implementation begins: by helping humans state what they mean, and by
giving machine reasoners a better surface from which to recover it. And Modeling sometimes goes
beyond raising these probabilities at all — the determinization frontier later in this section
names the case where a change of representation makes a reconstructed semantic property
mechanically decidable, removing the per-instance judgment altogether.

<!-- point: perfect-reasoner-bound | Within the three-stage model, a perfect reasoner drives p_R to exactly the encoding factor for the representation the factory was given; additional failure modes can only lower it — so capability purchases cannot discharge an obligation the organization never externalized. -->
The limit case makes the asymmetry exact. Write \(M\) for the reasoning model. Within the three-stage
model, for a fixed representation \(R\), as \(M\) approaches a perfect reasoner and the interpretation
and realization factors approach one,

<!-- eq: \lim_{M \to \text{perfect reasoner}} p_R = P(E \mid R) -->

Additional failure modes can only lower the overall probability of satisfactory realization. Even perfect
interpretation and realization remain bounded by whether the engineering intent was correctly encoded
in the representation available to the factory. The bound restates this book's account of engineering
responsibility in economic terms:
engineers can delegate increasing amounts of realization while remaining responsible for the
result, and an organization that has not determined and externalized the obligations the work must
satisfy cannot discharge that responsibility by purchasing a more capable reasoner.

<!-- point: perfect-fabrication-exposes-the-upstream-problem | Perfect fabrication does not eliminate the engineering problem upstream of fabrication — it exposes it: a stronger reasoner can reconstruct much, but cannot reliably recover consequential knowledge that never entered the factory. -->
Perfect fabrication does not eliminate the engineering problem upstream of fabrication. It
exposes it, quickly. A stronger reasoner can compensate for ambiguity by doing more inference: it can
recover conventions, inspect surrounding artifacts, search for analogous cases, test hypotheses,
and spend additional computation reconstructing what the engineer probably intended. Those
capabilities raise the interpretation and realization factors. They cannot reliably recover
consequential knowledge that never entered the factory.

<!-- point: fast-exposure-is-not-necessarily-a-defect | Fast exposure is not necessarily a defect: Agile exploits rapid fabrication for earlier feedback, and the danger depends on whether incorrect upstream assumptions are exposed by that feedback or survive it as latent failures — the coupling the dynamic model later makes explicit. -->
This is not necessarily a defect. Agile practice exploits rapid fabrication precisely because working
realizations can produce feedback sooner. The danger depends on whether incorrect upstream assumptions
are exposed by that feedback or survive it as latent failures that surface only in production.
Feedback of that kind does not contradict the bound. It exploits the coupling the dynamic model later
in this section makes explicit: each realization changes the engineering environment the next one
inherits.

<!-- point: the-bound-limits-process-design-and-exposes-a-tolerance-limit | Read in factory terms the bound limits process design — it cannot make an incorrectly encoded obligation correct — and tolerance exposes a different limit: with a property represented explicitly and independently evaluated, better fabrication drives interpretation and realization error down while the remaining error approaches the probability that the engineering representation itself is wrong. | terms: tolerance -->
Read in factory terms, the bound limits process design. As agent capability improves, process
design can raise the probability that a fabricator correctly interprets and realizes engineering
intent. It cannot make an incorrectly encoded obligation correct. Tolerance therefore exposes a
different limit. Where a consequential property is represented explicitly and evaluated
independently, improvements in fabrication drive interpretation and realization error downward
while the remaining error approaches the probability that the engineering representation itself is
wrong.

<!-- point: the-bound-does-not-argue-for-complete-specification | The bound does not argue for complete specification — that would recreate the earlier software factories' economics; the new possibility is realization freedom with the smaller set of consequential decisions explicitly preserved. -->
Nor does the bound argue for complete specification. Determining nearly every consequential and
inconsequential realization decision in advance would recreate much of the economic problem that
defeated the earlier software factories ({{chapter:software-factories}}). The new economic possibility is
different: a capable fabricator can be given enormous freedom over realization while the
engineering environment explicitly preserves the smaller set of decisions whose resolution
matters.

<!-- point: carrying-cost-completes-the-comparison | With carrying cost explicit, a representation can be economically preferable even when it is more expensive to construct and each realization through it is more expensive — and the carrying term covers every production structure a factory keeps useful, not only a formal model. -->
**Carrying cost completes the comparison.** The inequality above compared realization costs alone;
a representation must also be constructed and maintained. The total cost of holding a representation
and realizing through it is

<!-- eq: C_{\text{total}}(R) = C_{\text{carry}}(R) + \frac{c_R}{P(E \mid R)\, P(I \mid E, R)\, P(L \mid I, E, R)} -->

\(C_{\text{carry}}\) is broader than the cost of maintaining a formal model.
{{chapter:software-factories}} showed factories carrying engineering knowledge in models,
instructions, policies, skills, tests, generated configuration, context systems, and other production
structures. These structures differ in how much judgment they preserve and how strongly they govern
realization, but all incur some cost to remain useful as the software and its environment change.

An explicit model typically raises this carrying cost. It can still be economical even when it is
expensive to construct and each realization through it is individually more expensive: the model need
only reduce expected realization, reconstruction, failure, or assurance cost enough over its useful
life. Natural language is therefore a legitimate point in the design space, not a defect to be
engineered away; for many tasks its carrying cost is extremely low.

<!-- point: capacity-moves-both-sides-of-the-investment | Commodity intelligence changes both sides of the investment: greater implementation capacity can justify richer engineering structure while reducing the cost of carrying it, so the carrying term is not a fixed parameter of the environment. -->
Commodity intelligence changes both sides of this investment. Greater implementation capacity can
justify richer engineering structure, while the same capacity can reduce the cost of carrying that
structure. The carrying term is therefore not a fixed parameter of the environment: the same capacity
that raises the volume of realization the environment must govern also helps construct and maintain
the models, checks, and correspondence machinery that govern it.

<!-- point: expensive-reasoners-are-a-prediction-of-the-theory | Reasoning capability can substitute for representation: one arrangement repeatedly buys interpretation at inference time, another pays carrying cost so a cheaper reasoner suffices — which makes the vendor factory a theoretical object rather than a descriptive category. -->
The factorization also shows where reasoning capability can substitute for representation. When
natural language leaves a difficult interpretation problem, one response is a durable representation;
another is a more capable reasoner, more inference, richer context, or additional supervisory
reasoning. Those interventions may raise \(P(I \mid E, R)\) and \(P(L \mid I, E, R)\), but their cost is
incurred again as production continues. For an inexpensive reasoner \(M_s\) and a frontier reasoner
\(M_f\) over the same natural-language representation \(R_{\text{NL}}\), it may well be that

<!-- eq: P(I \mid R_{\text{NL}}, M_f) \gg P(I \mid R_{\text{NL}}, M_s) -->

A factory can compensate for weak explicit representation by spending more on reasoning capability.
One arrangement repeatedly buys interpretation at inference time; another pays carrying cost for a
representation that lets a cheaper reasoner suffice. Keeping consequential properties in
natural-language inference therefore makes reliability a recurring purchase.
The factory a vendor sells — capable fabricator, generic supervisory machinery, none of your
consequential intent ({{chapter:software-factories}}) — thereby becomes a theoretically interesting
object rather than a descriptive category: how much engineering structure can commodity reasoning
capability economically replace?

<!-- point: not-every-recurring-judgment-should-become-a-model | The substitution argument does not imply that every recurring judgment should become a model; the comparison depends on recurrence, carrying cost, inference cost, consequence, and how much the representation actually improves production or assurance. -->
This does not imply that every recurring judgment should become a model. The comparison depends on
recurrence, carrying cost, inference cost, consequence, and how much the representation actually
improves production or assurance.

<!-- point: repeated-use-amortizes-judgment | Under a simple linear maintenance model — build once, carry repeatedly, realize repeatedly — the fixed representation cost amortizes across n realizations and the judgment embodied in the representation is reused; the factories accumulating shared policy, models, and permissions at scale are amortizing judgment, not adding process. -->
**Repeated work changes the economics again.** For a single task, writing a structured model may
be extravagant. If the same representation supports \(n\) realizations, then, under a simple linear
maintenance model that splits the carrying cost into its fixed and recurring parts,

<!-- eq: C_n(R) = C_{\text{build}}(R) + n \cdot C_{\text{maintain}}(R) + n \cdot \bar C_{\text{realize}}(R) -->

Build once, carry repeatedly, realize repeatedly. The fixed cost amortizes across \(n\), while the
judgment embodied in the representation is reused: one policy can shape thousands of changes; one
learned constraint can protect every later task. The factories that accumulate shared policy, models,
context, and permissions as scale rises are not merely adding process. They are **amortizing judgment**.

<!-- point: the-decomposition-explains-factory-variation | The factories differ in where they carry engineering knowledge; the decomposition explains why those arrangements can coexist, and the relevant question is what probability and cost of satisfactory realization an arrangement produces and what knowledge survives for the next task. -->
{{sec:mage-in-the-wild}} showed factories making different choices about where engineering
knowledge lives and what their agents must reconstruct. The decomposition explains why those
arrangements can coexist: a factory may spend on explicit representation, on reasoner capability,
on human supervision, or on some combination, and each purchase moves different factors. The
relevant question is not whether a factory uses models in the abstract, but what probability and
cost of satisfactory realization its arrangement produces — and what engineering knowledge
survives for the next task.

<!-- point: a-sound-gate-blocks-covered-failure-regardless-of-producer | A is the machinery by which a selected tolerance becomes consequential, and a sound admission gate makes covered failure impossible to admit regardless of how the producer reasoned. | terms: tolerance -->
Now let \(A\) denote an independent admission mechanism. In the factory vocabulary of
{{chapter:software-factories}}, \(A\) is part of the machinery by which a selected tolerance becomes
consequential: evidence establishes whether the realization remains within the represented acceptable
region, and admission determines what happens when it does not. In the strongest region of Alignment,
suppose \(A\) is a sound deterministic predicate for the covered obligation: an unacceptable candidate
cannot pass. Then

<!-- eq: P(\text{covered failure admitted}\mid A)=0 -->

<!-- point: the-guarantee-is-independent-of-the-producers-reasoning | Alignment makes consequence independent of whether the producing reasoner got the covered judgment right. -->
That guarantee does not require the producer to reason well about the obligation. The agent can be shown no
structural model at all, repeatedly propose forbidden dependencies, and still be prevented from merging
them. Alignment can make consequence independent of whether the producing reasoner got the
covered judgment right.

<!-- point: assurance-without-modeling-can-be-expensive | Assurance without useful representation is expensive: if each independently generated candidate satisfies the covered obligation with probability p_R and rejected candidates are regenerated until one passes, the expected admission cost carries both production and validation cost on every attempt. -->
But assurance without useful representation can be expensive. If each independently generated candidate
satisfies the covered obligation with probability \(p_R\), each attempt costs \(c_R\) to produce and
\(c_A\) to validate, and rejected candidates are regenerated until one passes, then

<!-- eq: \bar C_{\text{admit}}(R,A) = \frac{c_R + c_A}{p_R} -->

<!-- point: same-guarantee-different-cost-over-different-surfaces | The same admission guarantee over two reasoning surfaces produces substantially different realization costs. -->
Alignment can therefore provide the same admission guarantee over two reasoning surfaces at very
different realization costs.

<!-- point: hiding-the-model-does-not-weaken-the-gate-only-raises-cost | Hiding the model does not weaken a perfect gate; it only raises the cost of reaching an acceptable candidate. -->
Suppose the architectural rule is checked perfectly at merge time. Hiding the structural model from the
agent does not weaken the gate: forbidden changes still cannot merge. But the agent may repeatedly
reconstruct the architecture incorrectly and collide with that gate. Exposing the structural model leaves
the guarantee unchanged while reducing the cost of reaching an admissible candidate.

<!-- point: alignment-protects-the-boundary-modeling-reduces-collisions | Alignment protects the boundary; Modeling reduces how often work collides with it. -->
**Alignment protects the boundary; Modeling reduces how often work collides with it.**

<!-- point: modeling-acts-before-admission-alignment-at-it | Modeling can act at several stages before admission — stating intent, recovering it, easing realization — while Alignment acts afterward or alongside realization by determining whether covered failures can acquire consequence. -->
The division can be stated by stage. Modeling can act at several points before admission: helping
engineers state consequential intent, helping reasoners recover it, and making satisfactory
realization easier. Alignment acts afterward or alongside realization, by determining whether a
covered failure can acquire consequence.

The four combinations therefore have different engineering meanings ([ref:modeling-alignment-quadrants]).

<!-- label: modeling-alignment-quadrants -->
<!-- table: *Modeling and Alignment are separable levers.* Modeling changes the economics and tractability of reasoning; Alignment changes whether the result can acquire consequence. The cells describe their primary contributions, not an exhaustive account. [short: The four Modeling × Alignment combinations] -->
| Modeling ↓ / Alignment → | No Alignment | Alignment |
|---|---|---|
| **No Modeling** | Repeated reasoning from a poor surface; residual failure can escape | Covered failure cannot escape, but rejection and regeneration may be expensive |
| **Modeling** | Satisfactory realization becomes cheaper or more likely, but residual failure can still escape | Satisfactory realization becomes cheaper while covered failure remains independently blocked |

<!-- point: modeling-changes-economics-alignment-changes-enforcement | Their primary contributions are separable — Modeling and Alignment are different levers. -->
The table is schematic rather than exhaustive. Modeling may itself improve reliability, and Alignment
mechanisms may be statistical rather than deterministic. The point is that their primary contributions are
separable.

### Software engineering changes the problem

<!-- point: simple-models-isolated-repeated-exposure-under-fixed-conditions | The simple models deliberately isolated repeated probabilistic exposure under fixed conditions. -->
The analysis so far treats successive realizations as independent and holds attempt probability and cost
fixed. This isolates the effects of exposure, repetition, and retry.

<!-- point: se-needs-a-model-where-each-realization-changes-the-next | Software engineering needs a different model: each realization becomes structure inherited by the next. -->
Software engineering requires a different model because one realization changes the conditions
inherited by the next. Winters et al. characterize software engineering as "programming integrated over
time": software is developed, modified, and maintained as both the system and its environment
change.[cite: winters2020] One realization therefore becomes part of the architecture, conventions,
representations, tests, and other structure inherited by subsequent work. A good change can make later
work easier to reason about; a locally acceptable but structurally poor change can make it harder.

<!-- point: creation-and-change-share-one-factorization | The factorization describes one realization whether it creates a system or changes one; change differs because it inherits architecture, representations, conventions, obligations, tests, and capital from earlier work. -->
**Creation and change.** A change inherits architecture, representations, conventions, obligations,
tests, and engineering capital produced by earlier work.

<!-- point: independence-omits-the-inherited-environment | Independence omits the inherited environment; success at each step depends on the environment carried into it. -->
Let the probability of satisfactory realization at step \(i\) be

<!-- eq: p_i = p(T_i, M, R_i, H, \mathcal{G}_i) -->

where \(\mathcal{G}_i\) is the governed engineering environment inherited at that step. The resulting realization \(a_i\) then
helps determine the environment available to subsequent work:

<!-- eq: \mathcal{G}_{i+1} = f(\mathcal{G}_i, a_i) -->

<!-- point: creation-is-the-boundary-case-of-change | Creation is realization from an initial environment, change is realization given an inherited one, and each realization determines the environment later work inherits — so the factory produces a sequence of changes, not independent realizations. -->
- **Creation** — satisfactory realization from an initial engineering environment \(\mathcal{G}_0\).
- **Change** — satisfactory realization \(a_i\) given the inherited environment \(\mathcal{G}_i\).
- **Inheritance** — realization \(a_i\) changes the environment available to later work,
  \(\mathcal{G}_{i+1} = f(\mathcal{G}_i, a_i)\).

The factory therefore produces a sequence of changes, not independent realizations.

<!-- point: coupling-creates-path-dependence-decay-self-reinforcing | The coupling creates path dependence: poor structure makes good realization harder, and decay can become self-reinforcing. -->
This coupling creates path dependence. A locally acceptable change can increase architectural irregularity,
obscure structure, or introduce inconsistent conventions, leaving later agents a worse reasoning surface. A
change that improves representations, architecture, constraints, or other engineering capital can instead
make later work easier to reason about and govern.

<!-- point: independent-vs-dynamic-model-answer-different-questions | The independent model isolates repeated exposure; the dynamic model preserves the coupling between realizations and environments. -->
The independent model and the dynamic model therefore answer different questions: the first isolates
repeated probabilistic exposure under fixed conditions, the second preserves the relationship between
successive realizations and the environments they create. These linked trajectories are the mathematical
counterpart of the feedback loops in [ref:mage-dynamic-model].

### Modeling Can Enable Alignment

<!-- point: modeling-can-turn-a-semantic-obligation-into-a-checkable-one | Modeling can turn a semantic obligation into a checkable one by changing the object over which it is decided. -->
The two principles interact in a second way. Some obligations can already be checked mechanically, and
Alignment can govern them directly. Others are semantic only because the relevant state must be
reconstructed from lower-level implementation. Suppose the architecture requires every route to a
remediation service to cross quota authorization: reasoning from implementation means reconstructing routes
from handlers, middleware, configuration, and dependency injection, but representing the permitted routes
explicitly turns the obligation into a graph question—does every allowed path cross the quota boundary? The
property did not become less consequential; Modeling changed the object over which it is decided, turning a
repeated semantic judgment into a checkable property.

<!-- point: two-kinds-of-hard-judgmental-vs-wrong-representation | Two kinds of hard question look alike in code: intrinsically judgmental, or buried in the wrong representation. -->
Some engineering questions are hard because the answer requires judgment. Others are hard because the
information needed to answer them is buried in the wrong representation. Both look like reasoning problems
when viewed from source code, but their engineering possibilities differ.

Better representation can support a genuinely judgmental decision without eliminating the judgment. But
when an obligation becomes mechanically decidable once the relevant structure is explicit, Modeling moves
that decision from repeated inference into repeatable machinery.

<!-- point: determinization-frontier-defined | The determinization frontier separates judgment supplied per instance from judgment the environment can carry repeatably — in factory terms, an economic boundary between judgments still bought probabilistically through the production process and properties made durable as tolerances. | terms: tolerance -->
The **determinization frontier** separates judgments that must be supplied per instance from judgments the
environment can carry repeatably. Alignment can mechanize an obligation that is already decidable.
Modeling can move the frontier by changing the representation until a previously reconstructed property
becomes decidable ([ref:determinization-frontier]). Read in factory terms, that separation is partly
economic. On one side sit the judgments a factory continues to buy probabilistically through its
production process; on the other, the properties it has chosen to represent explicitly and carry as
durable tolerances.

<!-- point: docable-default-everything-deterministic-was | DocAble's default: everything that could be deterministic was; commodity intelligence only where the problem requires semantic judgment. -->
My default in DocAble was simple: everything that could be deterministic was. I would rather have a
deterministic result I can rely on than a probabilistic result where the problem does not require judgment.
Heading structure, file metadata, schema validity, and similar properties can be checked directly. Writing
useful alternative text cannot. The determinization frontier separates those cases: use commodity
intelligence where semantic judgment is required; use deterministic machinery where it is not.

<!-- point: moving-the-frontier-reduces-the-probabilistic-surface | Moving the frontier reduces the probabilistic surface without making the agent deterministic or removing realization freedom. -->
Moving the frontier reduces the probabilistic surface: fewer consequential judgments depend on the agent
getting them right. It does not make the agent deterministic, and it does not require eliminating
implementation freedom. The environment can tightly govern what must be true while leaving the agent free to
choose among many acceptable realizations.

<!-- label: determinization-frontier -->
<!-- figure: assets/determinization-frontier.svg | *The Determinization Frontier.* Alignment can bind a property that is already decidable. Modeling can change the representation so that a property previously requiring semantic reconstruction becomes decidable and can then be bound by Alignment. Both routes move repeated judgment into machinery. -->

<!-- point: the-frontier-moves-incrementally | The frontier moves incrementally: from prose to invariant to bounded-model checking to temporal specification. -->
The frontier can move incrementally: from prose judgment to an explicit invariant; from an invariant to
exhaustive checking over a bounded state model; or, for temporal claims, to a specification over
executions.

<!-- point: at-the-limit-realization-becomes-transformation | At the limit a complete realization model makes realization a transformation, connecting MAGE to model-based engineering. -->
At the limit, a sufficiently complete realization model leaves little consequential freedom: realization
becomes a transformation problem, and deterministic generation may be preferable to an agent. This limiting
case connects MAGE to conventional model-based engineering; {{sec:software-rejoins-engineering}} returns to the relationship. Most
software systems occupy a different point in the design space. Engineering specifies what must be true; many
implementation choices remain acceptable. Commodity intelligence makes it economical to let autonomous
realization choose among them.

### The joint engineering problem

Modeling can make satisfactory realization easier to find. Alignment can prevent selected failures from
acquiring consequence. Better environments can preserve useful judgment for later work, but every
representation and mechanism costs something to build, maintain, reconcile, and carry.

<!-- point: objective-is-an-economical-division-of-labor | The objective is an economical division of labor among reasoner, environment, and remaining human judgment. -->
The objective is not to maximize Modeling or Alignment, nor to minimize the probabilistic
surface at any cost. It is to engineer an economical division of labor among the reasoner, the environment,
and the human judgment that remains.

<!-- point: engineer-jointly-chooses-representation-and-alignment | An engineer jointly chooses a representation and an Alignment strategy, trading carrying cost against realization and failure cost. -->
For a task and its obligations, an engineer chooses both a representation \(R\) and an Alignment strategy
\(A\):

<!-- eq: (R^*,A^*) = \arg\min_{R,A} \left[ C_{\text{carry}}(R,A) + E[C_{\text{realize}}\mid R,A] + E[L_{\text{failure}}\mid R,A] \right] -->

<!-- point: schematic-both-levers-carry-construction-and-carrying-cost | Schematically: Modeling changes realization cost, Alignment changes failure consequence, and both impose carrying cost — and the minimization prices the factory's standing choice between buying inference quality repeatedly and making a property durable as a tolerance. | terms: tolerance -->
The expression is schematic. Modeling principally changes the cost and difficulty of
satisfactory realization; Alignment principally changes the probability and consequence of unacceptable
realization; both impose construction and carrying costs. This is where the factory's standing choice
gets priced: the realization term absorbs inference quality bought again on every change, and the
carrying term absorbs the structure that makes a property durable as a tolerance instead. In
high-assurance settings the problem may instead be stated as minimizing total cost subject to an
assurance requirement,

<!-- eq: P(\text{covered failure escapes}\mid R,A)\le \varepsilon -->

For an adequately implemented deterministic gate over a decidable obligation, \(\varepsilon\) can be zero for
that covered property even while realization remains probabilistic.

## The Factory's Value Proposition

<!-- section-terms: engineering-capital -->

<!-- point: a-factory-is-an-investment-in-productive-capacity | A factory is an investment in productive capacity, so its economics depend on the value and marginal cost of the changes it produces over time, and for software those recurring products are transformations of a maintained system rather than copies. -->
A factory is an investment in productive capacity. Its economics therefore depend not only on startup
cost, but on the value and marginal cost of the changes it produces over time. For software, those
recurring products are not copies but transformations of a maintained system: features, repairs,
adaptations, and migrations.

<!-- point: separate-construction-from-continued-production | The cost model separates factory construction from continued production: startup cost, a stream of change costs, carrying cost, and expected failure loss. -->
We can therefore separate factory construction from continued production. For a factory that has produced
\(n\) changes,

<!-- eq: C_{\text{factory}}(n) = C_{\text{startup}} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_{\text{change},i} + C_{\text{carry}}(n) + E[L_{\text{failure}}(n)] -->

The cost of a change includes the human attention required to direct, coordinate, review, diagnose, and
intervene; the machine intelligence used for realization and reasoning; computational infrastructure;
validation and rework; and any additional engineering structure created in producing the change.
\(C_{\text{startup}}\) captures the initial investment required to establish the factory, and
\(C_{\text{carry}}\) the cost of keeping its productive capacity usable. The pair \((R,A)\) enters this
expression on both sides: representations, controls, and evidence raise \(C_{\text{startup}}\) and
\(C_{\text{carry}}\) while potentially lowering each \(C_{\text{change}}\) and the expected failure loss.

<!-- point: the-marginal-question-matters-most | The marginal question matters most: a successful factory may be expensive to construct and economical to use, because the productive capacity makes the stream of subsequent changes cheaper, faster, safer, or possible at all. -->
The corresponding marginal question is especially important:

<!-- eq: MC_{\text{change},n+1} = C_{\text{factory}}(n+1) - C_{\text{factory}}(n) -->

A successful factory may be expensive to construct and economical to use. The investment is justified not
because its first product was inexpensive, but because the resulting productive capacity makes a
sufficiently valuable stream of subsequent changes cheaper, faster, safer, or possible at all.

### Two Margins, Not One

<!-- point: software-has-two-margins | Software has two distinct margins, a major difference from manufacturing: the production margin (the next acceptable change) and the replication margin (serving that change's benefit to the next user). -->
Software adds a distinction manufacturing does not have, and it should be stated explicitly: there are two
margins here, not one. The *production margin* asks what it costs the factory to make the next acceptable
change to the software. The *replication margin* asks what it costs, once that change exists, to provide its
benefit to the next user, document, transaction, installation, or copy. For packaged software the second can
approach zero. For a hosted service it is not literally zero — compute, storage, and support scale with
use — but it can remain small relative to the engineering cost of changing the product.

### The Idle Factory Still Depreciates

<!-- point: carrying-cost-splits-into-operational-and-capital | Carrying cost splits into operational carry and capital maintenance: a software factory can be cheap to leave physically idle while its engineering capital continues to depreciate. -->
Carrying cost also divides, and the division is where the physical analogy breaks most instructively. A
semiconductor fabrication plant sitting idle is still enormously expensive: buildings, equipment, utilities,
and staffing continue whether or not anything is produced. An agentic software factory can idle at almost no
physical cost. Repositories, models, configuration, tests, and governance mechanisms sit on disk; cloud
services scale toward zero; agent subscriptions stop.

<!-- eq: C_{\text{carry}} = C_{\text{operational carry}} + C_{\text{capital maintenance}} -->

The first term can be extraordinarily low for software. The second need not be: dependencies move, models
cease to correspond to the system, tests go stale, and organizational knowledge disappears. Software
factories can be cheap to leave physically idle while their engineering capital continues to depreciate.

### What the Changes Are Worth

<!-- point: what-a-change-is-worth-is-a-model-of-value | What the stream of changes is worth cannot be specified universally: each factory's outcome quantities are themselves models of value chosen for a particular engineering or organizational decision. -->
Against these costs stands what the stream of changes is worth, and that side of the comparison cannot
be specified universally. A product factory may value
capabilities delivered to customers; a security factory may value risk retired; an accessibility factory may
value documents made accessible and remediation effort avoided. These quantities are themselves models of
value chosen for a particular engineering or organizational decision — the same claim {{chapter:modeling}}
made about metrics generally. The theory does not define what an outcome is worth. It asks whether the
outcomes produced justify the full cost of the production arrangement.

<!-- point: mage-concerns-the-first-arrow | The economic chain runs factory → software capability → use → value, and MAGE primarily concerns the first arrow; the organization's business model determines how capability becomes value. -->
The factory produces changes to the software; the software produces outcomes for its users. A company may
spend substantially to produce one change and then distribute its benefit across millions of transactions at
comparatively small incremental cost, so factory productivity and product value meet at a boundary rather
than being the same quantity:

<!-- eq: \text{factory} \rightarrow \text{software capability} \rightarrow \text{use} \rightarrow \text{value} -->

MAGE primarily concerns the economics of the first arrow. The organization's business model determines how
the resulting capability becomes value.

### Correctness Is Not the Factory's Objective

<!-- point: the-probability-model-explains-the-factory-economics | The probability model explains the terms of the lifecycle factory economics: higher p_R raises durable throughput and lowers the cost of a change; Alignment exchanges validation cost for reduced escape risk; models preserve reasoning as engineering capital. -->
Correctness, however, is still not the factory's final objective. It is one of the mechanisms
through which production becomes valuable. The lifecycle expression above prices a factory as a
startup investment plus a stream of change costs, a carrying cost, and an expected failure loss.
The probability model explains those terms from the inside. Higher \(p_R\) increases durable
throughput, because more attempts satisfy their obligations, and lowers the cost of a change by
avoiding failed attempts, repeated inference, rework, intervention, and recovery. Alignment
exchanges validation cost for reduced escape risk — spending inside the change term to shrink the
expected failure loss. Models and mechanisms preserve successful reasoning as engineering capital
rather than requiring the factory to purchase it again on every realization.

<!-- point: correctness-alone-does-not-establish-value | Correctness alone does not establish value: a factory can reliably and efficiently produce software that nobody needs. -->
But correctness alone does not establish value. **A factory can reliably and efficiently produce
software that nobody needs.** The probability model explains how engineering intent survives
fabrication; whether successfully realizing that intent produces outcomes worth their full cost
is the value question the factory economics ask, and no correctness result answers it.

<!-- point: the-economic-interpretation-of-modeling-and-alignment | The economic interpretation of Modeling and Alignment: valuable where the cost of externalizing, preserving, and enforcing consequential knowledge is lower than the expected cost of repeatedly reconstructing it, incorrectly realizing it, or allowing its violation to escape. -->
This provides the larger economic interpretation of Modeling and Alignment. They are not valuable
because explicitness is intrinsically desirable, nor because every decision should be mechanized.
They are valuable where the cost of externalizing, preserving, and enforcing consequential
knowledge is lower than the expected cost of repeatedly reconstructing it, incorrectly realizing
it, or allowing its violation to escape.

<!-- point: the-problem-moves-as-fabrication-approaches-perfection | Commodity intelligence changes the terms of the comparison — several quantities move at once: the engineering problem does not disappear as fabrication approaches perfection, it moves toward deciding what must be preserved, inferred, established before admission, and what the capability is worth. -->
Commodity intelligence changes the terms of that comparison. As reasoning becomes cheaper and more
capable, more decisions can economically remain with the fabricator; at the same time, greater
realization capacity increases the amount of work that can cross a weakly enforced boundary
before a human can inspect it. The engineering problem therefore does not disappear as
fabrication approaches perfection. It moves toward deciding what must be preserved, what may
safely be inferred, what must be established before admission, and what the resulting production
capability is worth.

<!-- point: part-v-evidence-is-depth-and-variation-not-effect-sizes | Chapter 5 supplied depth and variation, not population effect sizes; the theory is the proposed explanation of it. -->
{{chapter:software-factories}} supplied two kinds of evidence: longitudinal depth from the originating case and comparative
variation from independent industrial reconstructions. Neither supplies population-level effect sizes or
causal estimates. The theory above is the proposed explanation of those observations. {{sec:what-the-theory-predicts}} states
predictions that require stronger external tests; {{sec:scope-conditions}} asks where they should be expected to hold.
