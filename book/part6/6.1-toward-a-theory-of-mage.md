# From Production to Cumulative Progress

<!-- point: the-pieces-this-chapter-assembles | Chapter 5 examined the complete software factory; the theory focuses on its GEE and how reusable engineering structure supports the encoding and realization of intent. -->

<!-- print-columns-start: theory-perspectives -->

Producing more software changes is not necessarily progress. The changes must do what engineers intended, be worth the cost of producing them, and leave the system in a condition that supports further work.

These are three different questions. We will examine each in turn, beginning with what it means for an agent to produce the software an engineer actually wanted ([ref:theory-perspectives]).

| **Economical production.** What did it cost to produce and accept the result? |
| **Cumulative progress.** Did today’s work make subsequent engineering easier or harder? |

## Did We Build What We Intended? {#encoding-and-realization}

<!-- point: the-starting-point-is-the-master-equation | The encoding–realization decomposition applies the chain rule to distinguish adequate encoding from fulfillment, before extending the account to delivery cost and accumulated structure. -->

Let us begin with a single engineering task that an engineer chooses to delegate to an agent. The engineer must express the consequential obligations, and the agent must interpret them and produce an acceptable result. Either can fail. The representation may omit or misstate what matters, or express it in a form the agent cannot use effectively. Even with an adequate representation, the agent may misunderstand an obligation or fail to implement it.

We can model these sources of failure. Let \(E\) mean that the encoding captures the consequential engineering intent and \(F\) that the resulting artifact fulfills the encoded obligations. Let \(R\) denote the representation used to communicate that intent, such as a natural-language prompt, an engineering model, or a collection of requirements and design documents. The probability of both events is

<!-- eq: p_R = P(E \cap F \mid R) = \underbrace{P(E \mid R)}_{\substack{\text{Encoding: engineers state}\\\text{what must hold}}} \;\underbrace{P(F \mid E,R)}_{\substack{\text{Realization: agents fulfill}\\\text{those obligations}}} -->

The **encoding–realization decomposition** separates two questions: whether the engineer has adequately expressed the intended obligations, and whether the agent produces an artifact satisfying them.[^encoding-realization-chain-rule] The joint event \(E\cap F\) represents faithful realization of adequately encoded intent. Fulfillment of the encoding alone does not establish that the engineer expressed the right obligations. We write \(p_R\) as a shorthand for this joint probability; \(P(\cdot)\) denotes probability in the equation. Realization includes interpreting the representation, planning, implementing, checking, and correcting the result. An agent may initially misunderstand an obligation and later repair its work; it may also infer an obligation that the encoding omitted. Consequently, faithful production through the representation and observed task correctness are not identical.

[ref:probability-in-the-software-factory] places this decomposition inside the software factory introduced in [ref:process-design-and-tolerance]. The earlier diagram distinguished process design from tolerances. Here, the process is divided into encoding and realization, and the encoded property defines the region of acceptable results. Independent evidence helps establish whether a realization falls within that region. The figure therefore connects the probability model to the factory’s production and admission decisions. Its probability labels omit the conditioning on \(R\) for legibility.

##### What Affects the Two Probabilities?

<!-- point: the-encoding-term-is-also-a-human-channel | A representation can improve P(E | R) by helping the engineer express intent correctly even if conditional realization is unchanged; Modeling improves the human channel as well as the agent channel. -->

The first term is easy to overlook in an agentic setting. A structured model may help a machine interpret intent more reliably than prose, and it can also help the engineer express the intended property correctly in the first place. Different representations make different distinctions visible, force different decisions to be made explicitly, and make omissions easier or harder to notice. A representation can therefore improve \(P(E \mid R)\) even if it leaves \(P(F \mid E,R)\) unchanged. This is one reason Modeling is an engineering activity rather than merely a technique for supplying context to an agent.

These probabilities also depend on the work and the engineering arrangement in which the representation is used. A dependency model that helps an agent modify a familiar service may be inadequate for a new architectural design. The same representation may produce different results with different agents, tools, or evaluation procedures. Comparing representations therefore requires attention to the tasks and conditions under which they are used, rather than treating the probability of success as an intrinsic property of the representation.

<!-- point: capable-agents-can-raise-the-encoding-term-through-feedback | Feedback from a failed realization can help diagnose an encoding problem; capable agents may improve encoding indirectly when that diagnosis supports revising intent. -->

Agents can also help engineers improve an encoding. When an implementation fails to express the intended behavior, the discrepancy may reveal an omitted obligation or an ambiguity in the representation. The engineer must determine whether the failure arose from the encoding or from the agent’s realization. This is one use of Modeling during and after implementation ([ref:4.1-the-dynamics-of-mage:heading:greenfield-and-brownfield-mage]): implementation and evaluation can expose what the engineer has not yet made explicit.

<!-- point: the-two-factory-techniques-act-on-different-factors | Process design changes the conditions and cost of realization. Explicit tolerances help encode intent and make selected properties available for independent evaluation. | terms: tolerance -->

Process design changes the conditions under which agents realize intent: context, tools, decomposition, routing, permissions, and other features of the harness can change \(P(F\mid E,R)\), its cost, or both. A better agent can improve the same term. Explicit tolerances help engineers state the intended property and make it available for independent evaluation. Tests, analysis, measurement, or human judgment can assess whether a realization falls within that tolerance. Where the evidence supports enforcement, a gate can reject work that violates the obligation. This separates helping production succeed from deciding whether its result should be admitted.

<!-- point: stronger-reasoners-can-recover-meaning | Better reasoners may reconstruct meaning from a weak representation, but some lost distinctions require a different representation or additional evidence. -->

A stronger reasoner can sometimes recover meaning that a weaker one misses. A terse requirement may be enough for an agent that knows the domain and can inspect the surrounding system. Better retrieval, tools, and feedback can also help it discover what the representation leaves implicit. These possibilities affect the cost and reliability of realization; they are reasons to compare richer representations with more capable inference rather than assume that either must win.

Implicit information differs from unavailable information. A reasoner may recover an unstated obligation from domain knowledge, surrounding code, or evidence it can obtain. But if two cases require different decisions and nothing available to the reasoner distinguishes them, further reasoning over the same information cannot determine which decision is appropriate. Progress then requires additional evidence and, where necessary, a revised representation that preserves the distinction.

<!-- point: reliable-realization-does-not-repair-wrong-encoding | With encoding quality fixed, perfectly reliable fulfillment leaves faithful production bounded by the encoding term; this does not imply complete specification. -->

Suppose the agent becomes increasingly reliable at fulfilling the encoded obligations. With the encoding held fixed, the probability of faithful production then approaches \(P(E\mid R)\). More reliable fulfillment does not correct errors in what we ask them to build. The practical consequence is to examine the encoding when better agents keep faithfully producing the wrong thing.

<!-- point: the-bound-does-not-argue-for-complete-specification | Useful freedom lets agents choose satisfactory realizations while engineers specify consequential obligations; complete specification is not the objective. -->

The appropriate degree of specification depends on the work. Some transformations leave little useful freedom, and the cost of ambiguity may outweigh any benefit from delegation. Elsewhere, specifying nearly every realization decision in advance recreates much of the specification cost discussed in {{chapter:software-factories}}. It also sacrifices a major advantage of commodity intelligence: agents can explore implementation choices that engineers need not make themselves. The engineer can specify consequential obligations, leave other choices open, and let the agent find a satisfactory realization. The economic value of Modeling and Alignment therefore depends partly on how much useful freedom the environment can preserve while maintaining adequate control.

## What Did It Cost? {#cost-and-failure-separately}

<!-- point: lifecycle-cost-counts-correct-delivery | Cost per correct admitted change counts construction, all attempts and upkeep over comparable requested work and a declared horizon. -->

Having considered whether an agent produces the intended result, we turn to what it costs. Software development has always required implementation, review, testing, integration, and repair, with different amounts of human effort and automation. Conventional development provides a baseline against which to evaluate agentic alternatives. The relevant comparison is not how much an agent costs to produce code, but how much each arrangement costs to deliver acceptable software.

Agentic development changes that accounting. Inference, subscriptions, orchestration, and computing infrastructure introduce expenditures of their own. Engineers may spend less time implementing and more time specifying work, examining evidence, diagnosing failures, or maintaining the machinery that governs agents. The required expertise, staffing, and distribution of work may also change. Some costs fall, others rise, and some are newly introduced. Whether the overall arrangement is economical depends on the work delivered, its quality, and the full cost of producing and maintaining it.

An agent may finish a change quickly, yet leave the engineer with hours of checking and repair. Another arrangement may take longer to prepare because it needs a model or a new check, but require fewer attempts. Comparing their costs means counting the preparation and failed attempts as well as the change we eventually accept. For \(N\) requested changes, let \(D_N\) count those independently verified as correct and admitted within a declared evaluation horizon. Each request may require several attempts. The total cost and cost per correct admitted change are

<!-- eq: C_N=C_{\text{build}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(C_{\text{run},i}+C_{\text{carry},i}\bigr), \qquad c_N=\frac{C_N}{D_N},\quad D_N>0. -->

Construction covers the initial investment in the engineering environment. Running costs include production, checking, human review, and repair across all attempts; carrying costs cover the upkeep needed to keep the environment useful. A failed or abandoned task still contributes its costs to the numerator. If nothing correct is delivered, report that result and the expenditure rather than assigning a cost per delivery. Shared costs need an explicit allocation, and human effort and machine expenditure need declared valuations before they can be added. Elapsed time remains a separate outcome.

<!-- point: cost-comparisons-retain-work-quality-and-horizon | Comparable requested work, quality and horizon prevent an attractive cost ratio from concealing rejected or unfinished work. -->

This accounting does not assume that tasks cost the same, that retries are independent, or that the environment stands still. It does require comparable work. An engineering process that accepts only easy changes may have an attractive \(c_N\) while leaving important requests unfinished. Comparisons should therefore hold the requested work and evaluation horizon fixed, report what was delivered and left unresolved, and measure escaped failures and false rejection alongside cost. Repeated attempts often change the specification, tools, or next plan; observed delivery costs can count that learning without pretending that every retry starts over under identical conditions.

##### The Cost of Production and Assurance.

<!-- point: assurance-without-modeling-can-be-expensive | Validation, false rejection, and repair consume running cost even when the admission mechanism’s protection stays unchanged. -->

Admission adds the cost of gathering evidence and acting on it. A gate can provide the same protection while one representation causes agents to collide with it much more often than another. Each rejected candidate may require further inference, diagnosis, or human attention. These costs belong in \(C_{\text{run},i}\), whether the rejection catches a real defect or mistakenly turns away correct work.

<!-- point: human-attention-belongs-in-lifecycle-cost | Human review and intervention consume engineering resources even when machine execution is inexpensive. -->

Human attention is part of this accounting. A production system that generates changes cheaply but requires an expert to reconstruct each one has moved work onto that expert. The relevant cost includes the time needed to interpret the evidence, diagnose failures, and decide whether the result is acceptable. > #### Inset — Human Attention Is Not a Free Control {#inset-human-attention-not-a-free-control}
>
> Software engineering has long relied on human review, but review is neither free nor perfectly reliable. Engineers who implement a change acquire context through the work itself. A reviewer must reconstruct enough of that context to judge the result, often without having participated in the decisions that produced it. Studies of code review document useful corrections and associations with post-release quality, but also show that larger reviews require more effort and yield a smaller proportion of useful feedback [cite: beller2014modern; mcintosh2014impact; bosu2015useful].
>
> Agentic development can widen this difference. An agent may produce a substantial change quickly, while the engineer responsible for accepting it must determine what the agent changed, why it made those decisions, and whether the result satisfies the intended obligations. The engineer’s effectiveness as an inspector need not match their effectiveness as an implementer, or their effectiveness when reviewing work produced through conventional collaboration. More output can therefore increase both the amount of review required and the difficulty of performing it well.
>
> This resembles the ironies of automation identified by Bainbridge [cite: bainbridge1983]. Automation can remove routine execution while leaving people responsible for detecting unusual failures and intervening when the system behaves unexpectedly. Humans are not particularly good at sustained vigilance, especially when the system usually behaves correctly. An engineer who has not participated in implementation may also lack the context needed to diagnose a rare failure quickly.
>
> MAGE offers an alternative to inspecting every implementation decision. Recurring obligations can be represented explicitly, checked independently, and enforced where the evidence permits. Human attention can then concentrate on uncertain evidence, novel failures, tradeoffs, and decisions requiring expert judgment. Whether this arrangement improves review quality and reduces total attention is an empirical question, examined in the proposed review experiment in [ref:6.4-research-agenda:heading:capacity-and-environment].

##### Maintaining or Reconstructing Knowledge.

<!-- point: carrying-cost-completes-the-comparison | A representation earns its construction and upkeep through useful work enabled or later costs avoided, compared with inference over less explicit knowledge. -->

Should we keep the knowledge explicit or pay to figure out the same thing again? Carrying cost is part of that comparison. The factories in {{chapter:software-factories}} preserve engineering knowledge in models, instructions, policies, skills, tests, and context systems. All require some effort to remain useful as the software changes. A structured model earns its construction and upkeep when it enables valuable work or reduces later reconstruction, realization, failure, or assurance costs enough over its useful life. For a simple or infrequent task, a natural-language description may already do the job at very little cost.

The alternative is often to buy inference when the question arises. A capable agent may reconstruct a dependency from source cheaply enough that a maintained graph would not repay its cost. For another task family, reconstructing that same dependency hundreds of times may be much more expensive than keeping it explicit. The comparison depends on the work, the available reasoner, the assurance required, and the expected reuse of the representation. A model can also repay its cost in a single use when the question is sufficiently consequential.

<!-- point: vendor-factory-tests-representation-versus-inference | A packaged agent system may substitute capable inference and generic machinery for some explicit structure; compare what it delivers, costs, and leaves for later work. -->

This gives packaged agent systems a place in the theory. A capable reasoner and generic supervisory machinery may economically replace some structure that a weaker reasoner would need. How much can they replace, and for which obligations? We can compare what those arrangements deliver, what escapes their assurance, and what they cost, while also asking what engineering knowledge survives for the next task.

<!-- point: accounting-compares-arrangements-but-choice-remains-open | Lifecycle accounting supports comparison; choosing reasoning, representation and assurance is a constrained engineering decision. -->

This accounting makes alternative engineering arrangements comparable, but it does not tell us which arrangement to choose. A stronger foundation model may reduce the need for explicit representations; a better representation may let a cheaper foundation model perform the same work. A gate may reduce escaped failures while increasing rejection and repair costs. The engineering problem is to choose among these arrangements under requirements for correct delivery, acceptable risk, and human attention. The cheapest implementation attempt need not be the cheapest way to deliver acceptable work.

## How Does Today’s Work Affect Tomorrow’s? {#the-dynamic-model}

<!-- point: se-needs-a-model-where-each-realization-changes-the-next | Models, controls and architectural changes affect the reliability and cost of later work. -->

The cost of one engineering change is not the whole story. A change can also affect how easily the next change can be made. Adding a dependency model may spare later agents from reconstructing the architecture; adding a validator may prevent a recurring defect; and correcting an architectural irregularity may make subsequent implementation more reliable. Conversely, a change that introduces inconsistency or obscures important relationships can make later work harder.

We can now extend the analysis beyond individual tasks. The probability model in [ref:6.1-toward-a-theory-of-mage:heading:encoding-and-realization] described whether one delegated task was faithfully realized, while [ref:6.1-toward-a-theory-of-mage:heading:cost-and-failure-separately] accounted for the cost of delivering acceptable work. In practice, the models, controls, and procedures available for one task can be changed by earlier engineering work. We therefore need to represent how the engineering environment evolves.

<!-- point: independence-omits-the-inherited-environment | Faithful production depends schematically on the task, foundation model, and available GEE; an episode can change that GEE without necessarily improving it. -->

Let \(T_i\) be the task undertaken at step \(i\), \(M\) the foundation model, and \(\mathcal{G}_i\) the governed engineering environment available at that step. The environment contains the representations, tools, procedures, and controls used to direct and evaluate work. We write \(p_i\) for the probability of faithful production on task \(T_i\). It is the same kind of probability introduced in [ref:6.1-toward-a-theory-of-mage:heading:encoding-and-realization], now evaluated for a particular task under the available model and engineering environment. The representation \(R\) is one part of \(\mathcal{G}_i\).

Let \(a_i\) describe the engineering work performed during the episode, including any changes to the environment itself. We can then write

<!-- eq: p_i=p(T_i,M,\mathcal{G}_i), \qquad \mathcal{G}_{i+1}=f(\mathcal{G}_i,a_i). -->

The first relation says that the likelihood of success depends on the task, the available reasoner, and the engineering environment. The second says that the work performed during an episode can change that environment. A failed implementation may still produce a useful model or control, while an accepted change may introduce complexity that makes later work harder.

For now, holding the foundation model fixed lets us examine the effects of changes to the GEE. The model can also change over longer periods. These relations describe dependencies worth investigating; they do not assign numerical effects or imply that every engineering episode improves the environment.

<!-- point: theory-of-mage-informal-statement | Agentic capacity and the available GEE jointly shape what the factory can accomplish; experience can change the GEE and affect later production. -->

Agentic capacity and the GEE jointly shape what a software factory can accomplish, but they play different roles. Work orders specify the intended changes; foundation-model capability, concurrency, and implementation speed determine the available agentic capacity. The GEE supplies the representations, procedures, evidence, and controls through which that capacity is directed and governed. [ref:mage-dynamic-model] relates these inputs to attempted work and realized performance, then shows how experience can change the environment available for later work.

<!-- point: diagnosis-and-authority-connect-pressure-to-adaptation | Corrective feedback requires structural diagnosis and authority to adapt the GEE; the resulting changes affect subsequent work. -->

The corrective feedback path begins when attempted work exposes recurring failures, escaped defects, repeated human intervention, or friction from existing controls. Engineers diagnose whether the problem is local to an implementation or reflects a deficiency in the GEE. DocAble’s duplicate-dispatch episode gives a concrete example ([ref:chapter:appendix-a-governed-delegation]). Repeated assignments motivated a completed-phase gate governing subsequent assignments. When the gate misidentified a phase and rejected legitimate work, its phase-identity extractor required correction. The episode follows the feedback path in [ref:mage-dynamic-model]: observed failure, diagnosis, adaptation of the GEE, and changed conditions for later work. Such adaptation may introduce a representation or stronger enforcement, reconcile conflicting mechanisms, or retire an obsolete control; improving the GEE’s fit can require removing machinery. When changes address the underlying cause, subsequent work can avoid repeating the failure. Without diagnosis and authority to revise the shared environment, the organization may instead repeat local repairs indefinitely.

##### What Makes the Environment Useful?

The dynamic model raises a practical question: which changes to the GEE actually improve subsequent engineering? Adding more models or controls is not necessarily beneficial. An environment may contain extensive machinery that no longer corresponds to the system, duplicates existing checks, or obstructs legitimate work.

Four properties help assess whether the GEE is suited to the work it supports:

1.  **Useful representations.** The information needed for the work is explicit at an appropriate level of abstraction.

2.  **Adequate coverage.** Important obligations have appropriate evidence and enforcement.

3.  **Coherence.** Models and mechanisms reinforce one another rather than contradicting or needlessly duplicating.

4.  **Economy.** Their future value justifies their maintenance, friction, and context cost.

Coverage does not mean mechanizing every obligation. Some properties admit deterministic checks; others require statistical evidence, semantic evaluation, or human judgment. A useful GEE makes those boundaries explicit.

<!-- point: not-a-maturity-model-improve-the-limiting-part | The properties are dimensions for diagnosing what limits the work, not maturity stages; the appropriate response may improve representations or assurance, or reconcile and retire controls. -->

These properties help us find out what is slowing the work down or allowing failures to escape. They identify different possible weaknesses, not stages of a maturity model [cite: bollinger1991critical]. Repeated reconstruction suggests a representation problem; escaped failures call for examining coverage and enforcement; and excessive control friction may justify reconciliation or retirement rather than additional machinery.

##### How Do We Measure Progress?

<!-- point: activity-counts-are-not-outcome | Easy-to-count quantities (commits, lines, control files, support ratio) measure activity or investment, not outcome. -->

The dynamic model distinguishes the engineering environment from the results it produces. That distinction matters when evaluating MAGE. Adding models, validators, or controls changes the environment, but their existence does not establish that engineering has improved. Likewise, additional agent invocations, commits, or lines of code show that work occurred, not that the resulting software is more useful or dependable.[^activity-data]

We therefore need measures of what the engineering process accomplishes. **Durable throughput** concerns useful changes that survive integration, rework, and operation. **Defect escape** records consequential failures that reach later lifecycle stages or production. **Human-attention burden** captures the engineering judgment required to achieve those results. [ref:mage-outcomes] identifies candidate observations for each.

These outcomes should be examined together. A factory may increase throughput by accepting more defects, or reduce defect escape by requiring extensive human inspection. It may also invest heavily in controls that produce little benefit for the work being performed. Comparing the outcomes against changes in the GEE helps distinguish increased implementation activity, stronger assurance, and genuine improvements in engineering productivity. The cost model tells us what acceptable delivery costs; these additional measures help establish whether the delivered work remains useful, what failures escape, and how much human judgment the process consumes.

None of these descriptions supplies a measured effect size. Together they support a more focused question: under what conditions do representations, independent enforcement, and accumulated engineering knowledge turn additional agentic capacity into useful progress? The next section develops four propositions that evidence could challenge.

[^encoding-realization-chain-rule]: In general, the probability that two events \(A\) and \(B\) both occur is the probability of \(A\) multiplied by the probability of \(B\) given \(A\): \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)\). This is the probability chain rule; it does not require the events to be independent. Here we apply the same identity conditional on the representation \(R\), with \(A=E\) (correct encoding) and \(B=F\) (fulfillment), yielding \(P(E\cap F\mid R)=P(E\mid R)P(F\mid E,R)\).

[^activity-data]: The activity-versus-outcome gap also appears in industry data.
