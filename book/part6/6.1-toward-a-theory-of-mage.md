# A Theory of MAGE

The book has argued from one case, seen in depth. A theory lets that case speak past itself: it names the
constructs, draws the mechanisms between them, and states what evidence would make the account fail. What
follows is a dynamic model of one phenomenon — how a fleet of coding agents turns abundant implementation
capacity into durable engineering progress, or into churn. A single system cannot fix universal
coefficients, so the model assigns none; read every claim as directional and conditional.[^theory-kind]

[^theory-kind]: In the terminology of theory-building scholarship, MAGE is a middle-range theory: it proposes mechanisms and scope conditions for a bounded class of phenomena rather than universal laws. The originating case supplies process depth; the industrial reconstructions test analytic generalization.

The central claim is not that agentic velocity is inherently productive. Velocity amplifies the fit
between the fleet and the engineering environment it runs in. When that environment is incomplete,
incoherent, or too weak to hold the work, velocity produces churn, escaped defects, repeated intervention,
and governance friction. Those signals create pressure to adapt the environment. Engineers respond by
modeling missing structure, converting recurring failures into durable mechanisms, and retiring governance
that has grown excessive. When the adaptation works, later agents inherit a more legible action space, and
more raw velocity becomes durable progress.

> ### Theory of MAGE (informal)
>
> Agentic velocity does not create engineering progress. It amplifies whatever governed
> engineering environment surrounds it.

## The dynamic model

[ref:mage-dynamic-model] draws the whole theory on one diagram: a single set of constructs rather than
four separate pictures, because the four dynamics act on the same system. It reads as a **dynamic causal
model** — the constructs joined by the paths the four dynamics run over, read as a system. It borrows the
loop-reading convention of system dynamics [cite: meadows2008thinking] without the full signed-polarity
notation; the links carry verbs.

The spine runs left to right. **Agentic capacity** — model capability, concurrency, raw implementation
rate — exercises a **governed environment** whose effective quality the model calls *E*. That environment
shapes **agentic work** into **realized performance**: a vector of three measures that move in different
desirable directions — durable throughput up, defect escape down, human attention per unit of durable
output down. The gap between the performance the fleet looks capable of and what it durably delivers
registers as **governance pressure**. Pressure meets **structural diagnosis** — the judgment that reads a
signal as a local defect or as evidence of missing structure. Diagnosis drives **governance adaptation**,
which changes *E*. The changed environment reopens the loop.

Four dynamics run over that spine, each colored and numbered so the figure survives in grayscale:

- **Blue — the bootstrap path.** Prior engineering knowledge seeds the initial environment before any
  agent runs.
- **Red — the churn/discovery path.** Work outruns the environment; churn and failure make the missing
  governance visible.
- **Green — the governance-adaptation path.** A structural diagnosis reshapes the environment for the
  next agent.
- **Purple — the capability-amplification loop.** A stronger environment raises **deployed agentic
  capacity** — feasible task size, concurrency, ambition — which pushes work onto an ungoverned frontier.
  It does not raise the foundation model's intrinsic capability.

Read the red path first: it carries the pressure an engineer actually feels; blue lowers it before it
starts, green relieves it once it appears, purple explains why it never stays relieved. The red and green
paths compose into a **balancing** loop that drives the environment toward fit; the purple loop is
**reinforcing** — a better environment raises ambition, and ambition reopens the frontier. Balance toward
fit, then reinforce: that is why MAGE does not converge while capability grows or ambition expands.

## The four dynamics

Each dynamic gets a prose subsection; the master figure already established the four as one system.

### The blue path: bootstrap governance

Not all governance is discovered through failure. A project begins with knowledge it inherited and
knowledge it can anticipate — a brownfield system carries architecture, tests, and operational history; a
greenfield one is seeded from prior experience, external standards, and lessons carried from earlier
systems. **Bootstrap governance** encodes that ex-ante knowledge into the initial environment: a known
obligation or a prior lesson becomes a model, a mechanism, or a constrained architecture, and the sum sets
the environment's starting quality. A conversion in one project leaves a durable artifact that becomes
bootstrap knowledge in the next; bootstrap reuses it but is not itself a conversion. Bootstrap is never
complete — the environment a product needs is not fully knowable in advance — so the blue path lowers the
initial failure rate without closing the red loop.

### The red path: churn makes missing governance visible

The motive for governance investment is not velocity itself but the experienced gap between what the fleet
appears capable of and what becomes durable progress. When the environment is weak, raw implementation
capacity produces rediscovery, regressions, escaped defects, recurring intervention, and confidently wrong
changes that consume tokens without advancing the system. Pressure takes several forms; **in the DocAble
case, churn dominated**, and the book uses **churn** as the running name. Velocity earns its place as an
amplifier: it clusters latent weakness in time, so failure classes that might have arrived months apart
land in a single afternoon, and their proximity makes the structure they share visible.

Pressure is not yet knowledge. **Structural diagnosis** distinguishes a local defect from a structural
failure class. A local defect gets repaired and the matter ends. In practice the fork is not always
clean — a local repair ships while the structural question stays open, one incident can implicate several
structures, and a mechanism's cost can outweigh the recurrence it would retire — but the judgment it names
is the scarce work either way. A structural failure reveals a weak or absent model, abstraction, boundary,
invariant, oracle, mechanism, or governance seam: something whose absence keeps producing failures until it
is supplied.

### The green path: governance adaptation

> ### The MAGE adaptation cycle
>
> Velocity surfaces churn; churn is diagnosed as missing structure; the structure is converted
> into a durable mechanism; the improved environment turns more velocity into durable progress —
> which raises ambition until it finds the next gap, and the cycle runs again.

The core move is **governance conversion**: turn a recurring failure class into a durable mechanism the
environment enforces on every later agent. Mature adaptation keeps that move and adds four around it:

- **Represent** — make missing intent or structure legible, so agents reason over the right representation.
- **Convert** — turn a recurring failure into a durable mechanism.
- **Strengthen** — replace weak probabilistic guidance with harder enforcement where the obligation
  justifies it.
- **Reconcile** — resolve controls that duplicate, conflict, or fire in the wrong order.
- **Retire** — remove governance that has gone stale or net-harmful.

The output is a change in the shape of future work: later agents inherit a more compact representation,
clearer constraints, fewer invalid moves, earlier evidence, and narrower sanctioned seams. That is why
adaptation can be subtractive as well as additive — reconciling a control collision or retiring a stale
gate raises quality without adding a thing.

The quality adaptation moves needs a definition sharp enough to keep apparatus quantity from standing in
for it.

> **Effective governed-environment quality, *E*.** The degree to which the environment gives agents a
> current, task-relevant representation of the system, together with coherent, proportionate,
> machine-actionable coverage of its important obligations.

That quality has four parts, set out in [ref:env-quality-dimensions]; each asks a question the raw
apparatus count cannot answer.

<!-- label: env-quality-dimensions -->
<!-- table: *The four dimensions of E.* Effective governed-environment quality has four parts; each asks a question the raw apparatus count cannot answer. [short: The four dimensions of E] -->
| Dimension | The question it asks |
|---|---|
| Representation quality | Are the relevant properties legible in the environment's current models? |
| Enforcement coverage | Are important obligations covered by controls **proportionate to their decidability and risk** — hard mechanisms where they fit, judgment-based review where they do not? |
| Coherence | Do the mechanisms compose without harmful collision or duplication? |
| Economy | Is the governance burden proportionate to the risk and value it controls? |

Coverage is not hardness: a high-quality environment assigns hard mechanisms where the obligation is
decidable and review where it is not, rather than over-hardening every clause. So *E* is a conceptual
**state vector**, not a measured scalar — **E = (R, C, H, Q)** over its four dimensions. A large support
apparatus raises a dimension of *E* only if it is coherent, economical, and actually covers the obligations
that matter; the same apparatus can instead be a tower of governance that lowers *E* while the control
count climbs.

### The purple loop: capability amplification

Successful governance expands what the engineer attempts. A stronger environment makes larger delegated
tasks feasible, supports greater concurrency, invites more ambitious requirements, and lets the operator
run agentic capacity denser. Every one of those pushes work onto surface the environment has not yet
governed, and the expanded frontier exposes new weakness. The loop feeds back into *deployed* agentic
capacity — the frontier the operator dares attempt — not the model's inherent ability.

This is **capability amplification**, and it is why MAGE does not terminate. The environment can reach a
stable equilibrium — ambition settles, the system enters maintenance, agent capacity stops changing — and
fails to converge only while one of those keeps moving. It also answers a natural objection: that better
agents will eventually remove the need for governance. They do the opposite. A more capable fleet raises
attainable velocity and ambition, which raises the consequences of a weak model or a missing control. The
frontier moves out with the capability, and governance follows it.

## Constructs and moderators

Two conditions the book leaned on — **diagnostic capability-fit** and **authority** — are not global
switches over the whole model. Each acts on one transition, and stating where sharpens what it predicts.

**Diagnostic capability-fit** sits on the pressure-to-diagnosis step. The deciding capability is the human
or organizational capacity to read a signal as structural, not the fleet's implementation capability — do
not read it as the model/task fit of Part 4. A capable fleet given to someone who cannot tell a local
defect from a structural one produces motion without direction: the pressure arrives, but no diagnosis
converts it. **Authority** sits one step later, on diagnosis-to-adaptation. The person who reads the
recurring failure has to be able to change the environment — the architecture, the mechanisms, the gates —
or the diagnosis dies as a local patch and the class never dies with it. Stated generally, H7 names the
engineering preconditions for learning: a discipline improves only where diagnostic capability-fit and the
authority to change the environment reach each other.

Authority need not live in a single architect; in an organization it is a functioning path from an
observed failure to shared infrastructure. Split ownership is not fatal on its own — the failure comes when
the path terminates in a local patch, and the class survives to recur under the next owner.

One moderator sets the theory's reach. **Domain governability** is the degree to which a domain's important
properties can be represented and mechanically checked at all. A schema conforms, an invariant holds, an
output validates — those are governable. Aesthetic, political, or socially negotiated properties are not,
and no mechanism settles them. Where governability is low, *E* has less to grip, and more of the work stays
in human judgment. The authorship boundary the closing Part draws is one instance of this, not a separate
law.

## A capability model, not a maturity model

MAGE is a capability model, not a maturity ladder, and the distinction matters because the field has been
here before. For two decades the dominant frame was the staged maturity model — the CMM and its CMMI
successor — which ranked an organization on a five-rung ladder and told it to climb. The defensible
objection: **the bottleneck in front of a project selects its next investment, not a rung above it**
[cite: bollinger1991critical]. "Capability model" here means a non-staged repertoire of abilities selected
by need.

A project's current bottleneck picks the next capability. When escapes climb, reach for the artifact-side
controls; when agents churn against a surface they cannot hold, invest in the models; when recovery keeps
pulling a human back in, harden the fleet's substrate. Dependencies may impose a local adoption order, but
that order is a dependency, not a rank. The reason there are no levels is the reason there are no fitted
coefficients: a single case cannot validate a progression that means the same thing everywhere. What
replaces "what level am I" is plainer — which capabilities do you have, and what does each one buy against
the bottleneck you actually face.

## Outcomes and observables

The model earns its keep only if its outcomes can be watched. Three carry the theory, and they are the
three dimensions the productivity literature already names — velocity, quality, and sustainability — read
for a fleet rather than a team. [ref:mage-outcomes] states each and the observables that would track it.

<!-- table: *The Outcomes.* The three primary outcomes the theory predicts, the way to state each so it reads as durable progress rather than raw activity, and the observables that would track it. [short: The three MAGE outcomes and their observables] -->
| Outcome | What it measures | Example observables |
|---|---|---|
| **Durable throughput** | Raw implementation converted into lasting system progress | landed changes net of rework; churn share; the slope of sustained throughput |
| **Defect escape** | Relevant failures reaching later lifecycle stages or production | escaped-defect rate; reopen rate; silent-corruption escape |
| **Human-attention burden** | Human judgment required per unit of durable output | interventions per landed change; review time; conversion effort |

The distinction between activity and outcome is already visible in industry data. LinearB's 2026
benchmark — 83,000 developers across 253 organizations [cite: linearb2026] — reports large gains in merge
rate among heavy AI users, then shows why merge rate alone misleads: among the heaviest AI users, yield
fell even as merge rates roughly doubled. DX deputy CTO Justin Reock told *The New Stack* that rising AI
spend has not produced proportionate shipping velocity, and that the trouble has moved from comprehension
to confidence: "We can understand the code … and make changes to it more easily. But we trust less what
we're releasing." [cite: riggins2026] The measurement question is not whether AI increases activity. It is
whether that activity survives review, rework, integration, and operation as durable progress.

MAGE names its outcomes to catch exactly this. Gross implementation velocity is an input, not a result.
Durable throughput is the output; defect escape records what apparent velocity concealed; human-attention
burden records the scarce judgment the surrounding system still consumes. State that burden as a rate, not
a ceiling: as recurring failure classes convert into durable governance, attention per landed change
falls, even if total governance work keeps growing. These observables are directional targets, not turnkey
metrics — each needs instrumentation the theory does not supply, and the easiest-to-count quantities (the
support ratio, the control count, the model-sync rate) measure investment, not whether the environment is
any good.

## Principal predictions

Here the model sticks its neck out. The dynamic model yields **eight principal hypotheses**, each naming a
mechanism and the single observation that would sink it. None receives an independent, population-level
test from this case — one repository cannot test a claim about a population — but the case supplies the
observations from which several were induced. Every row is stated as a directional, conditional claim: the
shape it should take and the conditions under which it should hold, not a measured law. If the field wishes
to falsify MAGE, these are the experiments to run. [ref:mage-hypotheses] lists the eight with the
observation that would falsify each.

Two rows carry conditions worth stating in the open. **H3 treats determinism as one strong class of
repeatable mechanism, not the whole category** — anomaly detection, metamorphic and differential testing,
and model-based semantic review are statistical yet repeatable, and count when adequately scoped. H3 does
not claim hard controls never fail; it claims a correctly-encoded, adequately-scoped mechanism is *less
sensitive to throughput* than finite review, so the gap between them opens as the fleet speeds up. **H6
promises only that the *per-change* oversight share falls as classes convert**, not that the total
oversight bill stops growing. Strip those conditions and both claims overreach; stated with them, each says
only what the evidence can bear.

Several claims read more precisely as consequences of the eight than as coequal predictions. Speed and
stability not trading is a corollary of H2 and H3 together; faster conversion as tooling matures is a
learning-curve specialization of H7; a self-healing substrate follows from H3 and H6; grammar coverage
predicting format-space escapes and a fidelity gate killing silent corruption are product-specific
instances of H4 and H3.

### The reasoning-horizon proposition

H4 is the falsifiable form of a wider claim the book has built toward since Part 1.

> **The Reasoning-Horizon Proposition.** Software engineering is a long-horizon reasoning task.
> Productivity degrades once the working reasoning state a task requires exceeds the effective reasoning
> horizon of the human-agent system. Executable models extend that horizon by replacing
> implementation-level state with semantically richer abstractions that **preserve a larger fraction of
> task-relevant information** under repeated compaction — but only when the model is task-relevant,
> synchronized, and cheaper to interpret than reconstructing the same facts from code. A model that fails
> those conditions *shortens* the horizon: it routes reasoning toward a stale picture the agent then pays
> to detect and undo.

The proposition names the mechanism; H4 states the test. A context window is one instance of a finite
horizon, not the horizon itself, so the claim should hold under longer windows and new memory
architectures. It draws its own boundary: **MAGE moves the scalability wall; it does not remove it.** Beyond
the point where working memory becomes the bottleneck, decomposition becomes unavoidable. The bound sharpens
H5 — an unmodelled surface is one the fleet must hold in raw implementation state, at a shorter horizon,
where churn returns.

### The compounding-governance proposition

A second wide claim runs parallel, and H6 is its falsifiable form.

> **The Compounding-Governance Proposition.** Governed engineering environments compound: as recurring
> engineering judgment converts into durable mechanisms, an increasing fraction of future effort **over
> the failure classes those mechanisms cover** can extend capability rather than re-solve settled
> problems — for as long as the accumulated engineering capital stays fit. These are returns to
> **accumulated engineering capital over covered classes**, not global returns to total governance
> expenditure.

Judgment spent once, encoded into a mechanism, lowers the recurring reasoning every later change demands;
H6 supplies the testable signature. The returns are increasing, not unbounded, and they attach to
engineering *capital* — coherent, quality-bearing governance — not apparatus quantity. The next section
develops that boundary; here it is enough that the proposition is the positive dual of churn.

### Learning propagation

H8 completes the chain H1 and H7 leave open: once a lesson is encoded into a shared, machine-actionable
environment, how fast and how reliably does it reach later work. Read with them, the three name the
organizational-learning pipeline end to end — discover faster, convert effectively, propagate
automatically. The lesson need not come from a failure. A better architecture, a proven invariant, a
regulatory reading — each propagates the same way once it becomes a model, a constraint, a validator, or a
gate, and future agents inherit it without every engineer first having to learn it.

This connects MAGE to the literature on organizational learning. Senge ties durable organizational
capability to the mechanisms by which local learning becomes collective. Argyris and Schön
[cite: argyris1978organizational] draw the closer distinction: single-loop learning fixes the immediate
defect while preserving the governing assumptions; double-loop learning revises the rules that produced it.
MAGE's governance conversion is recognizably double-loop, with one twist their account does not need — the
revised structure can be **executable**, so agents inherit it automatically rather than by being taught.
That is the sharpened form engineering capital takes: institutionalized learning whose effects outlast the
individual who learned the lesson. The book's own making illustrates the mechanism; it does not test it. H8
is offered, like the others, for external multi-case study.

## Two directional relations

Two hypotheses have a form clean enough to write down. Neither is a fitted law; each is a *shape*, the
arithmetic behind the arrows. Durable throughput is raw velocity net of what churn eats:

> **V_durable = V_raw · (1 − c)**   and   **∂c / ∂E < 0**

The first is definitional; the theory is entirely in what moves *c*. As effective quality rises, more of
the relevant surface fits the fleet's window before it churns — so churn share falls monotonically in *E*,
but **not** in governance apparatus *quantity*, and the two must not be conflated. The next section
develops why an overgrown estate churns again even as its control count climbs.

The second relation contrasts two ways to hold a failure class. Let *p* be the risk-weighted rate of
defect opportunity, *r(V)* the fraction finite human review still catches at velocity *V*, and *κ* the
risk-weighted fraction covered by effective mechanisms:

> **ε_review ≈ p · (1 − r(V))**   and   **ε_control ≈ p · (1 − κ)**

Human attention is finite, so *r(V)* falls as velocity rises and ε_review climbs toward *p*; a
correctly-wired mechanism runs on every change without consuming attention in proportion to throughput, so
*κ* does not. For adequately-scoped mechanisms, *κ* is less sensitive to throughput than *r(V)*, and the
daylight between "looked obeyed" and "was enforced" widens as the fleet speeds up — H3 in one line. Read
both as a **toy comparison**, not a model.

## Does governance eventually become a burden?

Accumulation is non-monotonic: past the point of fit, more apparatus makes things worse — the honest edge
that stops "more governance is always better." Churn has a dual. Where churn is the regime in which effort
dissipates, **compounding** is the regime in which it accrues: each governance conversion banks
*engineering capital* that lowers the future cost of change, so later work extends capability instead of
re-solving what judgment already settled. [ref:churn-vs-compounding] sets the two side by side — the same
environment, opposite signs.

Read left and downward, churn is a balancing regime: effort outruns a weak environment and dissipates into rediscovery, undo, and reconciliation, and the class recurs. Read right and upward, compounding is a reinforcing regime: failure becomes judgment becomes a conversion that banks engineering capital, the environment grows more capable, and later work is more ambitious at a falling per-change cost. What matters is the sign, not a new loop; the reinforcing arm is the green-to-purple composition the master model already draws. It holds only in the well-fit region: past fit, accumulation stops compounding and rejoins the churn curve's right band. -->

The right arm forces a distinction between two kinds of accumulation, and the book has been naming its
terms all along. [ref:churn-compounding-duality] pairs each negative dynamic with the positive counterpart
a well-fit environment produces.

That last pairing is conditional: repeated manual enforcement of a stable, decidable obligation becomes a
reusable mechanism, but some judgment is deliberately left manual. The right column is not automatic —
accumulation earns it only when it stays capital, which *E*'s `economy` and `coherence` dimensions are what
decide.

Name the two sides. **Bureaucracy** is accumulation that raises the cost of change without reducing future
reasoning: an extra checklist, an approval gate, a duplicated doc, a process re-applied by hand each time.
It adds friction and buys nothing back. **Engineering capital** is accumulation that lowers the recurring
judgment a change demands: a validator, a structured model, an architectural constraint, a reusable
mechanism. Capital compounds; bureaucracy does not. This is why the relationship between governance and
churn is non-monotonic in apparatus quantity — an under-governed environment churns from missing structure,
a well-fit one runs lowest, and an *over*-governed one churns again as controls collide, block legitimate
work, and burden context. More apparatus past the point of fit is not more capital; it is bureaucracy
wearing capital's clothes.

> **A heuristic for any governance decision.** Does this increase churn, or increase engineering capital?

Stated for action: **does the recurring reduction in future reasoning and risk justify the friction,
maintenance, and context this mechanism adds?** A mechanism can do both at once, so the decision is a
weighing, not a sort into two bins. And capital **depreciates**: a validator for a since-retired subsystem
becomes friction; a constraint tuned to an architecture the fleet has outgrown now blocks legitimate work.
So "does not compound" names two failures — apparatus that never reduced future reasoning, and capital that
has outlived its fit — which is why mature adaptation is subtractive: `reconcile` and `retire` manage
capital depreciation, not merely cleanup of bad apparatus.

## Evolution of this theory

The theory did not arrive whole. Its empirical core came first: the case-study paper *Cheap Code, Costly
Judgment* [cite: davis2026cheapcode] analyzed the first twelve weeks of the DocAble build and induced a
five-step governance-conversion loop — velocity exposes failure, the engineer classifies it as local or
structural, structural failures convert into governance, later agents inherit a narrower action space, and
repeated conversions raise the environment's capacity to absorb future work. That loop remains the
empirical kernel. Continued development extended it in four ways that carry lighter epistemic weight:
ex-ante bootstrap governance became a first-class path beside ex-post conversion; modeling became a coequal
route to governability, not one mechanism among many; governance accumulation proved non-monotonic, so
mature adaptation reconciles and retires as well as adds; and the single loop resolved into interacting
loops. The result is still a governance-conversion theory at its core — now describing MAGE as an adaptive
system for governing the engineering environment itself.

## Modeling feeds Alignment

The book has carried Modeling and Alignment side by side, as if they were parallel pillars. They are not
parallel: one feeds the other. **Modeling makes intent explicit; Alignment makes it binding.** A model has
to exist before anything can be enforced against it — without one, some obligations stay semantic, judged
probabilistically over raw code; with the right model, the same obligation becomes a predicate a machine
settles on every change. So the through-line of the method is a causal edge, not a symmetry: **modeling
expands what Alignment can determinize.** That is the second of a model's two jobs. The first — compress
the system so an agent reasons farther within a finite window — Part 2 argued. The second exposes structure
so some reasoning leaves the agent entirely and moves into a deterministic check; it serves scale, and a
book about comprehensibility alone would miss it.

Make the second job concrete. Say the architecture requires that no request reach the remediation service
without passing quota authorization. Verified against raw code, that is reasoning over hundreds of call
sites and injection paths, and the honest answer is a probabilistic judgment. Give the system an explicit
process-and-dependency model in which every allowed route is a represented edge, and the obligation
collapses to a query — for every path from a request to the remediation service, the quota gate lies on the
path — checked cheaply on every change. The property did not get easier; the *object the question is asked
over* changed, from an implementation to a model, and that is what made it decidable. With the model, no
per-change human reasoning stands between the change and the merge, which is what raises the ceiling on how
much autonomy is safe to grant.

A structured model left un-enforced is only advice: the fleet may reason through it and still drift, and a
drifted model is worse than none because it confidently misdirects. Bind it, and each obligation the model
makes checkable banks as engineering capital. Modeling supplies the representation; Alignment attaches the
authority that keeps it from decaying. A model becomes capital only once something refuses to let the
system contradict it.

## The three levels of evidence {#three-evidence-levels}

MAGE is a theory induced from one deeply observed system and tested analytically against independent
industrial accounts; the originating case provides process depth, the external reconstructions test whether
the mechanisms travel. One deep case cannot fix universal coefficients, and the book has never pretended it
could. What a single case can do is generate constructs and mechanisms. To learn whether those travel, the
theory is set against contemporary industrial field reports — organizations that built their own governed
environments with no shared theory to guide them. Those reports do not validate MAGE; practitioner accounts cannot establish
causation. They corroborate it, supplying convergent external evidence for its central mechanisms while
exposing operationalizations and boundary conditions the originating case could not have seen.

Three levels of evidence sit behind the argument, and the terminology should say so.

- **L1 — the originating case.** DocAble, watched from inside the machine: why each mechanism was
  introduced, what failure forced it, what alternatives were rejected, the months-long build history that
  produced it. This is genuine process tracing. **"Case study" names this level, and only this level.**
- **L2 — the reconstructions.** Cloudflare, Docker, Siemens, Shopify, Spotify, and Zenseact, read from
  outside. Public engineering reports show what mechanisms each system runs, how they fit, what MAGE calls
  them, and, as tellingly, what is conspicuously absent. They do not show the engineering history, so no
  month-by-month narration is attempted. These are **reconstructions**, not case studies — calling them
  case studies would promise DocAble-depth evidence no public account can supply.
- **L3 — validation across the reconstructions.** Read the six side by side and the same engineering
  grammar recurs: six starting points, one governing move, judgment converted into an explicit
  representation an environment enforces. Agreement across systems that differ in almost everything is the
  evidence a single case cannot give.

State the asymmetry as a feature. DocAble supplied mechanism depth — full process visibility, weak external
diversity. The industrial cases supplied variation — heterogeneous contexts, weak internal process tracing.
Together they support analytic rather than statistical generalization: the aim is not to estimate how often
organizations adopt a practice, but to explain why organizations that differ in nearly everything still
converge on similar engineering structures. [Part 5 introduces the case](5.1-the-problem-and-the-bar.html)
the theory was formed from. Where those cases pressed, they refined the theory — strengthening it in
places, narrowing it in others. A theory that only ever gets confirmed would be the suspicious result. This
one changed while its causal skeleton held.

## Closing

The claim of MAGE is not that more governance produces better software. It is that abundant implementation
capacity makes the fit of the engineering environment decisive. Known obligations can be encoded before the
work begins. Unknown ones become visible only through churn, failure, and friction. Judgment separates the
local defect from the missing structure; authority lets that diagnosis alter the shared environment; models
and mechanisms reshape what the next agent can know and do. When the loop works, velocity buys durable
progress. When it does not, velocity only reaches the churn wall sooner. That is the theory this book
leaves for the next case to test.
