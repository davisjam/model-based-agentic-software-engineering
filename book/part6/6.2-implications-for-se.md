# What Changes in Software Engineering

Early on, this book argued that AI does not retire the software lifecycle; it reassigns one seat — the
D moves to the fleet, the E stays with the engineer [ref:sdlc-to-selc]. The theory now developed says
why that cuts deeper than a change of who types. The lifecycle stays recognizable, but the artifacts
that support it, and the skills that perform it well, begin **migrating into the governed environment
itself** — migration, not replacement. One mechanism drives it: **velocity exposes failure.** Agents
change fast enough that missing abstractions, weak boundaries, and absent oracles surface in an
afternoon rather than a quarter, close enough together that you can see the structure they share. What
you do with that perception is the rest of this chapter.

## What migrates into the environment

The theory gives the chapter's observations one shape. Five engineering properties, each once resident
in a person, move into the environment: **representation**, **repository coherence**, **accumulated
experience**, **reusable reasoning**, and **effective capability**. Each maps onto a dimension of
environment quality or a loop the theory already drew. The sections that follow take them in turn.

## Representation engineering

The book has called judgment the scarce resource. True, and incomplete — it leaves the reader nothing
to practice. Name the skill instead. **Representation engineering: designing, maintaining, and governing
the explicit artifacts through which humans, agents, and mechanisms understand and act on a system.**
The scarce activity is the design, synchronization, and governance of those representations — the
structured models, specifications, invariants, and synchronization mechanisms that decide what a future
agent can know and safely do. "Get good at judgment" states a disposition. "Get good at representation
engineering" states a curriculum.

It is not a rebrand of an existing field. It is broader than requirements engineering, which fixes
*what* to build rather than the living artifacts agents reason over. It is distinct from knowledge
representation, a logic-and-AI tradition about symbolic inference. It is narrower in aim than model
engineering and MDA, which sought to *generate* the system from the model. And it *includes* context
engineering and architecture as parts rather than the whole: the artifacts, their synchronization, and
the mechanisms that keep them honest.

### Working at the model level

Working at the model level, with an agent doing the work, is where representation engineering pays off
most visibly. **A task-relevant synchronized model can simultaneously reduce irrelevant context,
support faster navigation, and expose invariants for checking.** These three gains share one cause —
the representation — but their magnitude and joint occurrence remain empirical questions. The case study
shows them arriving together; it does not establish, in a controlled way, that they always do, or that
the model, its synchronization, and its gates come without cost. They do not: authoring a model, keeping
it in sync, and building the gates are real work.

**A model is a distinct level of abstraction to work at.** It forms the model bridge between the
documentation and the code: machine-checkable like code and semantically compressed like a good design
document. An agent has a bounded window and reasons over whatever you put in it. Fill it with raw source
and you spend tokens on detail that does not bear on the change, and the agent reasons over the wrong
things. Fill it with the model and the join to the relevant code, and a larger fraction of the context is
task-relevant. The gain lives in the representation: the agent, no smarter than before, is now reasoning
over the *right* one. As long as the agent works that representation faithfully, the models do the
lifting — and because on a large, cross-cutting change a human can follow the model where the diff is
unreadable, you keep review and control at the same time.

The model level does not abolish essential complexity. Accessibility remediation across four document
formats, each with its own tag model and conformance spec, is genuinely hard, and no model makes it easy.
What the model level attacks is the representation the agent reasons over — the accidental difficulty our
notations and tooling pile on top, not the essential difficulty inherent in the problem (see the Brooks
primer). It strips the accidental cost of holding a large system in a small window, and leaves the
essential difficulty sitting in plain view where a human's judgment can meet it. That is the Modeling
Thesis paying off against churn: bind intent to implementation through a model, and the churn wall moves
out of reach.

This reframes the field's last run at making models central.
[CASE and Model-Driven Architecture](2.2-models-and-the-semantic-gap.html) reached for the maximal
version — make the model the system and execute it — and stalled for several reasons: semantic gaps,
tooling, model-and-code round-tripping, organizational adoption, expressiveness, generated-code quality,
process rigidity. Maintenance cost was one major barrier among several. Heavy models could not keep pace
with fast-changing code, and the price only paid off where mistakes were catastrophic. Agents change that
price, so how far to model is worth reopening.

### Repository quality acquires a representational dimension

Classical software engineering read repository quality through code: complexity, modularity, coupling,
maintainability. Those still matter. The theory adds a dimension they miss. Once the environment
synthesizes each agent's context from the repository's own representations, quality comes to depend on
whether those representations agree. Call the failure **representational entropy**: the same engineering
reality encoded many times over — in terminology, in models, in prose, in prompts, in specs — with the
encodings drifted out of step. The name is a teaching handle, not Shannon entropy; the empirical chapter
names its measurable cousin, the degree of drift among the encodings. This generalizes DRY. The old rule
banned duplicated *implementation*. The new one bans duplicated *meaning* left unsynchronized. A stale
doc, a conflicting prompt, a model that no longer matches the code: each raises the entropy and starves
the context the environment can assemble.

This seems to collide with the beautiful garden the empirical chapter describes, where redundant
encoding is a *strength* — one fact grown in many beds, surviving the loss of any single artifact. Synchronization is the distinction.
Redundant copies held equal by a drift gate give resilience: they agree, so any one of them serves.
Redundant copies left to drift give entropy: they disagree, so none of them can be trusted. DRY-for-meaning
does not forbid the many beds. It forbids the many beds growing different plants. Coherence, one of the
theory's four dimensions of environment quality, is precisely the property that separates the two.

### Reasoning compiled into the environment

**Portions of recurring reasoning are compiled into selection rules, models, and checks.** As
representations improve, more engineering reasoning gets performed once, captured, and reused. Dynamic
Context Injection is the worked case: the environment maps the files an agent is about to touch to the
exact constraints that govern them, then hands over that slice, so the agent inherits the relevant
structure instead of reconstructing it from raw source [appendix: dynamic-context-injection]. The
relationships, the models, the metadata perform a portion of the reasoning before the agent starts work.
A retrieval mapping or a constraint selection performs prior classification and routing, not the
open-ended reasoning the model still does. The reasoning is not gone. It is *amortized* — done by the
environment, drawn down by every later interaction. The fitting word is **compiled**, and it rhymes with
governance conversion: an insight, turned once into reusable structure, spent many times. The theory
chapter's primer on reasoning horizon and semantic compression develops the mechanism.

The same compilation shows up as product architecture. Salesforce's Agent Graph externalizes
reasoning into design-time graphs whose subagents are managed by finite state machines —
"guided determinism": deterministic workflow structure combined with probabilistic
conversational reasoning, keeping the model's freedom only where it is valuable. When part of
the reasoning is stable enough to represent explicitly, move it out of the model's private
inference into an inspectable structure [cite:salesforce2026agentgraph].

## Models as a common working language

This is the claim the whole book has been climbing toward, and it has earned a line of its own:

**Software joins every mature engineering discipline once maintaining models becomes cheap.**

Every mature engineering discipline reasons about what it builds through a model rather than the artifact
itself — the load diagram, the circuit schematic, the control loop, the thermodynamic cycle. Models are the
common working language of mature engineering. ("Universal" here means every mature field relies centrally
on reduced representations — not that every engineering decision is made through one; mature fields also use
prototypes, experiments, full-scale tests, and direct inspection.) Software was the odd field out, and not
for lack of trying: it built models it could not afford to keep. The two theses of this book are what let it
join at last. The Modeling Thesis makes the model the working representation the fleet reasons over; the
Alignment Thesis holds it to the code with a gate. Together they turn "the model is the language" from a
slogan into a checked practice — a picture accurate enough to trust *and* cheap enough to keep.

A historical claim sits underneath, worth stating plainly. Software was forced to spend its best effort on
the profession's *accidents* — syntax, builds, dependency hell, the friction of getting a change from a
head to a running system. Agile belongs on that ledger, read precisely: it reacted against heavyweight,
stale representations whose upkeep cost more than they returned — comprehensive documentation standing in
for working software — not against every form of model [cite: agile-manifesto2001]. Refactoring, tests,
domain modeling, and executable specifications were always compatible with drawing the map. The map drifted
only where keeping it honest by hand cost more than it returned.

Agents change that arithmetic. They absorb the accidental work — typing the code and keeping the map in
sync — while agents and CI perform the recurring enforcement at low marginal cost, though engineers still
design the checks. A larger share of engineering effort moves toward the essential: the system's real
behavior, its correctness, its design.

That relocation does not shrink the engineer's responsibility; it moves where responsibility is exercised.
An engineer never had to hand-implement every choice to be accountable for it — choosing a compiler with a
known defect profile delegated the code generation and kept the answerability, and that was always the
shape of engineering judgment. Agents extend the arrangement to the whole system: you delegate the
keystrokes and remain answerable down to the code and past it, to the properties a model names and a gate
checks. The locus of accountability did not move; the ratio of thinking to typing did.

The drift gate is where that residual has to be kept honest. It proves the model and the code agree; it
does not prove either is right
([the model can be a mirror, not a spec](2.2-models-and-the-semantic-gap.html)). Authoring the model that
says what agreement should *mean* — which properties it asserts, which "ought"s belong in it — is the part
that does not automate. The essential residual Brooks warned would not yield is still here, at a new
address: not "write correct code," but "author the model that says what correct means."

The last turn is the optimistic one. Modeling used to be architect-only work — the one person who could
hold the whole system in their head drew the map, and everyone else worked below it, implementing against
a picture they did not author. That division was a cost artifact. The map was expensive to draw and
expensive to keep, so few people drew it. Freed from the cost of *typing* and of
[*keeping the map in sync by hand*](3.8-keeping-models-in-sync.html), more engineers can work through
task-relevant views of the system — reasoning about its shape, and about how their own change moves the
whole. Modeling can become more widely participatory, because engineers can work through local views
without personally maintaining every derived artifact. That is the job the engineer's work becomes —
fluent authorship in the language every engineering discipline has always spoken, now finally cheap
enough for software to speak it too.

MAGE is not primarily a collection of software practices. It is an observation about engineering. Mature
engineering disciplines achieve reliability by engineering the environment in which work occurs rather than
relying on continuous individual vigilance. Commodity intelligence pushes software engineering toward the
same equilibrium.

## Governance discovery and mechanization

### From governing what you know to discovering what you don't

**For researchers:** the discovery problem below — governing what no one wrote down in advance — is where the empirical questions concentrate.

The tidy version of governance says: figure out your obligations in advance, encode them as mechanisms,
and let the agents run inside the fence. Do that. It works, up to a point. For the accessibility product I
had conformance checkers, content-preservation and provenance checks, and the ordinary commodity linters,
all of them known before a single agent touched the code. All of them necessary, of course. But they were
not sufficient, and the gap is the whole ballgame: the mechanisms you could not write down in advance,
because you only learn them by failing.

Under agentic velocity, *many of the mechanisms you need cannot be written down before the work begins.*
You discover them by failing. An agent makes a plausible, confident, wrong change; you look at it and ask
not merely "is this acceptable?" but "does this failure reveal a hole in my governance?"; and when the
answer is yes, you convert the hole into a durable mechanism. Governance, on this account, is a process the
environment runs continuously — one that turns agent-produced failures into architecture and lints and
gates that constrain the next agent. The environment *learns* — more precisely, the organization converts
observations into persistent artifacts and mechanisms. It does not autonomously learn in the
machine-learning sense; engineers and agents modify it, and the modifications accumulate.

This is why oversight, on its own, does not scale. The oversight model asks a human to supervise each
change. At the volumes agents produce, no one reads the diffs, and a human who tries becomes the
bottleneck the velocity was supposed to remove. The move that scales is conversion: convert a failure
class *once*, and later instances can be screened without repeated human inspection of the same class.
Work that produced no failure signal gets admitted. Work that exposed a recurring failure class earns a
new mechanism. The more classes you convert, the less vigilance you need.

Industry is arriving at the same move under its own name. GitHub's Applied Science team,
making agents the primary contributors on a project that moved +28,858/-2,884 lines across
345 files in under three days, reports the same operational move MAGE calls governance
conversion: when an agent repeats a mistake, change the environment — "blame process, not
agents" — including a reserved test space the agent cannot touch [cite:mcgoffin2026appliedscience].

### Managing the workforce is not governing the environment

**For organizations:** the failure mode here is treating an agent fleet as headcount to manage rather than an environment to govern.

The volume is only half of why supervision fails. The other half is a difference in kind, and it is worth
naming the school that difference defeats, because that school is the reasonable one. The natural response
to a fast, fallible workforce is to reach for the practices that tamed fallible *human* teams: brief the
worker, review its output, require a sign-off, keep an evidence trail — adapt the organization to the
agent.

Human-team practices assume continuity, social accountability, and tacit knowledge to a degree fresh
agent sessions do not — and transplanting the visible rituals (brief, review, sign-off, evidence trail)
without replacing those hidden supports is insufficient. The distinction is real without being absolute:
human understanding is itself incomplete and transient, agents can retain state through artifacts and
memory systems, and review practices can be mechanized. The point is not that agents are a lesser grade of
junior engineer. It is that the supports human organizations lean on are not the ones a fresh session
inherits, so the reliability has to come from the environment instead.

You cannot org-chart your way to trust in probabilistic output. What the environment supplies instead is a
model it holds equal to the code by construction — a machine checks model–code agreement, and model
invariants, before the work is admitted. That is why this book argues for rebuilding the discipline around
the environment that governs the agents, rather than offering a manual for absorbing them into an existing
shop.

### Soft mechanisms saturate; the substrate has to become machine-actionable

Here is a proposition I would put money on. **As velocity rises, tacit and review-centered mechanisms
saturate on human attention; deterministic mechanisms do not consume human attention linearly with
throughput.** A hard mechanism can still saturate — on compute, latency, queue depth, false-positive
burden, maintenance, or coverage. What it does *not* do is demand a proportional share of a person's
reading for every unit of work it clears. That is the asymmetry that matters at fleet scale.

The reason is arithmetic. A convention is a probability flip. Flip it a thousand times a day and the
one-in-a-hundred failure isn't rare — it's a schedule. A convention that an agent follows ninety-nine
times in a hundred looks like a working mechanism when one engineer works at human speed; the faster the
fleet runs, the sooner the hundredth violation arrives — many times a day, on the clock. This is an
intuitive illustration, not a formal argument — it assumes independent trials at a stable failure rate,
and a convention is not itself a probability flip; adherence under conditions is what is probabilistic.
Words are soft, and soft holds right up until the volume finds every edge you left in.

I have a concrete one. My cleanup routine had to distinguish a finished agent's abandoned worktree from a
running agent's — and my rule for it lived in the instructions I wrote for each cleanup pass: "check that
the agent isn't still in flight before you delete its workspace." It read as sound, and it failed three
times in five days, each time destroying a live agent's work. The trap was timing: a freshly dispatched
agent looks, for its first minutes, exactly like a finished one — a single bookkeeping commit and nothing
else — so any check that reads the commit history at one instant misclassifies it. Rewording the
instruction did not help; I reworded it three times. What ended the recurrence was moving the check out of
the prose and into the tool: the dispatcher now writes a marker file when an agent boots and the tool
deletes it only after the agent is truly done, and the cleanup *refuses* to touch any workspace whose
marker is still present. The convention was correct and unenforceable; the marker is a fact the tool reads
the same way every time, and it cannot be reworded away.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [agent registry](appendix-a-agent-registry.html).

So the governance has to move down to where a machine can enforce it: into types the compiler checks,
schemas a validator reads, lints and analyses that gate a commit before a human ever sees it. None of this
rejects the soft mechanisms, of course. The briefs, the templates, the prose conventions still aim the
agents; they do real work. But they must be *mated* to deterministic mechanisms that hold the line whether
or not the agent cooperates. The soft layer guides; the hard layer holds. At velocity you need both, and
the failure mode of relying on the soft one alone is quiet: the convention looks obeyed right up until the
day the volume finds the gap.

There is a counterintuitive corollary here that took me a while to trust: the stronger the guardrails, the
faster the fleet can safely run. An agent that hits a deterministic guardrail gets an immediate, legible
signal it can act on, and recovers from its own mistake without a human in the loop. The guardrail that
would have felt like friction to a human team is, to an agent fleet, the thing that lets the throughput
continue at all.

Read the spectrum as increasing machine-actionability and enforcement regularity, not ontological
hardness. The classes differ on several axes at once — determinism, invocation guarantee, enforcement
strength, evidence quality, admission effect — and a reviewing agent is not simply "harder" than prose in
every sense; a doc-derived test can still be flaky. When does a soft rule earn promotion to a hard one?
Repeated or consequential failure is the evidence; apply the risk-and-cost test — recurrence likelihood,
consequence, mechanism cost, decidability, false positives. [ref:soft-hard-spectrum] draws the spectrum
this section has argued, with the mechanism classes seated along it.

### Engineering environments accumulate experience

Organizations used to improve mainly because their people did. An engineer met a failure, learned from it,
carried the lesson to the next task. The knowledge lived in heads and left when they did. Governance
conversion moves the lesson elsewhere: a recurring failure becomes a model or a mechanism, and the
environment holds it for every later agent. The environment grows more experienced, and its experience
does not walk out the door.

Organizational theory named these moves long before agents existed, and the names are worth borrowing.
Argyris and Schön separated single-loop learning — correcting an error inside the existing rules — from
double-loop learning, which revises the governing rules themselves [cite: argyris1978organizational].
Governance conversion is double-loop learning mechanized: an agent's failure is not merely patched but read
as a defect in the governing structure, and the structure is rewritten so the class cannot recur. Senge
described the learning organization as one that keeps enlarging its capacity to create its own future
[cite: senge1990fifthdiscipline]; a governed environment is that idea with the learning deposited in
executable artifacts rather than in shared disposition. And distributed cognition established decades ago
that knowing is spread across people, tools, and artifacts rather than sealed in one mind
[cite: hutchins1995cognition] — organizational memory can outlast its members [cite: march1991exploration]
[cite: argote2000knowledge]. The agentic shift is not that knowledge leaves heads for artifacts; that is
old. It is how much of that memory becomes *executable* and *consumed at task time*: a lint or a gate does
not merely record a lesson, it enforces it on every change, and Dynamic Context Injection hands the
relevant slice to the agent before it starts.

## The engineer's role and organizational conditions

### The judgment moved, and it moved toward you

**For the practitioner:** this is the implication that lands on your desk first.

The premise is settled: implementation moved to the fleet, judgment stayed with the person, the engineer's
center of gravity shifted from author to architect. Rather than restate it, name what it demands. **Three
competencies come to dominate the work: model design, governance design, and evidence engineering** —
authorship in the working language the fleet reasons through, the discipline of turning a failure class into
a mechanism that holds, and the construction of an environment whose output can be consumed as assurance.
Each is a section of this chapter wearing its practitioner face. Better agents make all three *more*
consequential, not less: they raise your ambition and your concurrency, so the decisions about what to build
and how to bound it are what separate durable progress from an expensive pile of churn.

Two things gate whether this works — the conditions under which the whole method holds or fails.

- **Capability-fit.** The theory's *diagnostic capability-fit* moderator, defined in the theory chapter,
  has an organizational face here: a capable fleet handed to someone who cannot tell a local defect from a
  structural one produces motion without direction. The governance does not design itself; a person has to
  look at a failure and decide it is the symptom of a missing abstraction. The consequence for how you
  staff and assign authority is what this chapter owns; the moderator itself belongs to the theory.
- **Authority.** The person who sees the recurring failure has to be able to *change the environment* — the
  architecture, the mechanisms, the gates. This sounds trivial when one engineer owns the whole system. It
  is not trivial in an organization, where authority is split across teams, owners, review boards, and
  deployment pipelines. Split the authority and the same failure signal produces a local patch instead of a
  shared mechanism, and the class never dies. Agentic tools compress team-scale coordination into a single
  workflow; that compression is exactly what makes authority the pressure point.

### What this changes for measurement, quality investment, and education

**For researchers and educators:** the open measurement problems and the shift in what to teach live here.

A few consequences follow, and I will keep them short because each could be its own argument. Staffing
follows from the authority condition just named — who can change the environment decides how you organize
around it — so I do not treat it as a separate domain here.

**Measure governed throughput, not volume.** Commits landed, lines changed, tasks completed — these count
activity. They do not tell you whether the activity became durable software. The question that separates
progress from churn is whether agent-produced failures are getting converted into reusable governance, and
whether later work benefits from those conversions without the whole thing losing coherence. Optimize for
volume and you will get volume; you will also get a fast pile of slop with no one able to read it. I have
the commits to prove that too.

**The marginal cost of quality dropped, so treat quality work as affordable.** The refactors and
architectural improvements that a conventional team defers because they are too expensive become feasible
when an agent fleet can carry out the mechanical part in a day. I moved an entire front end to a typed
language over a weekend and saw no detected regressions under the project's own checks. The same speed that
manufactures technical debt can also pay it down — and, because related failures cluster in time, can make
the architectural gap *visible* in the first place. Speed cuts both ways; which way depends on whether you
convert what it exposes. Convert it and the debt turns to its opposite — *engineering capital*, the stock
that compounds — the duality the theory chapter draws out.

**Read model failure as a framing problem before a capability ceiling.** When an agent fails a delegated
task, the tempting inference is that the model cannot do it. Sometimes that is true. In this case, many
apparent capability failures dissolved after decomposition and stronger interfaces — the difficulty was
mine, not the model's. The working disposition I ended up with was to assume that *I* was the problem: to
treat a failure as a cue to decompose further and constrain harder, not as evidence of a ceiling. There is
a paradox worth sitting with here. The model is valuable *because* it reasons over open-ended inputs, and
yet the method is largely about systematically removing its opportunities to reason unconstrained. The
paradox resolves once you remember what the machine is: a probabilistic sequence generator whose errors
compound across dependent decisions. You bound the probabilistic behavior rather than exclude it — soft
guidance going in, deterministic guardrails on the actions, and a deterministic envelope on what gets
admitted out.

**For research and education, attend to the environment, not only the agent.** We already know agents can
generate. The open question is which engineering environments make that generation *governable* — and
whether agents can be taught to notice inadequate governance themselves, rather than waiting for a human to
convert every failure by hand. For teaching, the implication is uncomfortable but clear: the comparative
advantage is shifting away from syntax-level production and toward specification, abstraction design,
validation, and process. If that sounds like a softer curriculum, I assure you it is a harder one — and it
is the one that will matter.

## Consequences for software engineering

Take the five migrations together and the discipline's center of gravity shifts from the code to the
environment that governs it. Where the work lands, how you measure it, what you invest in, and who holds
authority all follow from that shift — the sections above traced each. One consequence remains, and it is
the one the next study should test.

**Effective capability may become a property of the environment, not only of the model.** Picture two
organizations. One runs slightly stronger models over an ordinary repository: sparse metadata, weak
synchronization, little explicit structure. The other runs slightly weaker models over a repository whose
semantics are richly modeled, kept in sync, and used to synthesize each task's context. The old intuition
backs the first; it holds the more capable intelligence. The theory raises a different possibility: the
second may win, because it has engineered the more capable *environment*.

State this as exactly what it is. It is an implication of the theory, not a result this single case
established — a hypothesis the book hands to the next study, phrased the way [ref:mage-hypotheses] phrases
the rest, and the direct disciplinary reading of **H4**: repository quality *may* substitute, at the
margin, for model capability, because rich representations let the environment perform some of the
reasoning before the agent begins. Whether the trade holds, and how far, is the kind of whole-environment
question the empirical program that follows is built to ask.

### Development completeness

The book has made the governed engineering environment the primary engineering asset. One consequence
lands on open source, and it wants stating carefully, because the reflex is to overclaim. Software firms
have long shipped source while withholding what makes source easy to change — an absent design manual,
internal build tooling, the operational know-how that never left the building. Agentic engineering does
not invent that gap between having the code and being able to evolve it. It enlarges it. As more of an
organization's effective development capability moves into models, skills, validators, policies, and
context machinery, a larger fraction of what lets the originating team change the system quickly and
safely comes to sit outside the production source tree.

The open-weights debate is the near analogy. Model weights without the training data, recipe, and
evaluation regime are inspectable and modifiable, and still do not hand you the originating lab's
capacity to produce the next version. Source without the governed environment is the software echo:
forkable code that does not by itself reproduce the loop through which the upstream fleet reliably
modifies it.

The literature already knows this shape, one layer at a time. The Open Source Definition ties openness to
the "preferred form in which a programmer would modify the program" and rejects deliberately obfuscated
source [cite:osiOpenSourceDefinition] — openness never meant "whatever artifact happens to be
human-readable." Reproducible-build research makes the next move downstream: source availability does not by
itself establish that a distributed binary corresponds to that source, so trustworthy reconstruction hinges
on controlling the build environment and instructions, not on the source alone [cite:lamb2021reproducible]
[cite:fourne2023reproducible] [cite:vu2021lastpymile] [cite:courtes2023guix] [cite:malka2025nix]
[cite:sharma2025canonicalization]. And legally open code can still be hard to extend when the surrounding
knowledge — documentation, newcomer paths, the links from a feature back to its code — is missing
[cite:steinmacher2015barriers] [cite:puhlfurss2022documentation]. The Open Source AI Definition then met the
same question one field over, extending "preferred form for modification" past weights to the data and
process information a skilled person needs to rebuild a substantially equivalent system
[cite:osiOpenSourceAIDefinition]. MAGE suggests the analogous next step for agentic software: as environment
artifacts become part of the effective means by which a project is modified, practical modifiability may
increasingly depend on whether those artifacts are shared too.

> **Inset — the right-to-repair analogy.** A machine can be owned outright and still be hard to repair
> without the maker's diagnostic tools and manuals. In July 2026 the FTC and five states settled with
> Deere & Company, requiring it to give farmers and independent shops the repair resources authorized
> dealers already held: diagnostic and fault-code tools, component reprogramming, restart capability, and
> technical documentation [cite:ftc2026deere]. The owner already had the tractor; what was withheld was
> the capability to diagnose and restore it. Read as an engineering principle, not legal precedent, it
> names the distinction this section draws — possessing the artifact is not possessing the means to evolve
> it. The progression runs continuously: OSI ties openness to the form used for modification; reproducible
> builds show source alone may not rebuild the artifact; right-to-repair shows owning the artifact may not
> supply the tools to maintain it; MAGE suggests source alone may increasingly fail to reproduce the
> capacity to evolve a system safely at scale. So the open-software question becomes not only whether the
> source is available, but how much of the effective development environment is shared with it.

This does not redefine open source, and it should not. OSI openness concerns licenses and rights, and
OSI itself treats reproducibility as a requirement layered on top of openness rather than identical to
it. The cleaner move is a second axis. Source openness asks whether outsiders hold the legal and
technical artifact they may modify. **Development completeness** asks how much of the originating team's
capacity to modify it has been shared as well. That axis admits degrees:

- source available → build reproducible → tests reproducible → engineering-knowledge available →
  governance reproducible → agentic-development-environment reproducible.

A project can be fully open at the first rung and progressively less complete at the later ones. The
progression measures; it does not indict. Where a project stops describes it, and no rung is owed.

The supply-chain reading is the sharper half. Conventional supply-chain thinking already counts build
tools, compilers, and CI systems: components that shape an artifact without shipping inside it. The
extension is that judgment-bearing artifacts now join that chain. Third-party skills, MCP and tool
servers, agent profiles, policy bundles, context providers, and validators can causally change what code
an agent produces even though none of them ship in the product. This connects straight to the governance
graph the book drew. An imported skill can collide with a local one; a vendor validator can carry
assumptions the project's model rejects. Those are supply-chain composition problems inside the
environment, not the artifact. Industry is beginning to treat them that way. A major vendor now runs
third-party skills through a multi-stage supply chain of review, scanning, evaluation, signing, and
provenance [cite:nvidia2026verifiedskills], and a study of policy-as-code adoption finds governance and
configuration concerns already externalized into machine-readable project artifacts across hundreds of
repositories [cite:foalem2026policyascode]. Portions of the environment are becoming source-like
artifacts in ordinary open source.

The commercial angle follows without sensation. Firms have long opened code while keeping operational
expertise, internal infrastructure, and tooling; a proprietary governed environment is the agentic-era
extension of that old asymmetry, with the moat sitting in accumulated engineering capital rather than in
hidden source. A security-of-sharing corollary rides along: an organization may be able to publish
larger, more consequential systems if the safety-critical engineering capability stays encoded in an
environment it does not distribute. The flip side is equally real. If downstream users must then maintain
the release without that environment, withholding it can make the release less sustainable rather than
more secure — a recent treatment of the open-weights problem points the same direction, arguing that
reconstructing an AI system may need code, data, weights, toolchain, and hardware configuration, not
source alone [cite:hatta2026reproducibility].

Where the analogy stops is where the novelty sits, and it is narrow. Environment artifacts may constitute
a new layer of a project's effective development form and its supply chain precisely because they encode
the judgment through which autonomous agents modify the source. The source tree tells you what the system
is; the governed engineering environment increasingly decides how fast and how safely it can become
something else. The book does not settle what to do about that. It leaves three questions for a later
program:

1. What constitutes development completeness in an agentic open-source project?
2. Which environment artifacts should be counted part of the software supply chain?
3. Can commercial open source hold a durable advantage by opening the artifact while retaining parts of
   the engineering environment?

And behind all three, the one that frames them: what does it mean to share *enough* of a governed
engineering environment for an outsider to reproduce not merely the artifact, but the originating
project's capacity to evolve it?

That question has a sibling in the section that follows. Where this one asks what an outsider must be
*given* to reproduce a team's capacity to evolve a system, the assurance frontier asks how much of that
same environment's output an auditor can *consume* as evidence.

### The assurance frontier

The book named one frontier and worked it hard: the determinization frontier, the moving line
between what still needs semantic judgment and what a machine can settle as a predicate. There is a
second frontier beside it, and this section only points at it. Call it the **assurance frontier**:
how much of the assurance a regulated system must demonstrate can become an emergent property of the
engineering environment, rather than a separate certification activity bolted on at the end? The book
does not answer that. It marks the question and leaves the work to a later study.

Start with what regulated engineering does today. It runs a pipeline: engineer, implement, collect
evidence, audit, certify. Traceability exists mostly to make the audit possible. Models often exist
because a standard requires them. Documentation is treated as overhead. MAGE changes why those
artifacts exist. A fleet cannot reason over a system it cannot see, so the environment already holds
models, constraints, validators, provenance, and a traceable governance graph — built because the
agents need them to work, not because an auditor will ask. The compliance artifacts become
by-products of good engineering instead of a tax laid on top of it.

Traceability shows the inversion most sharply. Today it reads as bureaucracy, a mapping kept for
auditors at a cost the project resents. In a model-rich environment it is a reasoning substrate: the
fleet has to know which obligation governs which code and which change touched which property,
because that is how context gets synthesized and invariants get checked. You build it because agents
need it. Auditors then consume what was already there. Documentation follows the same path — one
artifact, several readers: humans, agents, the deterministic validators that gate a change, and the
auditor at the end.

The positioning chapter drew this shape for Siemens: one model serving reasoning, simulation,
verification, certification, and maintenance, now with agent context added to the list. The model is
authored once and queried many ways. Certification, on this reading, becomes one of those queries
rather than a fresh assembly job.

The standards this touches are closer than they look, and two are worth naming. NIST's AI Risk
Management Framework organizes its work under **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage** — a vocabulary a
governed environment already speaks, close enough that MAGE could plausibly serve as an
implementation methodology for parts of it. UL 4600 asks for a convincing **safety case**: an
argument with its evidence, assumptions, and limitations, not a checklist met. A model-rich
environment produces most of those ingredients as it runs. The functional-safety and airworthiness
regimes behind them — ISO 26262, DO-178C, DO-330, IEC 61508, and the FDA's software-as-a-medical-
device guidance — differ in the particulars, but each, at bottom, asks the same thing: show me
evidence. MAGE's answer would be to engineer an environment that produces evidence as a side effect
of operating. Two cautions belong here in the same breath. We have not demonstrated conformance to
any of these standards; the claim is proximity, not satisfaction. And the vocabularies rhyme without
being identical, so "an implementation methodology for the RMF" is a hypothesis to test, not a result
to report.

Safety and security cases are where the shift is easiest to see. A case is a claim, an argument, and
the evidence beneath it. That evidence can increasingly be drawn from artifacts the fleet already
produces: provenance, model–code parity, validator output, simulation, tests, static analysis,
runtime telemetry. The engineer's work moves from assembling the evidence to judging whether the
assembled evidence supports the claim. That relocates the human's attention; it does not remove the
human. The argument still needs an author, and authoring it — deciding what the evidence must mean —
is the part that does not automate.

This reframes a common worry. The reflex is that regulation slows AI-assisted engineering. The
likelier story runs the other way: **regulation rewards environments.** An organization that already
built a model-rich governed environment, for velocity and its own reasons, should find regulated work
easier rather than harder, because the artifacts an auditor asks for are the ones the fleet was
keeping anyway.

A prediction follows, stated plainly even though the book cannot test it. If MAGE-style practice
spreads beyond software, the first communities to adopt it may not be startups. They may be
aerospace, medical devices, automotive, and industrial automation — fields that already think in
models, traceability, evidence, and assurance. Their core abstractions are the ones MAGE repurposes,
so they may recognize the method faster than mainstream web engineering does. The move MAGE makes is
subtle: it turns artifacts that were primarily *compliance* artifacts into *engineering* artifacts
that simultaneously drive autonomous development and satisfy assurance needs.

None of this is established here. It is a research program — a MAGE II, or the TSE paper that follows
this one — and it rests on a single conjecture the book states and does not prove:

> We conjecture that model-rich governed environments naturally support assurance arguments because
> many of the required artifacts — traceability, provenance, constraints, evidence, and validation —
> already exist as first-class engineering objects.

Whether that holds, and how far, is the question a later study is built to ask. It closes one loop
the theory already drew: engineering capital funds the governed environment, the environment yields
evidence as it operates, the evidence supports an assurance argument, and the argument carries a
certification — one loop, run for a purpose the book leaves to the work that comes next.
