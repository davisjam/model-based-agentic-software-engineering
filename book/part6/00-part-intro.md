{{chapter:software-factories}} examined DocAble’s software factory and related arrangements reported by other organizations. These organizations differ in the engineering knowledge they preserve, the controls they enforce, and the work they delegate to agents. A representation that saves substantial effort in one environment may duplicate knowledge already available in another; a control that enables safe delegation may also impose costs that outweigh its benefit elsewhere. What determines whether an investment improves the reliability, cost, and cumulative value of engineering?

We begin with three questions: did the agent produce what the engineer intended, what did it cost, and what did the work leave behind for subsequent engineering ([ref:chapter:6.1-toward-a-theory-of-mage])? Four theoretical propositions then explain how representations, independent enforcement, and engineering capital can affect the use of additional agentic capacity ([ref:chapter:6.2-what-the-theory-predicts]). We identify the conditions under which these relationships should matter ([ref:chapter:6.3-scope-conditions]) and then examine how measurements and comparisons can test them and guide engineering investment ([ref:chapter:6.4-research-agenda]).

> ### Key Ideas {#the-synthesis}
>
> Agentic capacity makes implementation work cheaper, but more implementation does not necessarily mean more engineering progress. MAGE addresses the relationship between the two.
>
> Successful delegation requires engineers to express what they want and agents to produce it. But engineering does not consist of one attempt. Systems change repeatedly, and the work done today can make tomorrow’s work easier or harder.
>
> The question is how engineers make today’s work useful for later tasks: which knowledge to represent, which obligations to enforce, and which decisions should remain with a reasoner. The objective is durable progress at a justified cost, rather than maximum machinery or maximum implementation speed.
