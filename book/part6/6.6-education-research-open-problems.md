# The Profession: Education and Research

Discussion of AI and software engineering tends to collapse toward two poles.
One says that AI changes everything: implementation is delegated, natural language replaces programming, and engineers mainly need agents, prompts, and harnesses.
The other says that little of consequence has changed: coding agents are another productivity tool and the established practice can continue much as before.

MAGE implies a less comfortable answer for both.
The implementation substrate has changed enough to reorganize the economics and daily practice of software engineering.
The principles required in response are largely familiar: requirements still have to be discovered, abstractions chosen, quality attributes traded, security boundaries defended, and claims justified by evidence.
**The principles survive precisely because the practice has to change.**

The professional question follows.
If implementation moves disproportionately to the fleet, what competence and responsibility remain with the engineer?
And if those are the scarce parts, what should the profession teach and study?

## Authority follows competence

MAGE is not a shortcut around engineering knowledge.
It increases the leverage of that knowledge and therefore the consequence of getting it wrong.
An agent can increasingly carry the method itself: it can recognize modeling situations, choose among known model families, construct a state machine, query a dependency graph, propose an invariant, or recognize that recurring inspection should become machinery.
That is a substantial transfer of engineering repertoire, and as the method becomes machine-readable — packaged as a mastery-skill an agent reasons through, which [appendix: appendix-skill-recipe] builds from the same construction recipe — knowing its mechanics is less of what distinguishes an engineer.
It still does not establish that the chosen abstraction captures what matters, that the proposed invariant expresses the intended property, or that the resulting tradeoff is acceptable.
Engineering competence includes knowing the repertoire; the engineer remains answerable for what the system is supposed to mean.

Engineering authority is not merely technical.
Societies entrust engineers with consequential decisions because engineers are expected to understand the systems they certify and to stand behind them.
The institutional details differ across domains, but the principle holds: authority follows competence and responsibility.
Engineers can carry legal, professional, and sometimes personal liability for the decisions they sign.
An AI system can generate an artifact or assemble evidence.
It cannot assume the professional responsibility attached to an engineer's judgment.

Engineering has always meant moving between levels of abstraction.
The skill is not to live at the lowest level; it is to know which level exposes the property you need, and to descend when the current abstraction stops answering the question.
Working with agents is another version of that move.
The shallow reading calls it natural-language programming: say what you want, receive code.
The engineering reading is different.
The agent may know the repertoire and propose the move; the engineer remains answerable for what the system is supposed to mean — which distinctions matter, which properties deserve preservation, what representations are fit to carry them, what evidence is sufficient, and when the environment's assumptions must change.
As the repertoire moves to the agent, the engineer's leverage moves upstream: less supervising each action, more authoring the environment those actions run inside.
The division is durable enough to name; [ref:authorship-division] sets the two columns side by side.

The boundaries are not metaphysical.
An organization may delegate parts of the right column — a review board, a platform team, a senior reviewer — and MAGE does not forbid it.
What MAGE changes is that the delegation becomes explicit and governed rather than implicit and unaccountable.
Review moves upstream in the same way (see "Review after implementation becomes abundant," earlier in this Part); authorship is its companion — what the engineer decides is worth engineering, before any change is there to review.
**Natural language may be one interface to the work. It is not the engineering method.**

## Sign-off moves toward models and evidence

Imagine an agentic system in which the fleet constructs most of the implementation, maintains selected engineering representations, runs validators, and assembles evidence for the claims the design is supposed to satisfy.
Human review then looks less like authorship of the implementation and more like technical acceptance: inspect the representations, challenge their assumptions, examine the evidence attached to important claims, request deeper analysis where the case is weak, and decide whether the result is fit to accept.

This is a direction, not an empirical finding of this book.
The stronger claim is nevertheless hard to avoid at scale: **implementation-level inspection cannot be the primary human assurance mechanism for large systems.**
That was true before coding agents; no engineer establishes confidence in Linux by holding its source code in working memory.
Agentic implementation volume makes the mismatch more immediate by producing large unfamiliar changes faster than a person could conventionally inspect them.

Models provide intermediate review surfaces.
A structural representation can expose decomposition and permitted dependencies; a behavioral representation can expose legal states and transitions; a decision model can expose authority; a measurement model can connect an engineering claim to the observations meant to support it.
Tests, static analyses, benchmarks, simulations, and operational measurements then provide evidence about those claims.
Neither representation nor evidence is sufficient alone.

The relationship settles into four lines.

> **Models** state selected claims about the system and the properties that matter.
> **Evidence** says why we should believe those claims.
> **Governance** says what evidence is required and what authority follows from it.
> **The engineer** remains answerable for whether those are the right claims, representations, tradeoffs, and standards of evidence.

That last line is where professional responsibility lands, and it is worth stating plainly what it is.
Accountability moves address, not owner.
An engineer never had to hand-implement every choice to remain answerable for it: choosing a compiler with a known defect profile already delegated the code generation and kept the answerability.
Agentic systems extend the arrangement to the whole system.
The engineer delegates the keystrokes and stays answerable down to the code and past it — to the properties a model names and the evidence a validator produces.
The locus of accountability does not move; the ratio of thinking to typing does.

Early evidence describes that shift rather than only predicting it.
A six-month longitudinal study of engineers adopting AI assistants found most of them writing less code and spending more time directing, checking, and correcting what the model produced — a mode its authors name *supervisory engineering work*.
It did not come free: flow and cognitive load both eroded even as output held [cite: vella2026impact].
Sign-off through models and evidence is the disciplined form of that supervisory work — the alternative is to supervise output the reviewer has no legible surface to reason over.

Two conditions decide whether that answerability is real rather than nominal.
The first is **capability-fit**: a reviewer who cannot tell a local defect from a structural one produces motion without direction, and stronger agents make the distinction more consequential, not less, because they raise ambition and concurrency faster than they raise confidence.
The second is **authority**: the person who recognizes a structural failure has to be able to change the environment — the architecture, the models, the mechanisms — or the lesson dies as a local patch and the failure class never closes.
A lone engineer who owns the whole system meets both by default.
Split the seat across owners, review boards, and deployment pipelines, and both become pressure points — which is why authority returns below as an organizational research question, not only a technical one.

The models themselves remain objects of engineering.
Their fidelity is not assumed; the environment must maintain whatever correspondence each model claims to the realized system.
Traceability, re-derivation, comparison, and drift detection are ways to produce that evidence.
MAGE does not replace testing and inspection with models.
**It gives their results a structure in which to mean something.**

## What should we teach?

If engineers increasingly review systems through representations and evidence rather than reconstructing every property from implementation, education should shift accordingly.
This book is not an education study, so the argument is a professional hypothesis rather than a curricular finding.
But the hypothesis is surprisingly conservative.

Most of the foundations remain.
Algorithms still matter.
Operating systems still matter.
Security arguably matters more.
Programming does not disappear, nor does software engineering.
What changes is the allocation of attention: somewhat less to producing routine implementation by hand; more to selecting abstractions, specifying properties, measuring systems, validating claims, reasoning about quality attributes, and governing automation.
Modeling, programming languages, and formal methods deserve more room—not because every engineer should become a language theorist, systems modeler, or verification specialist, but because these fields teach durable ways to choose abstractions, make invalid states harder to express, state properties precisely, and separate a claim from the evidence that establishes it.

A capstone should therefore demonstrate more than a working artifact.
Ask students to bring the **engineering case**: requirements, models, quality attributes, tradeoffs, measurements, validation evidence, governance decisions, and an explicit argument for acceptance.[^abet-capstone]
The point is not to add paperwork.
It is to teach students that the artifact is one component of the engineering claim.

The implication for program objectives is smaller than the change in daily work.
In my view, most engineering programs need little change in **what they claim an engineer should become**.
The large change is in how students practice and demonstrate those capabilities.
A sea change in engineering work may produce only a small change in the definition of an engineer.

[^abet-capstone]: Existing accreditation already asks for a culminating design experience that integrates
    standards and multiple constraints and builds on earlier coursework, with software-specific criteria
    adding requirements analysis, security, verification and validation, and processes for complex
    software systems [cite: abet-eac-criteria]; computing-accredited programs likewise require a major
    integrative project [cite: abet-cac-criteria]. The open question is not whether students should
    integrate their knowledge but what should count as evidence that they have — which is why the capstone
    should carry the engineering case, not only the demo.

## No course called Agent Tooling

One omission should be deliberate.
The core curriculum should not be rebuilt around the current agent interface.
Prompt conventions, harness architectures, model APIs, and context-management tricks matter today and students should use them, but they change too quickly to define the durable intellectual core of software engineering.

Teach the principles underneath them: finite reasoning and abstraction; action boundaries and authority; evaluation of probabilistic components; representation and model fidelity; independent evidence; security; measurement; and the design of environments in which uncertain components can be used safely.
Let students encounter current agents throughout the curriculum rather than quarantining them in a course whose title will age with the toolchain.
**If MAGE has longevity, its educational consequence has to outlast the tool of the month.**

## What the shift asks of an education

There is a harder workforce implication, best stated as a risk rather than a forecast.
Routine implementation has historically provided an accessible first rung into software work: learn a language and a framework, become useful at construction, then acquire deeper architecture and systems knowledge through practice.
If demand for routine implementation contracts, that rung may narrow.
Requirements discovery, modeling, security, systems reasoning, validation, and engineering judgment are harder to compress into a short framework-oriented path.

This is not an argument for credentialism.
Excellent engineers will continue to enter through many routes.
It is a training problem: practitioners whose expertise is concentrated in implementation need paths into architecture, assurance, modeling, and system responsibility, while new entrants need ways to acquire those foundations even if the market demands less hand-written construction from them.

The professional line is the important one: **MAGE does not replace engineering expertise with natural language. It moves the exercise of that expertise toward choosing abstractions, defining properties, evaluating evidence, allocating authority, and remaining answerable for the system.**

## From theory to research

A theory earns its keep twice: first by explaining the evidence that produced it, and again by handing the field claims that could turn out to be wrong.
Parts V and VI have done the first job as far as the available evidence permits.
They cannot settle the quantities.
How much reasoning does a model actually save?
How much human attention can a governed environment amortize?
How quickly does engineering capital repay its construction cost, and how quickly does it depreciate?
When a local engineer learns something important, what determines whether the next hundred tasks inherit the lesson?

Those are not loose ends to disguise.
They are the empirical program implied by MAGE.
The questions fall into three families: **trustworthy representations, adaptive governance, and engineering economics**.
The eight hypotheses below make the program concrete.
They are not findings of this book; they are propositions the theory offers to the field.

## Trustworthy representations

Brownfield software raises the first problem immediately: can a useful engineering model be recovered from a system that was never built around one?
An agent can extract structure from implementation, but description is not obligation.
A model that perfectly mirrors defective behavior can simply canonize the defect.
Useful induction therefore has to distinguish what the system **does** from what some independently justified engineering claim says it **should** do.

Once a model exists, maintenance becomes a second problem.
"Keep the model equal to the code" is too crude.
A model of permitted dependencies should disagree with code that violates them.
A requirements model may intentionally describe an intended state the current implementation has not reached.
The engineering problem is to state and maintain the **correspondence each model claims** — for architecture, behavior, process, invariants, lineage, or other views — cheaply enough that a meaningful divergence appears near the change that created it.

The third problem is quality.
A model can be synchronized and still be useless.
Test suites provide the familiar warning: an oracle can faithfully encode the implementation rather than the requirement, or cover one narrow realization while missing the property that matters.[^oracle-overfit]
Models need analogous evaluation.
The field must measure not only fidelity to artifacts, but whether the representation captures the obligations and relations required by the engineering decisions made through it.

**H1 — Representation leverage.** For tasks whose required reasoning state exceeds comfortable implementation-level inspection, a task-relevant and trustworthy model will reduce reconstruction/context cost or increase the scale of work completed at matched quality relative to reasoning from lower-level artifacts alone. The advantage should increase as the required reasoning state grows.

**H2 — Representation integrity.** Stale, irrelevant, or poorly chosen models will reduce or reverse the benefit in H1; measures of model correspondence and obligation coverage should therefore predict later churn or defect escape beyond conventional implementation-level metrics.

**H3 — Representation-induced determinization.** For some system-level obligations, changing from raw implementation to an explicit representation of the relevant state or relation will make a repeatable predicate feasible where per-change evaluation over the implementation otherwise requires semantic reconstruction.

[^oracle-overfit]: The failure has an empirical record. A steward of a widely watched coding benchmark
    audited the problems a strong model kept failing and found many carried flawed tests — too narrow, or
    checking behavior the task never described — alongside contamination that let exposure substitute for
    reasoning [cite: openai2026swebench]. The lesson predates the current era: automation that optimizes
    against a visible oracle satisfies the oracle while silently breaking what it does not check
    [cite: smith2015overfitting].

## Adaptive governance

Governance conversion remains primarily a judgment operation.
A failure occurs; someone decides whether it is local or structural; someone decides whether future work should inherit a model, constraint, validator, architectural change, procedure — or nothing at all.
Agents can already implement many of those responses.
The research frontier is deciding **which investment is warranted, what the cheapest adequate form is, and when the correct answer is to leave the judgment where it is**.

An adaptive environment also has to subtract.
A system that automatically responds to every anomaly with another lint or gate will manufacture bureaucracy faster than capital.
Useful adaptation must add, strengthen, reconcile, and retire.
The obligation/mechanism census from Part III offers one observable surface: important obligations with inadequate coverage are gaps; mechanisms that no longer trace to a purpose are candidate orphans.
A useful adaptive system would reduce consequential gaps without simply driving mechanism count upward.

Organizations make the problem harder.
The person who recognizes a failure may not own the shared infrastructure capable of retiring the class.
A good diagnosis can therefore die as a local patch.
The relevant organizational variable is not whether authority sits in one architect but whether a functioning path connects **diagnosis to shared environmental change**.

**H4 — Mechanized assurance.** For obligations that are sufficiently decidable and adequately covered, repeatable independent mechanisms will reduce defect escape relative to assurance that requires proportional per-change human attention; the advantage should widen as autonomous throughput increases.

**H5 — Governance conversion.** For recurring failure classes, structural interventions targeted at the class will reduce subsequent recurrence or repeated human attention relative to local repair alone, net of the intervention's carrying cost. The relationship will become non-monotonic when controls accumulate beyond their useful fit.

**H6 — Learning propagation.** Engineering lessons will reach later relevant work faster and more consistently when diagnosis is connected to authority over shared, machine-actionable environmental structure than when the lesson remains local or must propagate primarily through interpersonal communication.

## Engineering economics

The economics family is the least settled of the three.
Representation leverage and governance conversion can be observed directly within an engineering environment; estimating their economic return requires a counterfactual — what the same work would have cost without the asset, under comparable intelligence, quality, and task conditions.
Model capability is changing at the same time, token prices are moving, organizations value human attention differently, and engineering assets depreciate.
The hypotheses below therefore define quantities worth measuring, not coefficients MAGE claims to know.

The strongest economic claim in MAGE is easy to say and difficult to price: durable engineering structure can make later work cheaper or safer.
The DocAble support ratio shows where source accumulated; it does not establish that the stock was valuable.
Likewise, removing one model artifact from a well-maintained repository can fail to reveal the model's contribution because the same knowledge survives in tests, code, documentation, and other synchronized representations.
The environment is a system; its return may not decompose cleanly artifact by artifact.

The quantities that matter are therefore returns and carrying costs.
How much reconstruction, review, rework, defect risk, or recovery cost does an asset retire?
How much compute, latency, maintenance, context, false-positive burden, and coordination does it impose?
How does that balance change as the system moves and the asset depreciates?

Engineering capital is not permanent.
Software-evolution research has long observed that software used in a changing real-world environment must adapt as the world around it changes, and that continued change tends to increase complexity unless effort is spent managing it.[^lehman]
The same problem applies one level up (Section 3.4.3).
Models, validators, skills, gates, and operating procedures must evolve with the system they govern, or they become liabilities.
An engineering asset earns a return only while it remains useful enough to justify its carrying cost.

[^lehman]: Lehman's work on software evolution made the analogous observation about software itself: software used in a changing real-world environment must continually adapt, and its complexity tends to increase unless effort is spent maintaining or reducing it. MAGE extends that caution to the engineering environment surrounding the software. Models, validators, constraints, and procedures evolve too — and can become liabilities when they no longer fit what they govern. See Lehman & Ramil 2003 [cite: lehman2003evolution].

One particularly interesting implication is that effective agent capability may partly belong to the **environment**, not only to the foundation model.
A weaker model operating over a richly represented, well-instrumented environment may outperform a stronger model over an opaque repository because the environment has performed some of the reasoning before the interaction begins.
Whether environment quality substitutes for model capability, complements it, or does neither is an empirical question.

This suggests another way to operationalize engineering capital.
Instead of trying to price each model, validator, or procedure separately, treat a commodity agent as a standardized consumer of the environment.
Hold the reasoning engine and task family fixed, vary the engineering environment, and measure how much productive capability the environment contributes: task scale reached at matched quality, reconstruction cost, intervention burden, defect escape, and durable throughput.
The difference is not a complete valuation of engineering capital — humans may benefit differently, and environments carry costs of their own — but it makes otherwise intangible engineering structure empirically visible.

Software-cost estimation offers both the precedent and the warning.
Function-point methods sought implementation-independent measures of functional size [cite: albrecht1979]; Jones used such measures to compare productivity and quality across technologies and practices [cite: jones2008measurement]; COCOMO related size and project characteristics to effort and schedule [cite: boehm1981economics].
Agentic engineering may require another recalibration, but there is no basis yet for simply replacing lines of code with "model points" or any other convenient count.
The empirical task is to discover which properties of the product and the engineering environment actually predict scarce effort.

**H7 — Environment moderation.** The effect of increasing agentic capacity on durable throughput, defect escape, and human-attention burden will depend on effective environment quality: capacity gains should translate into more durable progress in stronger environments and into more churn or intervention in weaker ones.

**Outer-loop corollary.** If commodity intelligence primarily accelerates source-code production, its benefits should remain concentrated in the coding slice of software work and increasingly encounter downstream limits in review, testing, integration, and coordination. If MAGE's account is right, stronger governed engineering environments should allow the same intelligence to assume increasing portions of the surrounding realization loop—debugging, test construction, refactoring, integration, migration, and other bounded engineering activities—while preserving durable throughput without proportionate growth in human-attention burden.

**H8 — Engineering-capital return and depreciation.** Useful models, mechanisms, architectures, and procedures will produce measurable reductions in future reconstruction, human attention, rework, or risk over the surfaces they cover; those returns will decline as the assets lose fit, and environments that reconcile or retire depreciated assets will outperform otherwise comparable environments that only accumulate machinery.

The eight hypotheses divide across the three families, each paired with the core quantity a study would have to measure. [ref:mage-hypotheses] collects them.

## Study designs

The hypotheses do not require eight unrelated studies.
Three designs cover much of the program.

A **multi-project longitudinal design** should vary or observe engineering-environment quality while tracking durable throughput, defect escape, and human-attention burden over weeks or months.
The strongest version is a crossed experiment: several reasoning engines from weaker to frontier, several otherwise comparable environments from thinly to richly engineered, and the same task families across every cell.
The interaction distinguishes three possibilities.
If richer environments primarily substitute for raw model capability, weaker models should close part of the gap in stronger environments.
If the two are complementary, the strongest models should gain most from strong environments.
If environment quality contributes little, the cells should move mainly with model capability.
This design bears directly on H1, H7, and H8.

The crossed experiment should also measure the distribution of human work. Hold the task family and reasoning engine fixed; vary environment quality; then record how much human attention remains in coding, debugging, test construction, review, environment setup, integration, recovery, and other activity classes. A richer environment that merely reduces code-production time would support the narrow productivity account. An environment that shifts several classes of realization work out of repeated human execution while maintaining quality would support the broader Professional Thesis. Kumar et al.'s sixteen activity classes give a defensible existing measurement vocabulary rather than requiring MAGE to invent another taxonomy [cite: kumar2025timewarp].

The same crossed design provides an operational measure of **engineering capital**. Hold the commodity reasoner and task family approximately fixed and vary the environment. If the same intelligence can complete larger tasks, reconstruct less context, require less human intervention, escape fewer defects, or produce more durable throughput in one environment than another, the difference is evidence of productive capacity stored outside the agent. The measure is not a complete valuation—the environment also has construction and carrying costs—but it makes otherwise intangible engineering structure observable through the capability it contributes to a standardized consumer. This is the operational form of the standardized-probe idea already stated above: commodity intelligence used as a relatively standardized consumer of the environment, so the "coding slice" discussion stops being rhetorical and becomes an experiment about whether engineering environments actually move work across activity boundaries.

A **subsystem-level design** should compare engineering surfaces that differ in representation and governance while holding as much surrounding repository context constant as possible.
Track model correspondence, reconstruction effort, intervention, churn, and defect escape.
A stepped introduction of a model or mechanism is particularly useful because the same subsystem can supply its own pre-intervention baseline.
This design tests whether representation leverage, determinization, and mechanized assurance appear where the theory predicts rather than merely correlating with generally well-run projects.

An **event-centered longitudinal design** should follow one failure class through its whole life: first occurrence, repeat exposure, structural recognition, local repair, any durable conversion, propagation to later work, later recurrence, and eventual maintenance or retirement of the resulting asset.
This is the strongest way to separate "the engineer got better" from "the environment learned."
Applied across organizations, it also tests whether authority pathways determine whether a local diagnosis becomes shared engineering capital.

Across all three designs, four measurement problems recur.
**Durable throughput** needs an operational definition that discounts rework and later failure.
**Human-attention burden** has to distinguish routine supervision from genuinely scarce engineering judgment.
**Environment quality** must be measured as representation, obligation coverage, coherence, and economy rather than as control count.
And **engineering-capital return** requires both benefit and carrying cost over time.
Until those quantities can be measured credibly, support ratio, mechanism count, and repository size should remain descriptive indicators rather than proxies for maturity or value.[^deferred-variables]

[^deferred-variables]: Finer-grained candidate variables — development completeness (how much of the
    capacity to evolve a system, not just its artifact, an outsider is given), redundant-encoding density
    (how many synchronized copies of a fact a repository carries), and agent-legibility (how readily an
    agent can find and use them) — are promising, but the book does not adopt them into its canonical
    vocabulary. They belong to a later measurement paper, or a dedicated measurement appendix, not the mainline.

## Why this may now be experimentally tractable

Agentic engineering creates an unusual empirical opportunity.
Human software-engineering studies have always had to contend with substantial variation in experience, familiarity, reasoning style, and individual practice.
Agentic systems do not remove experimental variation: foundation models change, sampling is stochastic, services evolve, and model behavior can drift across releases.
But more of the reasoning substrate is now **parameterizable and repeatable** than in an ordinary study of human development work.
The same repeatability that makes agentic engineering experimentally tractable also makes engineering capital newly measurable: commodity intelligence can serve as a relatively standardized probe of how much useful reasoning and control an engineering environment has accumulated.

A researcher can hold a model version, repository state, tool surface, prompt, sampling procedure, and engineering environment relatively stable while varying one mechanism.
Repeated trials can estimate stochastic variation rather than treating it as an unobserved difference between developers.
That makes some system-scale questions more directly interventional: not only *do teams using practice X report better outcomes?*, but *what changes when representation A is replaced by representation B, or admission policy A by policy B, under otherwise matched conditions?*

Existing AI-productivity evidence already demonstrates why this distinction matters: different studies have found gains in some settings and slowdowns in others.[^productivity-evidence]
That inconsistency is not an inconvenience for this research program.
It is evidence that "AI use" is too coarse an independent variable.
Capability, task, repository, engineer, and environment all matter.

MAGE is one theory for organizing those variables, not the experimental regime itself.
Other methods and architectures will compete with it, as they should.
The larger opportunity is a cumulative experimental science of agentic software engineering in which theories make explicit predictions about the engineered environment and are compared, refined, or replaced on evidence.

[^productivity-evidence]: The record already runs both ways. A field experiment across thousands of
    developers measured a real rise in completed tasks [cite: cui2025genai], while experienced developers
    in familiar repositories ran measurably slower with early tooling even as they believed themselves
    faster [cite: metr2025productivity]. That spread is the point: agent effects are context-dependent, so
    "AI use" is too coarse a variable to test on its own.
