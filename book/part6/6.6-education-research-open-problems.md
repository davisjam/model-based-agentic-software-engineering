# Education, Research, and Open Problems

Discussion of AI and software engineering tends to collapse toward two poles. One says that
AI changes everything: implementation is delegated, natural language becomes the new
programming language, and engineers mainly need to learn agents, prompts, and harnesses. The
other says that nothing fundamental has changed: coding agents are simply another
productivity tool, and the established discipline can continue much as before.

MAGE implies a less comfortable answer for both camps. The substrate has changed enough to
reorganize the economics and daily practice of software engineering. The engineering
principles required in response are largely familiar. Requirements still have to be
discovered. Systems still need useful abstractions. Quality attributes still compete.
Concurrency still races. Security boundaries still leak. Evidence still has to justify claims.
But when implementation becomes abundant relative to judgment, those activities move toward
the center of the work, and the mechanisms that support them must operate at a different scale.

In that sense, agentic software engineering is both new and old. The new machinery changes
where engineering effort pays; the old discipline tells us much of what that effort must
accomplish. Both poles miss the same thing. "AI changes everything" mistakes a changed
implementation substrate for the disappearance of engineering. "Nothing changed" mistakes
durable engineering principles for unchanged engineering practice. **The principles survive
precisely because the practice has to change.**

The reorganization of software engineering reassigned a seat: implementation moved to the
fleet, the judgment stayed with the person, and the profession changed with it. This chapter
walks the line that leaves open, then hands the field its measurements. Professional authority
requires competence. Meaningful oversight of an agent-built system runs through models,
because at scale nothing else can carry it. So the first question is what competence we must
teach — and, before that, how a human could responsibly sign off on a system a fleet built.

## Authority follows competence

MAGE is not a shortcut around engineering knowledge. It raises the level at which that
knowledge is exercised; it does not lower the need for it. To practice it well you still
have to be an engineer — to know the foundations well enough to recognize the properties
that matter, to know how those properties are represented and checked, and to have seen
enough real systems to know where abstractions break down. An agent can construct a state
machine or extract a dependency graph. It cannot thereby decide that the state machine is
the right model, that the customer's complaint was understood, or that the resulting
tradeoff is acceptable.

Engineering authority is not merely technical. Societies entrust engineers with consequential
decisions because engineers are expected to understand the systems they certify and to stand
behind them. The institutional details differ across domains, but the principle holds:
authority follows competence and responsibility. Engineers can carry legal, professional, and
sometimes personal liability for the decisions they sign. An AI system can generate an
artifact or assemble evidence. It cannot assume the professional responsibility attached to
an engineer's judgment.

Engineering has always meant moving between levels of abstraction. The skill is not to live
at the lowest level; it is to know which level exposes the property you need, and to descend
when the current abstraction stops answering the question. Working with agents is another
version of that move. The shallow reading calls it natural-language programming: say what you
want, receive code. The engineering reading is different — you choose the properties worth
preserving, the representations that expose them, the evidence that will count, and you revise
the environment when its assumptions fail. **Natural language may be one interface to the work.
It is not the engineering method.**

## Signing off means reviewing models

Picture the future that separation implies. An agent constructs most of a system, maintains
its models, runs the validators, and assembles an engineering case for the design. The
engineer's interaction with the implementation then looks less like authorship and more like
technical review: inspect the models, challenge their assumptions, examine the evidence
attached to their claims, request analyses where the case is weak, and decide whether the
system is fit to accept.

That is a plausible direction, not a result this book establishes. But the stronger version
of the claim is not that an engineer *may* review models instead of code. It is that, for a
non-trivial agent-built system, some version of model review may be the **only** plausible
form of human sign-off. An engineer cannot establish confidence in a large unfamiliar system
by reading hundreds of thousands of lines of generated implementation. Testing does not close
the gap: tests give evidence about selected behaviors, not a comprehensible account of the
whole. Red-teaming has the same limit. Static analysis, model checking, benchmarks, and
operational measurement each produce valuable evidence — and none of it tells the engineer
what it means without a representation of the system and the obligations being weighed.

Models supply that intermediate layer. A **Structural** model makes the system's decomposition
and its permitted dependencies reviewable. A **Behavioral** model exposes the legal states and
transitions. A **Decision** model states who may do what. A **Measurement** model connects an
engineering claim to the observations meant to support it. The engineer does not reconstruct
each property from implementation — that is the work the model exists to spare. The review asks
whether the models are appropriate and faithful, whether the claimed properties are the right
ones, and whether the attached evidence is enough.

This is not an argument for trusting the models instead of the implementation. The models
themselves must be governed. Their correspondence to the shipped system is part of the
assurance case: drift checks and traceability — the system's **Documentation** held as a
governed artifact rather than left to rot — keep the reviewable representation connected to the
thing that will actually run. **MAGE does not eliminate inspection and testing. It gives those
activities somewhere to land.**

The relationship settles into four lines:

- **Models** say what we believe the system is and what properties matter.
- **Evidence** says why we believe those claims.
- **Governance** says what evidence is required, and what happens when it fails.
- **The engineer** decides whether those are the right claims, models, and standards of evidence.

Part VI has circled this ground from other sides. The reorganization chapter took the
**authoring** side, where a human stays answerable down to the code and past it, and the
**machine** side, where a gate admits only a system whose model it holds equal to the code by
construction. This chapter takes the **reviewing** side: not how a human authors a system, nor
how a machine admits one, but how a human could *accept* a system they did not author. At scale,
the model is the only surface on which they could.

Say the reason plainly, because it closes a loop the book has not quite named.
The [reasoning horizon](1.3-the-agentic-substrate.html#the-reasoning-horizon) that forces models on *agents* — a
bounded window cannot hold a large system, so the agent must reason over a compressed
representation — forces the same models on the *humans* responsible for the agents' work. A
person cannot hold five hundred thousand lines in view any more than an agent can. So Part VI
can cash [the earlier claim that models are the universal language of
engineering](6.5-software-rejoins-engineering.html) a second time. Models are not merely useful
because agents need compact representations to reason over. Humans need them too, because
agentic implementation volume makes implementation-level oversight impossible. **The same
reasoning-horizon problem that motivates models for agents eventually motivates models for the
humans responsible for the agents' work.**

## An old profession, reached late

None of this is software becoming strange. It is software engineering arriving, late, where the
older engineering disciplines have long stood — the *old* half of "both new and old" made
concrete. A civil, mechanical, aerospace, or electrical engineer already works through models
and specifications, delegates fabrication and computation, and signs off on the basis of models,
analyses, and standards-conformance rather than hand-built artifacts.

The sign-off argument above is the plainest case. A structural engineer stamps a design and
carries liability for it. They do not personally verify every weld, rivet, and bolt; they rely
on models, code and standards conformance, and validation evidence, and they stand behind the
result. That *is* model-mediated sign-off, and it long predates AI. What MAGE does is bring
software into that world — the same accountability the last section attached to an engineer's
judgment, now discharged the way older engineering has always discharged it.

The convergence is directional, not an identity, and the difference is worth keeping honest. The
older disciplines are governed by physical law: a bridge obeys statics, and a model's fidelity is
checked against nature whether or not anyone thinks to check it. Software's constraints are more
self-imposed. That gap is exactly why MAGE needs *authored* governance — the gates, drift checks,
and validators — to supply the discipline that physics gives the other fields for free. The
precedent informs the direction of travel. It does not make software engineering civil
engineering.

## What should we teach?

If engineers are to review systems through models rather than reconstruct them from generated
implementation, what must they know? MAGE supplies a reason to expect a shift in the answer; it
does not establish how to teach for it, and education research will have to determine that. This
book cannot settle it empirically — its case study is not an education study — so read what
follows as a hypothesis, not a finding. It is the academic cash-out of the skill the
reorganization chapter already named, representation engineering, stated as principles rather
than course by course.

The curricular response should be neither "teach the old curriculum unchanged" nor "replace it
with AI engineering." Surprisingly little of the traditional curriculum disappears, and that
should not surprise anyone. Its foundations were built to teach how computational systems behave
and how to engineer them, not merely how to type their implementations. What changes is the
allocation of attention: somewhat less to producing implementation by hand, more to modeling,
properties, measurement, validation, and judgment across levels of abstraction. Stated as a
principle rather than a syllabus, the charge is short: **teach students to specify, model,
evaluate evidence, reason about quality attributes, govern automation, and remain accountable
for generated implementation — then stop.**

Look at that list and the conservative result is striking: it is basically the same curriculum.
Algorithms still matters. Operating systems still matters. Security matters more, if anything.
Programming does not disappear, and neither does software engineering. The larger changes are in
emphasis — a little less weight on producing implementation by hand, more on selecting
abstractions, defining properties, measuring systems, and validating claims. These are
recognizable engineering activities whose relative value rises when implementation becomes cheap.
The one conspicuous addition is modeling and formal methods — not because every system should be
formally verified, but because engineers responsible for machine-produced systems need a
repertoire for making important properties explicit.

The culmination carries the same weight the profession does. ABET's engineering-accreditation
criteria already require a culminating major design experience that incorporates standards and
multiple constraints and builds on earlier coursework; the software-engineering criteria add
requirements analysis, security, verification and validation, and processes for complex software
systems [cite: abet-eac-criteria]. Computing-accredited computer science programs likewise require
a major project integrating knowledge acquired earlier [cite: abet-cac-criteria]. The question is
not whether students should integrate their engineering knowledge, but what should count as
evidence that they have. Read the capstone as the educational analogue of the sign-off scenario:
don't just demo the system — bring the engineering case. Models, requirements, quality attributes,
tradeoffs, measurements, validation evidence, governance, and an explicit justification for
acceptance.

ABET programs publish a short list of *Program Educational Objectives* — what graduates should
be able to do a few years out. Read against that term, the claim of this chapter has a sharp
form:

> In my opinion, no change in the Program Objectives of a curriculum should be necessary, except
> insofar as it is expedient to add AI to them.

A sea change in how engineering *works*; barely a hiccup in what an engineer should be *trained*
to be.

## No course called Agent Tooling

One omission is deliberate, and the thesis makes it a conclusion rather than a taste. If the tools
are what is fundamentally new while the durable engineering problems are not, the core curriculum
should teach those problems rather than reorganize itself around today's agent interface. So there
is no required course called AI for Software Engineers, Prompt Engineering, or Agent Tooling. Those
technologies matter, and students should use them throughout — in the introductory course, to
explore algorithms, to diagnose behavior in operating systems, to build and challenge threat models
in security, to write models in formal methods, and to run a fleet in a capstone. But agent
harnesses, context-management techniques, model interfaces, and prompting conventions change too
fast to earn a permanent slot in the core merely because they are important now. The durable
knowledge lies underneath them: why finite reasoning creates representation problems, how tools
expose action boundaries, how to evaluate probabilistic output, and how to construct environments
in which uncertain components are used safely. This is the anti-fashion test the rest of the book
keeps. If MAGE has any longevity, its educational consequence cannot be "teach the tool of the
month." It should outlast the tool of the month.

## What MAGE asks of an education

There is a less comfortable implication, and it belongs here as a risk rather than a forecast.
Implementation demand once created an accessible first rung into software work: learn a language
and a framework, become productive at construction, and acquire deeper engineering knowledge on
the job. If implementation production becomes abundant, that rung may narrow, because the work that
stays scarce — requirements discovery, architecture, modeling, systems reasoning, security,
validation, and judgment — is harder to compress into a short, framework-oriented bootcamp. That is
not credentialism, and it is not evidence that people without computing degrees cannot become
excellent engineers. It names a workforce-development problem: practitioners whose expertise is
concentrated in implementation may need routes upward into modeling, architecture, assurance, and
system responsibility, and new entrants may need longer bridges into the foundations. If the
profession's center of gravity moves, education and retraining have to move with it.

So the line to carry away is the professional one, not the curricular one:

> MAGE does not replace engineering expertise with natural language. It moves engineering
> expertise toward choosing abstractions, defining properties, evaluating evidence, and deciding
> what deserves authority.

Same foundations, a different price list, a different allocation of curricular time: the shift is
from economics, not from any failure of the foundations. **The curriculum changes less than one
might expect. The engineer's use of it changes considerably.**

## From the classroom to the field

Education names what the profession must learn. The open problems name what the field must
measure. A theory earns its keep twice — once by explaining the case that produced it, and once
by handing the field a list of things worth measuring next. This book argued from a single deep
case, seen at length. What it cannot do from one repository is settle the quantities: how far
judgment compiles into machinery, what a governed environment is worth, how a lesson propagates
once it is encoded. Those are not gaps in the argument. They are the argument running ahead of the
instruments the discipline currently knows how to build.

So the rest of this chapter is a research handoff, not a summary. It names seven open problems,
drawn from where the case ran past its own measurements, and for each it says what would make it
answerable. They sort into three families and one enabling condition. Three concern **the model** —
inducing one, keeping it honest, judging whether it is any good. Two concern **governance** —
synthesizing it, and running it across an organization rather than a repository. Two concern
**economics** — pricing a governed environment, and measuring the apparatus that sustains it.
Behind all seven sits the empirical regime that makes any of them tractable, and the chapter ends
there.

## Model induction

MAGE's claim about models is a claim about maintenance, not authorship: the model and the code
must stay equal under drift gates, whichever came first — authored and then implemented, induced
from existing code, or co-evolved. Two of those three origins are comfortable. The third is where
most software actually lives. A working system that never had a model is the common brownfield
case, and the open question is whether an agent can read it and produce a model worth keeping.

Faithful is the hard word. A model that merely encodes what the code already does inherits the
code's bugs and calls them intent — the same failure the oracle literature names, where a
generated check ratifies the implemented behavior rather than the specified one. Induction that is
worth anything has to recover *obligation*, not just *behavior*: what the system must hold,
separated from what it happens to do. That is a judgment, and today a human makes it. Whether it
can be made mechanically, at what fidelity, and how you would even score the result, is a decade of
work by itself.

The measurement hook is already visible. Ask what fraction of a system's real obligations an
induced model pins, versus how many it merely mirrors. A model with high mirror-fidelity and low
obligation-coverage is a liability dressed as an asset. The field needs a way to tell the two apart
before it can trust anything an agent induces.

## Model drift

The comparative matrix isolated one rung that no external case reached: a deterministic gate that
*blocks* an edit until the model and the implementation agree. The most mature industrial case
comes closest and stops short — its digital thread synchronizes and propagates across domains, but
the sources describe no gate that refuses an agent's change when the change breaks parity. That
single rung is MAGE's most distinctive move, and it is also the least understood as a general
technique.

The problem is that parity is easy to state and hard to generalize. A drift check over one view —
does the state machine in the code match the one in the model? — is tractable. A drift relation
that spans the whole Model Zoo, behavioral and process and invariant and lineage views at once,
cheap enough to run on every change and precise enough to name *which* fact diverged, is an open
engineering problem. At fleet scale drift is not an event; it is a constant pressure, because every
agent edit is a chance for the map and the territory to part. A gate that holds them equal without
becoming the bottleneck the whole method exists to avoid is the thing to build and measure.

The quantity to report is the drift-gate catch rate: of the divergences a fleet introduces, what
share does the gate stop before they land? No study has measured it, because outside this case few
environments have the gate to measure.

## Model quality

A model is a representation of intent; a test suite is a model of correctness. Both are fallible,
and the field has just been reminded how fallible at a scale it could not ignore. A steward of the
most-watched coding benchmark audited the problems a strong model kept failing and found more than
half carried flawed tests — too narrow, enforcing one implementation; too wide, checking behavior
the task never described — alongside contamination signatures that let the benchmark reward exposure
rather than reasoning [cite: openai2026swebench]. The lesson is older than the LLM era: automation
that optimizes against a visible oracle produces artifacts that satisfy the oracle while silently
breaking what it does not check [cite: smith2015overfitting]. The green bar was always a proxy, and
proxies overfit under pressure.

Agents sharpen it, because agents now write the oracles too. They add mocks at a higher rate than
human authors, and they tend to encode the implemented behavior rather than the specified one, so
the suite they generate ratifies the code's bugs. A passing suite is a fallible model of
correctness that must itself be governed. The open problem is how — and it runs at two scales that
share mechanisms but not scope. **Benchmark-oracle governance** keeps a whole field's shared
yardstick honest: contamination-resistant collection, live re-derivation, staleness controls.
**Repository-oracle governance** keeps one project's own controls faithful to its requirements: an
obligation census, property and fuzz tests against a stable spec point, a standing distrust of
anything an agent asserts about its own output.

Three measurands would turn the concern into a program. What fraction of a task's requirements are
pinned by a control versus merely asserted by an example. Contamination- and overfitting-resistant
evaluation built as standing infrastructure rather than a one-off refresh. And a mechanical
detector for oracle degradation — over-mocking, implementation-coupling — sitting in the review path
the way a structural lint already does. None of the three is solved. Each is fundable now.

## Governance synthesis

Governance conversion, as the book describes it, is a human operation. A failure recurs; an
engineer classifies it; the engineer chooses a durable mechanism from a closed menu — a model, a
constraint, a sensor, a validator, a gate, an interface, an architecture, or nothing at all — and
installs it. The move is repeatable enough to name, which raises the obvious next question: can the
environment perform it on itself? Can agents be taught to notice inadequate governance and convert
a failure into structure, rather than waiting for a human to do it by hand every time?

The mechanical half looks close. An agent can already write a lint, add a test, tighten a schema.
The judgment half is the frontier. Which gap merits a mechanism and which should stay a known
silence; which mechanism is the *cheapest adequate* one rather than the most elaborate; when the
right answer is no mechanism at all. Get that judgment wrong in the direction of zeal and you have
built the other failure the book warns about — an estate that accumulates controls forever and
charges its own carrying cost. A synthesizer that only adds is a debt engine. One that could also
reconcile and retire is a research object nobody has built.

What makes this measurable is the census. It already turns governance into two counts — gaps where
an obligation has no mechanism, orphans where a mechanism has no obligation. A governance
synthesizer is exactly a system that tries to drive gaps down without driving orphans up. That is a
control loop with an observable error signal, which is the precondition for studying it at all.

## Economics of engineering capital

The book claims that converted failures compound — a mechanism authored once pays on every later
change, and the environment banks the saving as engineering capital. The claim is easy to state and
treacherous to price, and the case study has the negative result to prove it. A per-task ablation
stripped a model artifact from a well-tended repository and looked for the recall it cost. It found
none. The redundant, synchronized copies the governance method deliberately maintains restored the
answer through the code, the tests, the comments, the docs. Recall collapsed toward zero *regardless
of what the map was worth*, because a healthy garden leaves no single-source fact for a strip to
remove. The instrument met the garden and the garden won.

The lesson is not that the environment is worthless. It is that its value does not live at the scale
a per-task strip can see. It lives at the macro scale — whole environments set against whole
environments — and that is where the measurement has to move. The theory offers a concrete,
disprovable bet to aim it: that repository quality may substitute, at the margin, for model
capability, because a rich environment performs some of the reasoning before the agent begins.
Whether that substitution holds, and how far, is the open economic question. A crossed design
answers it: several models from weaker to frontier, several repositories from thinly to richly
governed, the same reasoning engine run in every cell, and the interaction term read off the result.
A substitution effect lifts a weaker model in a rich environment toward a stronger model's cell. A
complementary effect shows the two multiplying. Either outcome is a mechanism-level finding the
field cannot currently produce, and neither has been measured.

## Measuring the support ratio

In the system this book draws from, the apparatus that governs the code — the tests, the lints, the
tooling, the models — outweighed the production code it governs. That ratio is the most concrete
face of engineering capital, and it is almost entirely unstudied. Is a support apparatus several
times the size of the product a sign of a mature environment banking assurance that manual review
cannot scale to provide, or a sign of an estate bloated with machinery that has outlived its reason?
The census gives the *sign* of any single mechanism — governed or orphan — but nobody has measured
the *quantity* across a real environment over time, or asked what a healthy trajectory looks like.

Two constructs hide inside the ratio and should not be merged. **Redundant-encoding density** is how
many synchronized copies of a fact the repository carries; it predicts resilience to the loss of any
one artifact, and it is what flattened the ablation above. **Agent-legibility** is how readily an
agent can find and use those copies, and redundancy can *cut* it when the copies drift or add noise.
A repository can be dense and illegible at once. Both are candidate environment-quality metrics, and
measuring them apart is part of the work.

The outcome that ties it together is governed throughput, not implementation volume — how much
*durable* progress an environment sustains per unit of agent effort over weeks and months, rather
than how many lines a fleet emits in an afternoon. That is the number the whole method claims to
move, and it is precisely the number the field has no standard way to report.

## Organizational governance

The single case is one engineer. It cannot say how a hundred coordinate around a fleet, and the
industrial layer showed the organization does not stay the same shape when it tries. Mechanisms
centralize while judgment decentralizes; scarce human decisions move upstream; private learning has
to become a shared artifact or it is waste; the unit of governance grows from the task to the fleet.
Each of those is an observation in want of a dynamics.

Two questions matter most. The first is propagation: once a lesson is encoded into shared,
machine-actionable structure, how does it actually reach later work, and how long does it take?
Propagation latency is a metric the book proposes and does not yet measure. The second is authority,
and it is sharper than it looks. Whether the person who *sees* a failure holds the authority to
*change the environment* is the difference between a mechanism that dies with one incident and a
failure class that never dies. That is an organizational design variable, not a technical one, and
it decides whether governance conversion happens at all.

Behind both sits the openness question the environment forces. As effective capability moves out of
the source tree and into models, skills, validators, policies, and context machinery, a larger
fraction of what lets a team evolve a system quickly comes to sit outside the code it ships. Source
without the governed environment is forkable code that does not reproduce the loop through which the
upstream fleet reliably changes it. So the field inherits a new axis — call it development
completeness — asking not whether the artifact is open but how much of the capacity to evolve it has
been shared. Which environment artifacts belong to the software supply chain, what an outsider must
be given to reproduce not the system but the ability to change it safely, and how much assurance a
regulated system can draw as a by-product of a model-rich environment rather than a certification
bolted on at the end: three open questions the book raises and deliberately leaves standing.

## Three ways to study it

The seven problems each carry their own measurement hook, but they are not seven separate studies.
Three study shapes would cover most of the model, and none needs this repository. A multi-project
longitudinal panel with environment-quality variation tests whether richer environments buy durable
progress — the same crossed design the economics problem above spells out. A subsystem-level panel,
tracking where models are missing, where churn concentrates, and where failures escape, tests the
discovery and drift loops. And an organizational comparison of how diagnosis and authority flow
tests whether the person who sees a failure can change the environment. A particularly strong
instrument would follow one failure class through its whole life — first occurrence, structural
recognition, the governance intervention, and later recurrence or escape — rather than leaning on
aggregate repository metrics that cannot separate "the environment matured" from "the operator did."

## What makes the list answerable

Set these seven beside each other and the same enabling condition sits under all of them. Five years
ago none was tractable, for one reason: the experimental subject would not hold still. Empirical
software engineering spent decades trying to measure interventions whose effects were small beside
the variation in who did the work, and randomizing around that variation rarely showed an effect
bigger than the spread between two developers. The noise came from the people.

What changed is not that intelligence got cheaper. It got *standardized*. A foundation model is one
subject a study can instantiate as many times as it has conditions, pinned to a version, its prompts
archived, its sampling variance estimated by repetition rather than assumed away. Hold the reasoning
engine fixed across arms and, for the first time, a researcher can fix one major source of cognitive
variation instead of randomizing around it. That single move turns system-scale questions from
observational to interventional — not *does practice X correlate with outcome Y across the teams we
could survey*, but *what happens to the outcome if governance mechanism A is replaced by mechanism
B* — and it makes the engineered environment a first-class experimental object alongside the people
and organizations that were always there. It also makes replication cheap: rerun the same model over
the same prompts in the same environment, rather than re-recruiting a matched population of
developers. Cheap replication is the precondition for a cumulative science.

The evidence for the shift is already on the record, and it does not all point one way — a field
experiment across thousands of developers measured a real rise in completed tasks, while experienced
developers in familiar repositories ran measurably *slower* with early tooling even as they believed
themselves faster [cite: cui2025genai] [cite: metr2025productivity]. A standardized input is one a
study can assign, not one that always helps, and that is exactly what makes it useful as a variable
to fix.

MAGE is one proposal for organizing this regime. It is not the regime, and it is not the last word
on any of the seven problems above; other methods, architectures, and governance approaches will
emerge, and the opportunity outlives all of them. The claim worth making is the broad one. Agentic
software engineering may finally offer interventions whose effects are large enough to see, explicit
enough to reproduce, and structured enough to build theory on. Whether MAGE proves correct matters
less than whether the field takes the opening — and treats agentic software engineering as an
experimental science whose theories can be proposed, compared, refined, and, on the evidence,
replaced.
