# Where the Theory Holds

A useful theory says where it stops. MAGE imposes real costs: models have to be built and maintained;
controls consume compute and attention; synchronization can fail; every new mechanism becomes another
thing the environment must carry. Those costs are justified only where the resulting structure buys
something back.

The right unit of analysis is usually **an obligation or engineering surface, not an industry label**. The
same scientific system may have highly governable build and reproducibility machinery around a kernel
whose scientific validity remains a matter of expert judgment. A startup may have little reason to model
its whole architecture while still needing a hard security boundary around payments. MAGE therefore
transfers unevenly even inside one repository.

## Two tests and one organizational condition

**Does the investment repay?** Recurrence is the usual source of return. If the same architectural
knowledge, review judgment, or failure class will be encountered hundreds of times, externalizing it once
can avoid purchasing the same reasoning repeatedly. But recurrence is not the only justification. A rare
obligation with catastrophic consequences may warrant extensive modeling and assurance because avoiding
one failure repays the investment. The economic question is therefore broader: **does the expected
reduction in future reasoning, risk, or recovery cost justify the construction and carrying cost of the
asset?**

**Can the obligation be governed adequately?** Some properties can be represented precisely and settled
mechanically: a schema conforms, a dependency is permitted, an invariant holds. Others admit statistical
evidence but not a definitive predicate. Still others depend substantially on aesthetic, scientific,
political, or social judgment. Governability is the degree to which an important obligation can be made
explicit, supplied with relevant evidence, and evaluated with adequate reliability and acceptable cost.
Deterministic checking is its strongest region, not its definition.

One organizational condition determines whether lessons can become shared assets: **authority**. Someone
who discovers a structural problem must have a functioning path to the architecture, models, controls, or
shared infrastructure that could address it. The authority need not sit in one person. It has to connect
diagnosis to the environment. Without that path, the organization can understand a failure perfectly and
still patch it locally forever.

## What the evidence can bound

The method was induced from one unusually deep longitudinal case. That gives the book strong within-case
evidence about sequence and mechanism and weak evidence about prevalence. DocAble was led by one primary
engineer with unusual authority over code, models, tooling, and infrastructure; it used frontier closed
models whose capabilities changed during the study; and it has no matched conventional-team
counterfactual. Repository-specific quantities such as support ratio and line motion therefore describe
this system rather than supplying portable coefficients.

The industrial reconstructions later in this Part add variation but not experimental control. They show
independent organizations adopting related engineering structures under different names and constraints;
they can corroborate a mechanism, expose another operationalization, or reveal an important boundary.
Public practitioner accounts cannot establish causation, and silence in those reports cannot establish
organizational absence. Taken together, the two sources support **analytic generalization**: asking whether
the same explanatory structure helps us understand materially different systems, not estimating how often
the practice occurs in a population.

## Typical profiles, not domain verdicts

The return varies by surface within a system, so read [ref:mage-typical-profiles] as a set of typical
profiles rather than verdicts on whole domains. A long-lived embedded system and a two-week embedded
prototype share a label and sit in different places; a tiny startup may still owe one payment obligation a
deterministic control.

The pattern is economic rather than categorical. Long-lived systems offer repeated opportunities to
amortize representation and assurance. High-consequence systems can justify the same investment even at
lower recurrence. Mixed domains divide internally: scientific software may support strong governance
around reproducibility and deployment while leaving scientific validity with domain experts; ML systems
can govern their pipelines and serving infrastructure much more strongly than the question "is this
learned model good?"

The opposite edge is equally useful. Do not build a cathedral of governance around work whose relevant
reasoning will never recur and whose consequences do not justify the investment. Use the cheapest adequate
mechanism. A prototype may need only tests around one dangerous boundary. If it becomes a long-lived
estate, the economics change before the label does.

## Two ways an investment fails

MAGE becomes a bad bargain in two broad ways. **The asset never earns a return:** the knowledge is not
reused, the risk is too small, or the machinery costs more to maintain than the judgment it replaces. Or
**the important property resists adequate governance:** it cannot be represented, evidenced, or evaluated
strongly enough to justify moving authority out of human judgment.

Most real systems contain both kinds of surface. The practical skill is therefore not deciding whether a
project "uses MAGE." It is deciding **which properties deserve durable representation or authority, how
strong the evidence should be, and which decisions should remain human.**

## The boundary is the theory

Naming where MAGE does not apply is not a retreat from the claim; it is the claim, stated with edges. A
method that paid everywhere would be a slogan: unfalsifiable, and therefore uninformative. The edges let a
reader predict. Bring MAGE to a long-lived, governable surface with a functioning path from diagnosis to
the shared environment, and the reasoning you externalize compounds into capital. Bring it to a throwaway,
a young search for product-market fit, or a property whose important obligations resist evaluation, and
you have paid for an apparatus with nothing to amortize.

Whether that structure recurs outside one project is the next question. The chapters that follow read
independent industrial practice for the same engineering moves, and let the comparison sharpen and bound
the theory.
