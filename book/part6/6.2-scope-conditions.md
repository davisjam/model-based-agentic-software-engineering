# Where the Theory Holds

A theory earns the name by stating where it stops. The [theory chapter](6.1-toward-a-theory-of-mage.html)
called MAGE middle-range and meant it precisely: not a universal law that every software project must
obey, and not raw description of one system, but a mechanism bounded by conditions worth naming. This
chapter names them. It draws two boundaries — the limits of the evidence the book rests on, and the
domains where the method pays for itself against the domains where it is overhead. The boundary is not an
apology. It is what separates a theory from a slogan.

The deciding variable is the book's own economics. MAGE's apparatus — the models a fleet reasons through,
the mechanisms that hold intent binding, the governance conversions that bank engineering capital — is an
up-front investment. Like any capital, it pays only when there is a recurring cost to amortize it against.
The recurring cost here is **reasoning**: the effort of understanding a system well enough to change it
safely, paid again on every change, by every agent. Where that cost is large and recurs, externalizing it
into an environment is cheaper than paying it fresh each time, and the investment compounds. Where the
cost is paid once and discarded, the environment never amortizes, and the same apparatus that compounds in
a long-lived estate is dead weight on a throwaway one.

## Two gates

Two conditions decide whether the investment pays, and a domain has to clear both.

- **Does the reasoning cost recur?** Engineering capital compounds only against a future that reuses it. A
  large, long-lived, many-hands system pays the understanding cost on every change, so structure that
  lowers it repays many times over. A one-off script pays it once. The question is not the domain's
  prestige but its longevity and change rate — how many times the same understanding must be reconstructed.
- **Is the domain governable?** The theory chapter names this the **domain-governability moderator**: the
  degree to which a domain's important properties can be represented and mechanically checked at all. Some
  properties are precisely specifiable — a schema conforms, an invariant holds, an output validates. Others
  are aesthetic, statistical, or socially negotiated, and no mechanism settles them. Where governability is
  low, the governed environment has less to grip, and more of the work stays in human judgment no apparatus
  can carry.

A third condition, weaker but real, is **authority**: a functioning path from an observed failure to the
shared infrastructure that would kill its class. The theory chapter treats authority as a moderator on the
diagnosis-to-adaptation step; here it surfaces as a scope condition on organizations, not domains. A team
with no seat that owns shared architecture cannot convert a recurring failure into anything durable — the
diagnosis dies as a local patch, and the class survives. That gate is why the same codebase can be inside
MAGE's reach at one organization and outside it at another.

## What one case can bound

Before the domains, the evidence. Everything this book claims rests on one system, authored by me, built
inside one vendor's agent ecosystem, deployed in production and used by me and my colleagues at Purdue
University. Every claim stays scoped to that case, and the honest way to read the results is to hold the
conditions of their making in view.

- **A single operator with unusual authority, expertise, and time.** One person held every role — author
  of the code, the governance, the models, and this book — and could change any of them at will, knew the
  domain deeply, and spent a volume of hours few could or should repeat. Each of those raises the ceiling
  on what governed autonomy reached, and none is typical. A team splits those roles across people who must
  negotiate, and nothing in my own case tested that friction.
- **Frontier models, moving tools.** The fleet ran on the strongest closed models available, and the
  substrate shifted under me across the build. The results may not hold on weaker or open models, and a
  practice that paid off one month need not the next.
- **No counterfactual.** I never ran the same work with a conventional team, so nothing here isolates the
  method's contribution from the operator's, the domain's, or the model's. The case describes; it does not
  experiment. Whether the compounding survives a change of hands, over years, I do not yet know.
- **Metrics defined against this repository.** The code-size and churn figures are measured against this
  project's conventions. They report a direction, not a coefficient that ports to a project that counts
  differently.

These are limits on transfer, not defects in the account — the terms on which someone else should read it.
What study designs would close each gap is the subject of [Open Problems](6.6-education-research-open-problems.html); here the
point is narrower. One deep case can generate constructs and mechanisms and show how they compose. It
cannot fix the universal coefficients a middle-range theory deliberately declines to claim.

## A map of domains

The method transfers unevenly across software itself, and the two gates say where. [ref:mage-domains]
places nine settings against them. Read it as a first cut, not a verdict on any particular project: a
long-lived embedded system and a two-week embedded prototype sit in different rows though they share a
label.

**Where the method is load-bearing.** The fits share a shape: a large, long-lived system whose important
properties can be checked.

- **Brownfield estates and web services** are the home ground, and DocAble is one of each — a web service
  retrofitted from an existing codebase. The understanding cost is high and recurs on every change; schemas,
  API contracts, tests, deploy gates, and observability make the important properties mechanically
  checkable; and the system lives long enough for each conversion to repay. This is the intersection the
  economics reward, and it is where the whole apparatus of this book was built.
- **Embedded systems** clear both gates when they are long-lived: timing, memory, and protocol conformance
  are precisely checkable, and the codebase outlives many changes. The honest caveat is on the first gate's
  premise. MAGE assumes implementation is the cheap input; in embedded work the loop is throttled by
  hardware-in-the-loop testing and slow flashes, so agentic velocity is bounded by the physical iteration
  rate, not the model. The environment still pays — it just cannot run as fast as it does on a web service.
- **Safety-critical software** is where governability is highest, because certification regimes already
  mandate the mechanically checkable properties MAGE would otherwise have to invent — traceability,
  coverage, deterministic evidence. The alignment discipline of encoding obligations into gates fits a
  world that already demands them. The caveat is authority, relocated: final admission to a certified build
  is an external regulatory act, so the governed environment carries the engineering but does not own the
  last gate. A larger share of the work stays deliberately in human hands by mandate, not by weakness of
  the method.

**Where the method splits.** Two domains clear one gate cleanly and fail the other by layer, so the verdict
is not yes or no but *which part*.

- **High-performance and scientific computing** live for decades, so the recurrence gate is met and the
  surrounding machinery — build systems, regression harnesses, reproducibility, numerical tolerances —
  governs well and repays. The inner numerical kernels resist it. Whether a simulation is *scientifically*
  valid, whether a floating-point reformulation preserves the physics, is often a domain judgment no
  mechanical check settles. Govern the scaffolding; hold the kernel.
- **ML systems** split the same way, more sharply. The engineering around a model — data pipelines, feature
  stores, training orchestration, eval harnesses, serving infrastructure — is ordinary long-lived governable
  software, and MAGE pays there exactly as it does on any web service. The model itself is the low-governability
  core. "Is this model good? Is the training data right?" rests on properties that are statistical and
  emergent, where a metric can be optimized without the underlying quality following. Govern the ML
  engineering; the judgment about the model stays where the domain-governability moderator predicts — with
  a person.

**Where the method is overhead.** The failures are cleaner than the fits, because they fail the first gate
outright: the reasoning cost does not recur enough to amortize anything.

- **One-off scripts** are the floor. The understanding is paid once and thrown away with the script. There
  is no future for engineering capital to pay into, so every model, lint, and gate is pure cost. Write the
  script and move on.
- **Small startups with no platform team** are the case flagged in the Field Guide ([appendix: appendix-field-guide]): the method will not help a
  fifty-thousand-line codebase with no platform team. Two gates fail at once. The reasoning cost has not
  begun to recur — the codebase is small and the product is still being searched for — and there is no
  authority seat that owns shared infrastructure, so a diagnosis has nowhere to become durable. The
  binding constraint is finding product-market fit, not governing throughput, and spending the founding
  months building an environment starves the search. Adopt the cheapest slices — a few tests, one lint that
  guards the thing that keeps breaking — and defer the apparatus until scale, longevity, and a platform
  seat arrive.
- **Greenfield** is the honest middle, and the label misleads. A greenfield prototype that stays a
  prototype is a one-off; a greenfield system that will grow large and long-lived is a brownfield estate
  that has not calcified yet, and the cheapest time to build the environment is before the estate hardens
  around ungoverned habits. The deciding variable is never the age of the code. It is whether the reasoning
  cost has begun to recur, and whether it will keep recurring long enough to repay the investment.

## Two ways the theory runs out

Step back and the map has a structure. The method runs out for exactly two reasons, one per gate, and every
domain above is an instance of one or the other.

- **The reasoning cost never recurs.** One-off scripts, throwaway prototypes, small young startups. Nothing
  is built many times, so nothing amortizes, and the apparatus is a tax on work that would have been cheaper
  done plainly. This is the economic edge: engineering capital is worthless where there is no recurring cost
  to lower.
- **The domain is not governable.** The scientific validity of a simulation, the quality of a trained model,
  the voice of a piece of writing. The important property cannot be represented and mechanically checked, so
  the governed environment has nothing to grip, and the work stays in judgment. This is the moderator's edge:
  alignment can only make binding what modeling could make explicit.

Most real systems sit inside both edges on their bulk and outside on a core, which is why the honest verdict
for a large system is rarely all-or-nothing. Govern the layers whose properties check; hold the layers whose
properties do not. The skill the method asks for is telling which is which.

> ### Field note — Why is this book's voice mine, not the fleet's? {#fieldnote-authorship-voice}
>
> The sharpest boundary I drew is personal, and it is an instance of the second edge above. I wrote this
> book the way I wrote the code — with structured models of its own structure, and a drift gate holding
> those models to the prose. An LLM assisted with the first draft. The final version is entirely mine:
> every sentence rewritten in my own voice, and that rewrite is where I stopped the method.
>
> **My boundary for this book is authorship.** I am willing to delegate analytical implementation under
> governed checks, but not the final voice through which I address a reader. Writing for other people is
> human-to-human communication, and voice is part of the message; it does not seem right to ask you to read
> my ideas at one remove, mediated by a machine that is not me. That is a choice about *this* book, made on
> my values — not a claim about what code or prose fundamentally are.
>
> It is tempting to draw the line as "code has no voice," but that is false. Code carries style, naming,
> pedagogical intent, aesthetic character. The honest distinction is narrower and about *verifiability*:
> many critical properties of code — correctness, performance, security — admit executable or analytic
> validation to a degree authorial voice does not. Governed autonomy transfers most cleanly where a property
> can be checked, and writing for other people has a large share that cannot. When you set your own
> boundary, ask which properties of your artifact are checkable and which rest on judgment or voice.
> Delegate under checks where you can verify; hold the rest yourself.

## The boundary is the theory

Naming where MAGE does not apply is not a retreat from the claim. It is the claim, stated with edges. A
method that promised to pay everywhere would be a slogan, unfalsifiable and therefore uninformative. The
edges are what let a reader predict: bring MAGE to a large, long-lived, governable system with a seat that
owns its infrastructure, and the reasoning cost you externalize will compound into capital; bring it to a
one-off, a young search for product-market fit, or a domain whose important properties resist checking, and
you will have paid for an apparatus that had nothing to amortize. The theory holds inside its scope
conditions. It says so, because that is what makes it a theory.
