# Educating the MAGE Engineer

Discussion of AI and software engineering tends to collapse toward two poles. One says that
AI changes everything: implementation is delegated, natural language becomes the new
programming language, and engineers mainly need to learn agents, prompts, and harnesses. The
other says that nothing fundamental has changed: coding agents are simply another
productivity tool, and the established discipline can continue much as before.

MAGE implies a less comfortable answer for both camps. The substrate has changed enough to
reorganize the economics and daily practice of software engineering. The engineering
principles required in response are largely familiar. Requirements still have to be
discovered. Systems still need useful abstractions. Quality attributes still compete.
Concurrency still races. Security boundaries still leak. Evidence still has to justify claims.
But when implementation becomes abundant relative to judgment, those activities move toward
the center of the work, and the mechanisms that support them must operate at a different scale.

In that sense, agentic software engineering is both new and old. The new machinery changes
where engineering effort pays; the old discipline tells us much of what that effort must
accomplish. Both poles miss the same thing. "AI changes everything" mistakes a changed
implementation substrate for the disappearance of engineering. "Nothing changed" mistakes
durable engineering principles for unchanged engineering practice. **The principles survive
precisely because the practice has to change.**

The last chapter reassigned a seat: implementation moved to the fleet, the judgment stayed
with the person, and [the profession changed with it](6.5-implications-for-se.html). This
chapter walks the line that leaves open. Professional authority requires competence.
Meaningful oversight of an agent-built system runs through models, because at scale nothing
else can carry it. So the last question is what competence we must teach — and, before that,
how a human could responsibly sign off on a system a fleet built.

## Authority follows competence

MAGE is not a shortcut around engineering knowledge. It raises the level at which that
knowledge is exercised; it does not lower the need for it. To practice it well you still
have to be an engineer — to know the foundations well enough to recognize the properties
that matter, to know how those properties are represented and checked, and to have seen
enough real systems to know where abstractions break down. An agent can construct a state
machine or extract a dependency graph. It cannot thereby decide that the state machine is
the right model, that the customer's complaint was understood, or that the resulting
tradeoff is acceptable.

That distinction matters because engineering authority is not merely technical. Societies
entrust engineers with consequential decisions because engineers are expected to understand
the systems they certify and to stand behind them. The institutional details differ across
domains, but the principle holds: authority follows competence and responsibility. Engineers
can carry legal, professional, and sometimes personal liability for the decisions they sign.
An AI system can generate an artifact or assemble evidence. It cannot assume the professional
responsibility attached to an engineer's judgment.

Engineering has always meant moving between levels of abstraction. Most application engineers
rarely reason about cache lines until a performance problem forces them down; most never touch
machine code, though a compiler writer must. The skill is not to live at the lowest level. It
is to know which level exposes the property you need, and to descend when the current
abstraction stops answering the question. Working with agents is another version of that move.
The shallow reading calls it natural-language programming: say what you want, receive code. The
engineering reading is different — you choose the properties worth preserving, the
representations that expose them, the evidence that will count, and you revise the environment
when its assumptions fail. **Natural language may be one interface to the work. It is not the
engineering method.**

## Signing off means reviewing models

Picture the future that separation implies. An agent constructs most of a system, maintains
its models, runs the validators, and assembles an engineering case for the design. The
engineer's interaction with the implementation then looks less like authorship and more like
technical review: inspect the models, challenge their assumptions, examine the evidence
attached to their claims, request analyses where the case is weak, and decide whether the
system is fit to accept.

That is a plausible direction, not a result this book establishes. But the stronger version
of the claim is not that an engineer *may* review models instead of code. It is that, for a
non-trivial agent-built system, some version of model review may be the **only** plausible
form of human sign-off. An engineer cannot establish confidence in a large unfamiliar system
by reading hundreds of thousands of lines of generated implementation. Testing does not close
the gap: tests give evidence about selected behaviors, not a comprehensible account of the
whole. Red-teaming has the same limit. Static analysis, model checking, benchmarks, and
operational measurement each produce valuable evidence — and none of it tells the engineer
what it means without a representation of the system and the obligations being weighed.

Models supply that intermediate layer. A **Structural** model makes the system's decomposition
and its permitted dependencies reviewable. A **Behavioral** model exposes the legal states and
transitions. A **Decision** model states who may do what. A **Measurement** model connects an
engineering claim to the observations meant to support it. The engineer does not reconstruct
each property from implementation — that is the work the model exists to spare. The review asks
whether the models are appropriate and faithful, whether the claimed properties are the right
ones, and whether the attached evidence is enough.

This is not an argument for trusting the models instead of the implementation. The models
themselves must be governed. Their correspondence to the shipped system is part of the
assurance case: drift checks and traceability — the system's **Documentation** held as a
governed artifact rather than left to rot — keep the reviewable representation connected to the
thing that will actually run. **MAGE does not eliminate inspection and testing. It gives those
activities somewhere to land.**

The relationship settles into four lines:

- **Models** say what we believe the system is and what properties matter.
- **Evidence** says why we believe those claims.
- **Governance** says what evidence is required, and what happens when it fails.
- **The engineer** decides whether those are the right claims, models, and standards of evidence.

Part VI has already circled this ground from other sides, and the chapter earns its place by
naming the side the others left open. The profession chapter observed that on a large change
"[a human can follow the model where the diff is
unreadable](6.5-implications-for-se.html)" — the payoff while the work is *active*. It argued
that an engineer stays "[answerable down to the code and past
it](6.5-implications-for-se.html)" — the **authoring** side, where accountability is exercised.
It showed that governance conversion lets you "[convert a failure class
once](6.5-implications-for-se.html)" so later instances screen without re-inspection, and that
the environment holds "[a model it holds equal to the code by
construction](6.5-implications-for-se.html)" — a machine gate on admission. This chapter takes
the **reviewing** side: not how a human authors a system, nor how a machine admits one, but how
a human could *accept* a system they did not author. At scale, the model is the only surface on
which they could.

Say the reason plainly, because it closes a loop the book has not quite named.
The [reasoning horizon](1.3-the-reasoning-horizon.html) that forces models on *agents* — a
bounded window cannot hold a large system, so the agent must reason over a compressed
representation — forces the same models on the *humans* responsible for the agents' work. A
person cannot hold five hundred thousand lines in view any more than an agent can. So Part VI
can cash [the earlier claim that models are the universal language of
engineering](6.4-engineering-beyond-software.html) a second time. Models are not merely useful
because agents need compact representations to reason over. Humans need them too, because
agentic implementation volume makes implementation-level oversight impossible. **The same
reasoning-horizon problem that motivates models for agents eventually motivates models for the
humans responsible for the agents' work.**

## An old profession, reached late

None of this is software becoming strange. It is software engineering arriving, late, where the
older engineering disciplines have long stood — the *old* half of "both new and old" made
concrete. A civil, mechanical, aerospace, or electrical engineer already works through models
and specifications, delegates fabrication and computation, and signs off on the basis of models,
analyses, and standards-conformance rather than hand-built artifacts.

The sign-off argument above is the plainest case. A structural engineer stamps a design and
carries liability for it. They do not personally verify every weld, rivet, and bolt; they rely
on models, code and standards conformance, and validation evidence, and they stand behind the
result. That *is* model-mediated sign-off, and it long predates AI. What MAGE does is bring
software into that world — the same accountability the last section attached to an engineer's
judgment, now discharged the way older engineering has always discharged it.

The curriculum lands the same way. Other engineering programs already teach the balance the
next section proposes: foundations in mathematics and physics, then modeling — CAD, finite-element
analysis, control theory, simulation — then professional judgment, standards, and a culminating
design capstone. Their program objectives already center models and judgment. So software
engineering's shift is convergence toward the long-standing shape of engineering education, not
a new invention.

The convergence is directional, not an identity, and the difference is worth keeping honest. The
older disciplines are governed by physical law: a bridge obeys statics, and a model's fidelity is
checked against nature whether or not anyone thinks to check it. Software's constraints are more
self-imposed. That gap is exactly why MAGE needs *authored* governance — the gates, drift checks,
and validators — to supply the discipline that physics gives the other fields for free. The
precedent informs the direction of travel. It does not make software engineering civil
engineering.

## What should we teach?

If engineers are to review systems through models rather than reconstruct them from generated
implementation, what must they know? MAGE supplies a reason to expect a shift in the answer; it
does not establish how to teach for it, and education research will have to determine that. This
book cannot settle it empirically — its case study is not an education study — so read what
follows as a hypothesis, not a finding. It is the academic cash-out of the skill the profession
chapter already named: [representation engineering](6.5-implications-for-se.html), stated course
by course.

The curricular response should be neither "teach the old curriculum unchanged" nor "replace it
with AI engineering." The foundations remain recognizable; what changes is their emphasis and the
level at which students exercise them. Surprisingly little of the traditional curriculum
disappears, and that should not surprise anyone. Its foundations were built to teach how
computational systems behave and how to engineer them, not merely how to type their
implementations. What changes is the allocation of attention: somewhat less to producing
implementation by hand, more to modeling, properties, measurement, validation, and judgment
across levels of abstraction. [ref:mage-curriculum] sketches the shift. Read the MAGE column as a
claim about emphasis, not a proposed accreditation standard.

<!-- label: mage-curriculum -->
| Course | Traditional emphasis | MAGE-era emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| CS 1 / Programming | Construct programs; syntax, control flow, decomposition, debugging | Retain programming fluency; add reading, modifying, critiquing, and validating generated implementation; distinguish intent from implementation |
| Discrete Mathematics | Logic, sets, relations, graphs, induction, combinatorics | More central: foundations for properties, invariants, state, models, reachability, and evidence |
| Data Structures & Algorithms | Representation, algorithms, complexity, implementation | Reason about data structures in their system context: persistence, indexing, logging, caching, locality, concurrency, and policies controlling their use; connect representation choices to measurable system properties |
| Software Engineering | Requirements, design, testing, process, teamwork | Requirements discovery under uncertainty; quality attributes; architecture; traceability; validation; governance; choosing what should become explicit and authoritative |
| Operating Systems | Processes, memory, concurrency, scheduling, filesystems | Preserve foundations; emphasize resource models, locality, concurrency properties, failure modes, measurement, and when higher-level abstractions leak |
| Cybersecurity | Vulnerabilities, attacks, defenses, secure implementation | Threat models, trust boundaries, security properties, policy, adversarial evidence, and assurance of rapidly generated systems |
| OOP / Programming Paradigms | Classes, inheritance, patterns, implementation organization | Candidate for consolidation: preserve abstraction, modularity, interfaces, types, information hiding, and contracts; reduce time devoted primarily to implementation idioms |
| Formal Methods & Modeling | Often elective, distributed elsewhere, or absent | New core emphasis: state machines, structural and behavioral models, properties, executable specifications, model checking, traceability, model fidelity and drift |
| Senior Design / Capstone | Integrate prior knowledge by designing and building a substantial system | Build the system and make the engineering case for it: models, requirements, quality attributes, tradeoffs, measurements, validation evidence, governance, and explicit justification for acceptance |

This would not require inventing the engineering capstone. ABET's current Engineering
Accreditation Commission criteria already require a culminating major engineering-design
experience that incorporates standards and multiple constraints and builds on earlier
coursework; the Software Engineering program criteria add requirements analysis, security,
verification and validation, and processes for complex software systems [cite: abet-eac-criteria].
Computing-accredited computer science programs likewise require a major project integrating
knowledge and skills acquired earlier [cite: abet-cac-criteria]. The question is therefore not
whether students should integrate their engineering knowledge, but what should count as evidence
that they have. The accreditation requirement permits a much richer realization than "build
something that works," yet programs can operationalize integration at very different levels of
rigor. Absent literature measuring capstone quality across programs, treat "actual practice is
inconsistent" as an observation to test, not a fact. The Senior Design row is the educational
analogue of the sign-off scenario: don't just demo the system — bring the engineering case.

Look again and the conservative result is striking: it is basically the same curriculum.
Algorithms still matters. Operating systems still matters. Security matters more, if anything.
Programming does not disappear, and neither does software engineering. The larger changes are in
emphasis — a little less weight on producing implementation by hand, more on selecting
abstractions, defining properties, measuring systems, and validating claims. These are not
inventions of the agent era; they are recognizable engineering activities whose relative value
rises when implementation becomes cheap. The conspicuous addition is modeling and formal methods,
not because every system should be formally verified but because engineers responsible for
machine-produced systems need a repertoire for making important properties explicit. That is what
we should expect if commodity intelligence changes the economics of engineering rather than
invalidating its foundations.

ABET programs publish a short list of *Program Educational Objectives* — what graduates should
be able to do a few years out. Read against that term, the claim of this chapter has a sharp
form:

> In my opinion, no change in the Program Objectives of a curriculum should be necessary, except
> insofar as it is expedient to add AI to them.

A sea change in how engineering *works*; barely a hiccup in what an engineer should be *trained*
to be.

## No course called Agent Tooling

One omission from [ref:mage-curriculum] is deliberate, and the thesis makes it a conclusion
rather than a taste. If the tools are what is fundamentally new while the durable engineering
problems the chapter opened with are not, then the core curriculum should teach those problems
rather than reorganize itself around today's agent interface.

So there is no required course called AI for Software Engineers, Prompt Engineering, or Agent
Tooling. Those technologies matter, and students should use them throughout the curriculum — but
they belong to the contemporary engineering substrate, not the foundation. Agent harnesses,
context-management techniques, model APIs, and prompting conventions change too fast to earn a
permanent slot in the core merely because they are important now.

The durable knowledge lies underneath them: why finite reasoning creates representation problems,
how tools expose action boundaries, how to evaluate probabilistic output, and how to construct
environments in which uncertain components are used safely. Fluency with the agent systems of the
day develops *while doing* software engineering — use agents in CS 1, to explore algorithms, to
diagnose behavior in operating systems, to build and challenge threat models in security, to write
models in formal methods, and to run a fleet in senior design. We do not build a core course for
every dominant IDE, cloud platform, or build system; we teach the principles that let engineers
understand the tools, choose among them, and replace them when the technology turns over.
**Building AI systems is a specialization. Engineering with commodity intelligence is becoming
ordinary practice.**

This is the anti-fashion test the rest of the book keeps. If MAGE has any longevity, its
educational consequence cannot be "teach Claude Code." It should survive Claude Code.

## What MAGE asks of an education

There is a less comfortable implication, and it belongs here as a risk rather than a forecast.
Implementation demand once created an accessible first rung into software work: learn a language
and a framework, become productive at construction, and acquire deeper engineering knowledge on
the job. If implementation production becomes abundant, that rung may narrow. The work that stays
scarce — requirements discovery, architecture, modeling, systems reasoning, security, validation,
and engineering judgment — is harder to compress into a short, framework-oriented bootcamp.

That is not an argument for credentialism, nor evidence that people without computing degrees
cannot become excellent software engineers. It names a workforce-development problem. Practitioners
whose expertise is concentrated in implementation may need routes upward into modeling,
architecture, assurance, and system responsibility. New entrants from other professions may need
longer bridges into the foundations than an implementation-centered bootcamp provides. If the
profession's center of gravity moves, education and retraining have to move with it.

So the line to carry away is the professional one, not the curricular one:

> MAGE does not replace engineering expertise with natural language. It moves engineering
> expertise toward choosing abstractions, defining properties, evaluating evidence, and deciding
> what deserves authority.

None of this establishes the curriculum of the future. It identifies what MAGE would demand of
one. We do not need to retrain software engineers to stop being engineers. We may need to retrain
them to spend more of their time doing the parts of engineering that implementation scarcity
previously crowded out. Same foundations, a different price list, a different allocation of
curricular time: the shift is from economics, not from any failure of the foundations. **The
curriculum changes less than one might expect. The engineer's use of it changes considerably.**
