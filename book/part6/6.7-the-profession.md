# The Profession

Commodity intelligence does not define the future profession by leaving behind a stable set of tasks for humans.
Those tasks will continue to move.
The more durable change is in the organization of engineering work.

As implementation becomes cheaper, representation, architecture, evidence, validation, governance, and judgment become relatively more valuable.
Software engineering therefore begins to look less like a discipline organized around producing implementation and more like other engineering disciplines in which realization is only one part of a larger system of design, constraint, evidence, and acceptance.

The industrial cases in this Part did not converge on one architecture or one way to use agents.
They converged on a family of engineering problems: what knowledge must be made explicit, where autonomous action is permitted, what evidence justifies trust, which obligations can be given authority, what remains a matter of judgment, and how lessons from one failure reach later work.

That is the professional consequence of commodity intelligence.
The profession is not shrinking toward whatever implementation tasks machines still perform poorly.
It is reorganizing around responsibility for the engineered whole.

The Preface began with a question about what we can safely delegate.
The Conclusion returns to that question—and then asks its converse: what cannot be delegated?
