# Open Problems

A theory earns its keep twice. Once by explaining the case that produced it, and once by handing the field a list of things worth measuring next. This book argued from a single deep case, seen at length. What it cannot do from one repository is settle the quantities — how far judgment compiles into machinery, what a governed environment is worth, how a lesson propagates once it is encoded. Those are not gaps in the argument. They are the argument running ahead of the instruments the discipline currently knows how to build.

So this chapter is a research handoff, not a summary. It names seven open problems, drawn from where the case ran past its own measurements, and it says for each what would make it answerable. They sort into three families and one enabling condition. Three concern **the model** — inducing one, keeping it honest, judging whether it is any good. Two concern **governance** — synthesizing it, and running it across an organization rather than a repository. Two concern **economics** — pricing a governed environment, and measuring the apparatus that sustains it. Behind all seven sits the empirical regime that makes any of them tractable, and the chapter ends there. None of this is settled in these pages. That is the point of putting it here.

## Model induction

MAGE's claim about models is a claim about maintenance, not authorship: the model and the code must stay equal under drift gates, whichever came first — authored and then implemented, induced from existing code, or co-evolved. Two of those three origins are comfortable. The third is where most software actually lives. A working system that never had a model is the common brownfield case, and the open question is whether an agent can read it and produce a model worth keeping.

Faithful is the hard word. A model that merely encodes what the code already does inherits the code's bugs and calls them intent — the same failure the oracle literature names, where a generated check ratifies the implemented behavior rather than the specified one. Induction that is worth anything has to recover *obligation*, not just *behavior*: what the system must hold, separated from what it happens to do. That is a judgment, and today a human makes it. Whether it can be made mechanically, at what fidelity, and how you would even score the result, is a decade of work by itself.

The measurement hook is already visible. Ask what fraction of a system's real obligations an induced model pins, versus how many it merely mirrors. A model with high mirror-fidelity and low obligation-coverage is a liability dressed as an asset. The field needs a way to tell the two apart before it can trust anything an agent induces.

## Model drift

The comparative matrix isolated one rung that no external case reached: a deterministic gate that *blocks* an edit until the model and the implementation agree. Siemens comes closest and stops short — its digital thread synchronizes and propagates across domains, but the sources describe no gate that refuses an agent's change when the change breaks parity. That single rung is MAGE's most distinctive move, and it is also the least understood as a general technique.

The problem is that parity is easy to state and hard to generalize. A drift check over one view — does the state machine in the code match the one in the model? — is tractable. A drift relation that spans the whole Model Zoo, behavioral and process and invariant and lineage views at once, cheap enough to run on every change and precise enough to name *which* fact diverged, is an open engineering problem. At fleet scale drift is not an event; it is a constant pressure, because every agent edit is a chance for the map and the territory to part. A gate that holds them equal without becoming the bottleneck the whole method exists to avoid is the thing to build and measure.

The quantity to report is the drift-gate catch rate: of the divergences a fleet introduces, what share does the gate stop before they land? No study has measured it, because outside this case few environments have the gate to measure.

## Model quality

A model is a representation of intent; a test suite is a model of correctness. Both are fallible, and the field has just been reminded how fallible at a scale it could not ignore. A steward of the most-watched coding benchmark audited the problems a strong model kept failing and found more than half carried flawed tests — too narrow, enforcing one implementation; too wide, checking behavior the task never described — alongside contamination signatures that let the benchmark reward exposure rather than reasoning [cite: openai2026swebench]. The lesson is older than the LLM era: automation that optimizes against a visible oracle produces artifacts that satisfy the oracle while silently breaking what it does not check [cite: smith2015overfitting]. The green bar was always a proxy, and proxies overfit under pressure.

Agents sharpen it, because agents now write the oracles too. They add mocks at a higher rate than human authors, and they tend to encode the implemented behavior rather than the specified one, so the suite they generate ratifies the code's bugs. A passing suite is a fallible model of correctness that must itself be governed. The open problem is how — and it runs at two scales that share mechanisms but not scope. **Benchmark-oracle governance** keeps a whole field's shared yardstick honest: contamination-resistant collection, live re-derivation, staleness controls. **Repository-oracle governance** keeps one project's own controls faithful to its requirements: an obligation census, property and fuzz tests against a stable spec point, a standing distrust of anything an agent asserts about its own output.

Three measurands would turn the concern into a program. What fraction of a task's requirements are pinned by a control versus merely asserted by an example. Contamination- and overfitting-resistant evaluation built as standing infrastructure rather than a one-off refresh. And a mechanical detector for oracle degradation — over-mocking, implementation-coupling — sitting in the review path the way a structural lint already does. None of the three is solved. Each is fundable now.

## Governance synthesis

Governance conversion, as the book describes it, is a human operation. A failure recurs; an engineer classifies it; the engineer chooses a durable mechanism from a closed menu — a model, a constraint, a sensor, a validator, a gate, an interface, an architecture, or nothing at all — and installs it. The move is repeatable enough to name, which raises the obvious next question: can the environment perform it on itself? Can agents be taught to notice inadequate governance and convert a failure into structure, rather than waiting for a human to do it by hand every time?

The mechanical half looks close. An agent can already write a lint, add a test, tighten a schema. The judgment half is the frontier. Which gap merits a mechanism and which should stay a known silence; which mechanism is the *cheapest adequate* one rather than the most elaborate; when the right answer is no mechanism at all. Get that judgment wrong in the direction of zeal and you have built the other failure the book warns about — an estate that accumulates controls forever and charges its own carrying cost. A synthesizer that only adds is a debt engine. One that could also reconcile and retire is a research object nobody has built.

What makes this measurable is the census. It already turns governance into two counts — gaps where an obligation has no mechanism, orphans where a mechanism has no obligation. A governance synthesizer is exactly a system that tries to drive gaps down without driving orphans up. That is a control loop with an observable error signal, which is the precondition for studying it at all.

## Economics of engineering capital

The book claims that converted failures compound — a mechanism authored once pays on every later change, and the environment banks the saving as engineering capital. The claim is easy to state and treacherous to price, and the case study has the negative result to prove it. A per-task ablation stripped a model artifact from a well-tended repository and looked for the recall it cost. It found none. The redundant, synchronized copies the governance method deliberately maintains restored the answer through the code, the tests, the comments, the docs. Recall collapsed toward zero *regardless of what the map was worth*, because a healthy garden leaves no single-source fact for a strip to remove. The instrument met the garden and the garden won.

The lesson is not that the environment is worthless. It is that its value does not live at the scale a per-task strip can see. It lives at the macro scale — whole environments set against whole environments — and that is where the measurement has to move. The theory offers a concrete, disprovable bet to aim it: that repository quality may substitute, at the margin, for model capability, because a rich environment performs some of the reasoning before the agent begins. Whether that substitution holds, and how far, is the open economic question. A crossed design answers it: several models from weaker to frontier, several repositories from thinly to richly governed, the same reasoning engine run in every cell, and the interaction term read off the result. A substitution effect lifts a weaker model in a rich environment toward a stronger model's cell. A complementary effect shows the two multiplying. Either outcome is a mechanism-level finding the field cannot currently produce, and neither has been measured.

## Measuring the support ratio

In the system this book draws from, the apparatus that governs the code — the tests, the lints, the tooling, the models — outweighed the production code it governs. That ratio is the most concrete face of engineering capital, and it is almost entirely unstudied. Is a support apparatus several times the size of the product a sign of a mature environment banking assurance that manual review cannot scale to provide, or a sign of an estate bloated with machinery that has outlived its reason? The census gives the *sign* of any single mechanism — governed or orphan — but nobody has measured the *quantity* across a real environment over time, or asked what a healthy trajectory looks like.

Two constructs hide inside the ratio and should not be merged. **Redundant-encoding density** is how many synchronized copies of a fact the repository carries; it predicts resilience to the loss of any one artifact, and it is what flattened the ablation above. **Agent-legibility** is how readily an agent can find and use those copies, and redundancy can *cut* it when the copies drift or add noise. A repository can be dense and illegible at once. Both are candidate environment-quality metrics, and measuring them apart is part of the work.

The outcome that ties it together is governed throughput, not implementation volume — how much *durable* progress an environment sustains per unit of agent effort over weeks and months, rather than how many lines a fleet emits in an afternoon. That is the number the whole method claims to move, and it is precisely the number the field has no standard way to report.

## Organizational governance

The single case is one engineer. It cannot say how a hundred coordinate around a fleet, and the industrial layer showed the organization does not stay the same shape when it tries. Mechanisms centralize while judgment decentralizes; scarce human decisions move upstream; private learning has to become a shared artifact or it is waste; the unit of governance grows from the task to the fleet. Each of those is an observation in want of a dynamics.

Two questions matter most. The first is propagation: once a lesson is encoded into shared, machine-actionable structure, how does it actually reach later work, and how long does it take? Propagation latency is a metric the book proposes and does not yet measure. The second is authority, and it is sharper than it looks. Whether the person who *sees* a failure holds the authority to *change the environment* is the difference between a mechanism that dies with one incident and a failure class that never dies. That is an organizational design variable, not a technical one, and it decides whether governance conversion happens at all.

Behind both sits the openness question the environment forces. As effective capability moves out of the source tree and into models, skills, validators, policies, and context machinery, a larger fraction of what lets a team evolve a system quickly comes to sit outside the code it ships. Source without the governed environment is forkable code that does not reproduce the loop through which the upstream fleet reliably changes it. So the field inherits a new axis — call it development completeness — asking not whether the artifact is open but how much of the capacity to evolve it has been shared. Which environment artifacts belong to the software supply chain, what an outsider must be given to reproduce not the system but the ability to change it safely, and how much assurance a regulated system can draw as a by-product of a model-rich environment rather than a certification bolted on at the end: three open questions the book raises and deliberately leaves standing.

## What makes the list answerable

Set these seven beside each other and the same enabling condition sits under all of them. Five years ago none was tractable, for one reason: the experimental subject would not hold still. Empirical software engineering spent decades trying to measure interventions whose effects were small beside the variation in who did the work, and randomizing around that variation rarely showed an effect bigger than the spread between two developers. The noise came from the people.

What changed is not that intelligence got cheaper. It got *standardized*. A foundation model is one subject a study can instantiate as many times as it has conditions, pinned to a version, its prompts archived, its sampling variance estimated by repetition rather than assumed away. Hold the reasoning engine fixed across arms and, for the first time, a researcher can fix one major source of cognitive variation instead of randomizing around it. That single move turns system-scale questions from observational to interventional — not *does practice X correlate with outcome Y across the teams we could survey*, but *what happens to the outcome if governance mechanism A is replaced by mechanism B* — and it makes the engineered environment a first-class experimental object alongside the people and organizations that were always there. It also makes replication cheap: rerun the same model over the same prompts in the same environment, rather than re-recruiting a matched population of developers. Cheap replication is the precondition for a cumulative science.

The evidence for the shift is already on the record, and it does not all point one way — a field experiment across thousands of developers measured a real rise in completed tasks, while experienced developers in familiar repositories ran measurably *slower* with early tooling even as they believed themselves faster [cite: cui2025genai] [cite: metr2025productivity]. A standardized input is one a study can assign, not one that always helps, and that is exactly what makes it useful as a variable to fix.

MAGE is one proposal for organizing this regime. It is not the regime, and it is not the last word on any of the seven problems above; other methods, architectures, and governance approaches will emerge, and the opportunity outlives all of them. The claim worth making is the broad one. Agentic software engineering may finally offer interventions whose effects are large enough to see, explicit enough to reproduce, and structured enough to build theory on. Whether MAGE proves correct matters less than whether the field takes the opening — and treats agentic software engineering as an experimental science whose theories can be proposed, compared, refined, and, on the evidence, replaced.
