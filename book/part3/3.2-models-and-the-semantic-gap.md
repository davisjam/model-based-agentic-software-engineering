# From Intent to Enforceable Obligation

<!-- point: part-two-handed-each-model-forward-as-a-typed-seam-this-part-builds-its-authority | Part II handed each model forward as a typed seam naming a constraint, sensor, validator, and gate; this Part builds those surfaces. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, model -->
Part II closed by handing this Part a table. Every model it built — structure, decision, behavior,
concurrency, measurement, documentation, and the scenario that joins them — arrived carrying a
property it could now state precisely and a quality that property serves. Beside each, in role and
shape, Part II named the authority surface the next Part would build to enforce it: a **constraint**
that makes the violation unexpressible, a **sensor** that produces evidence of what the code actually
does, a **validator** that evaluates that evidence against the model, and a **gate** that admits only
what agrees. Part II owned the chain through the property and stopped there on purpose. Building those
four surfaces is this Part's work.

<!-- point: alignment-does-not-replace-the-model-it-gives-the-model-authority | Alignment does not replace the model; it gives the model authority. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
The turn is one line. **Alignment does not replace the model; it gives the model authority.** A model
states what the system ought to be; alignment is what makes the system insist on it. Part II's
carry-away was the property an engineer can state. What this Part receives, and builds, is the surface
that makes the code obey it.

<!-- point: part-two-built-the-model-this-chapter-gives-it-authority | Part 2 built the model; this chapter is where represented intent gets authority. | terms: model, injection-point, semantic-gap -->
The last chapter gave you four places to inject a mechanism. Part 2 built the thing most worth injecting: a
structured model — a description of the system precise enough that a machine can hold the code up to it. This
chapter is the turn from advice to obligation — where a model stops describing what the system is and starts
constraining what the system is allowed to become.

<!-- point: the-turn-is-rung-4-to-rung-5-and-it-has-a-trap | Wire the model to a gate and it binds; but a green check can certify the wrong thing. | terms: model, thesis-alignment, drift-gate -->
On Part 2's capability ladder that is the step from rung 4 to rung 5: a model that says what shape the
system should have acquires a *checker*, and stated intent stops being advisory. This is the first move of
**represented intent becomes authority** — a model alone has none, and here it gets one. The move has a
trap, and it catches almost everyone who first wires a model to a gate: the check can pass, the build can go
green, and the property you meant to enforce can fail anyway. The rest of the chapter is that trap and the
two lessons it teaches — author the obligation *independently* of the code, and enforce it *where the
violation is legible*.

## A drift check proves agreement, not correctness

<!-- section-terms: drift-gate, mirror-vs-spec -->
<!-- point: a-drift-gate-proves-agreement-not-that-either-side-is-right | A drift gate proves agreement, not correctness. | terms: drift-gate, drift-caveat -->
<!-- index-def: drift-caveat -->
Here is the trap the opener warned of. A model wired to a
build-time drift check cannot *rot* — that much is real. But it can still be *wrong*. The gate
proves one thing: the model and the code **agree**. It says nothing about whether either of them
matches what you meant. Both can move together, in the same wrong direction, and the gate stays a
contented green the whole way.

Picture a model that says an endpoint is protected, and code that leaves it open. A drift check
catches that — but only because the model already carries the concept that the endpoint is *meant*
to be protected. Now derive the model from the implementation instead. The derived model reads the
open endpoint and records it, faithfully, as unprotected. The code is unprotected; the model says
unprotected; the drift check agrees. The bug is invisible, the gate is green, and everything is
"in sync."

<!-- point: a-mirror-model-reflects-the-code-only-a-spec-authored-from-intent-catches-a-wrong-is | A mirror reflects code; only a spec catches wrong. | terms: mirror-vs-spec, model-from-code -->
<!-- index-def: mirror-vs-spec -->
The power, then, was never in modeling as such; it was in the model being **authored
independently of the code**. A model derived from the code is a **mirror**: it reflects what *is*,
and a mirror cannot tell you the thing it reflects is wrong. A model authored from intent is a
**spec**: it says what *ought to be*, and only a spec catches a wrong "is." People will generate
a model from their codebase and believe they hold the same thing as someone who wrote one from
intent. They do not. The drift check looks identical in both cases. What differs is whether an
independent claim exists for the code to be measured against, or only a reflection agreeing with
itself. A derived descriptive model is not worthless — it becomes a *specification* the moment
independent intent is attached to it, checked against a policy or invariant authored apart from the
code. What a bare mirror lacks is that independent claim; supply one and the reflection acquires
something to be wrong against.

This is the passage where the built bridge lands. A spec wired to a gate is intent given
authority: **represented intent becomes authority.** *In other words:* the model stops being
advice and starts becoming something the build, the deployment pipeline, or the runtime refuses
to violate.

<!-- point: what-belongs-in-the-model-is-the-essential-residual-that-does-not-automate | Deciding what belongs in the model is the essential residual. | terms: residual, drift-gate -->
This does not weaken the drift gate; it tells you what the gate is *for*. Checking that the model
and the code agree is mechanical, and the book automates it. Deciding **what belongs in the
model** — which concepts it must carry, which "ought"s it must assert — does not automate. That is
the essential residual [Brooks](6.5-implications-for-se.html) warned would not yield, and under
this method it moves one level up: from "write correct code" to "author the model that says what
correct means." The gate is necessary. The authored model is what makes the gate mean anything.

That authored move has a name. Recall the **Representation–Alignment Proposition** from Part 2:
as engineering intent becomes more explicit and structured, the set of obligations that can be
enforced deterministically expands. That is the frontier the whole method rides, and on the
Alignment side it is exactly what the gate cashes — the richer the spec, the more of the residual
it pulls into the gate.

We met a corner of this already, without naming it. A done-check can confirm the model was
*updated* but not that it is *right* — the same asymmetry surfaces below, in the semantic-gap
passage on the definition-of-done audit. And it recurs in the test dimension: a journey test that
asserts only that the flow *ran* is a mirror of execution, while a typed task-closure
post-condition is the authored spec that says what "done" means — the [Scenarios
view](2.7-scenarios-and-the-joined-model.html) draws that line for oracles.

What the gate adds, and where it stops, is sharpest against a system that binds without gating.
Read these as **the surface made binding** — a representation strong enough to be worth wiring to a
check that fails on disagreement, so the model crosses from advising to holding.

<!-- worked-examples: binds-model-to-territory -->
### Example — Siemens (near-miss) {other}
Siemens keeps its models and code coupled across a digital thread, but the coupling
*synchronizes* rather than *gates*: a human confirms the BOM, and physics solvers stand in
for a build-time drift check. The correspondence is maintained, yet nothing fails the build
the instant the two diverge. It shows the binding without the hard gate — the near-miss that
marks what the gate adds.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble wires each model to a build-time drift-and-parity gate: when the model and the code
it describes disagree, the build fails, and the disagreement surfaces the instant it appears
instead of the instant it bites. The gate makes "the map equals the territory" a checked
property rather than a hope — the one edge that keeps docs, code, and tests a bound system.

<!-- takeaway -->
The shared move is **binding the model to the territory with a check that fails on
disagreement** — which proves the two *agree*, not that either is *right*: the gate keeps
the map honest, but only an independently authored spec makes it worth keeping.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

## The semantic gap

<!-- section-terms: semantic-gap, right-level-of-enforcement, model-drift -->

<!-- point: enforce-at-the-wrong-level-and-the-property-slips-through | Enforce a property at the wrong level; it slips through. | terms: semantic-gap, right-level-of-enforcement -->
Enforce a property at the wrong level and it slips through; [ref:semantic-gap] shows the fix — lift the check to where the meaning is legible.

<!-- label: semantic-gap -->
<!-- figure: assets/semantic-gap-levels.svg | *The Semantic Gap.* Bottom row: small events — single commits, calls, turns — where the property spreads across many, invisible in one, so enforcing here is the mistake. Top row: the whole unit of work, where it becomes checkable — lift the check to where meaning is legible. -->

<!-- point: model-drift-at-the-commit-is-the-wrong-granularity | Checking model drift at the commit is the wrong granularity. | terms: model-drift, semantic-gap -->
Now the trap. Say you want to enforce "if the agent substantially changed the call
graph but did not update the overlaid model, reject it." It sounds like a pre-commit
hook. It is not. An agent carrying
out a real task makes *many* commits toward one goal; the model may be legitimately out
of sync in the middle and correct again by the end. Enforce at the commit and you are
checking a sentence for grammar before the paragraph is written.

<!-- point: the-semantic-gap-is-enforcing-a-property-at-the-wrong-level-of-abstraction | The semantic gap is enforcing at the wrong level. | terms: semantic-gap, model-drift -->
<!-- index-def: semantic-gap -->
<!-- index-example: model-drift -->
This is the **semantic gap**: the failure you get whenever you enforce a property at the
wrong level of abstraction. Operating-system security lives with it constantly — the OS
sees individual system calls, but "this program is exfiltrating data" is a *pattern
across* calls, invisible in any single one; you have to stitch ten network calls
together before the leak is legible. Model drift is the same shape. The commit is the
wrong granularity.

<!-- point: enforce-at-the-level-where-the-property-is-legible-agent-return-for-model-drift | Enforce where the property is legible: at agent-return. | terms: right-level-of-enforcement, semantic-gap -->
<!-- index-def: right-level-of-enforcement -->
<!-- index-example: semantic-gap -->
The fix is to find the level where the property is actually legible, and enforce there.
For model drift, that level is the *agent returning from its task*, because at that
moment the entire feature and its plan are visible. You can see whether the work changed
the model, and whether the body of commits includes the tests, the documentation, and
everything else the task was supposed to carry. This is exactly where the companion
repository places its definition-of-done audit: a check that runs when an agent
finishes an Epic, over the whole unit of work, rather than at any single commit inside
it. "Correct" is a hard word — you cannot fully prove the model is right — but you *can*
check that it was updated and that the surrounding work is complete, and that is the
right semantic level to do it.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [definition-of-done audit](appendix-a-epic-definition-of-done.html).

<!-- gloss: Epic | A tracked unit of work larger than one commit: a feature or effort with its own file, plan, and definition of done, carried out over several agent dispatches. -->

## A second worked example: the context-fullness bank-hook

<!-- section-terms: semantic-gap, context-window, right-level-of-enforcement -->

<!-- point: compaction-guesses-below-meaning-so-it-can-only-approximate | Compaction guesses below meaning; it can only approximate. | terms: context-window, semantic-gap -->
The semantic gap shows up in the operator's own loop, not just the product. When a long
session fills the harness's context window, the harness *compacts*: it summarizes the
history and throws the raw record away to make room. That decision is a best-effort guess,
because the harness sees tokens, not meaning — it cannot tell that the three paragraphs
settling the architecture matter more than the forty walking a directory tree. It sits
below the semantics of "what is worth keeping," so it can only approximate.

<!-- point: fire-the-bank-hook-on-fullness-and-staleness-together | Fire the bank-hook on fullness and staleness together. | terms: hook-hard-control, right-level-of-enforcement -->
The property the operator actually cares about is not fullness. It is whether the *durable*
record — the strategy document, the hand-off notes — is current, so that a compaction loses
nothing important. The harness knows one number, how full the window is; it does not know
whether those documents reflect the last hour of decisions or went stale ninety minutes ago.
So move the check up to where the meaning is legible: a hook in the operator's loop that
fires on **two conditions together** — the window getting full *and* the durable records
having gone untouched while work continued. When both hold, it emits one reminder: bank the
current state. A companion post-compaction hook then knows exactly where to rebuild context
from, because the pre-work made those records the authoritative place to look.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [orchestrator lifecycle hooks](appendix-a-lifecycle-hooks.html).

Why both conditions, and not one? A full window is common; a stale record is common; either
alone fires the reminder constantly, and a reminder that fires constantly trains the operator
to stop and re-summarize the session instead of doing the work — self-summarization churn,
the loop spending its turns banking its own banking. Both at once is the rare case where a
compaction would actually lose something, and the only case worth the interruption.
**Fullness is not the semantic property; fullness plus stale durable state is.**

<!-- point: with-no-clean-rung-a-roughly-right-hook-beats-the-perfect-one | With no clean rung, roughly-right beats the perfect hook. | terms: right-level-of-enforcement, hook-hard-control -->
One honest note, because the semantic-gap idea should make you notice it. The
*theoretically* right moment to bank is the instant before compaction, and the harness even
offers a pre-compact hook there. It is the wrong hook: it fires too late for a comfortable
bank, and worse, writing the hand-off itself costs tokens, so a bank kicked off at the
boundary can be the very thing that trips the compaction it was meant to precede. There is no
clean rung here — so a hook at *roughly* the right level, earlier and on the dual condition,
beats the theoretically-perfect one. That is the honest difference from the drift example,
where agent-return genuinely is the level at which the property is legible and waiting for it
loses nothing.

Part 2 shows how to author and maintain these models; the reusable rule here is to enforce a
property at the level where it becomes observable.

A model wired to a drift gate holds you to what you knew to write down. But you do not
know a whole system in advance — you discover what you are building by building it, and
the sharpest failures are the ones no model predicted. That is the other half of
governance: not the obligations you specify up front, but the ones a fast-moving agent
reveals after the fact. The next chapter builds it, and gives the two forms every
mechanism finally takes — the model and its gate among them.
