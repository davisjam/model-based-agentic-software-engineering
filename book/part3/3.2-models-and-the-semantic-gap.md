# From Engineering Intent to Enforceable Obligation

<!-- section-terms: independent-obligation, semantic-gap, agreement-vs-correctness -->

<!-- point: enforcement-needs-an-independent-obligation-and-a-legible-boundary | Enforcement needs an obligation independent of the work being judged, and a boundary at which that obligation is legible. | terms: independent-obligation, semantic-gap -->

Enforcement requires two things: **an obligation independent of the work being judged and a boundary where the evidence needed to evaluate that obligation is available.** Without the first, a checker may only confirm that the system agrees with itself. Without the second, the mechanism tries to enforce a good obligation before the necessary evidence exists.

<!-- point: an-obligation-may-live-in-a-model-or-directly-in-a-type-or-policy | An obligation may live in a model, or directly in a type, permission, schema, test, or policy; Alignment does not require a particular representation. | terms: independent-obligation, modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->

{{chapter:modeling}} supplied many ways to make obligations explicit. Some live in models: permitted service edges, legal state transitions, ownership relations, operational bounds. Others live directly in types, permissions, schemas, tests, or policy. Alignment does not require every obligation to originate in the same kind of representation. It requires that the obligation be distinguishable from the thing being judged and that a mechanism can enforce the result.

<!-- point: software-engineering-has-a-traceability-heritage-alignment-builds-on | Requirements traceability connects intent, implementation, and evidence to support evaluation and change analysis; engineers separately decide how an evaluation should affect the work. | terms: independent-obligation, traceability -->

Once an obligation is explicit, engineers need to determine whether the implementation satisfies it. You may be familiar with this work from requirements traceability, which connects a requirement to relevant design elements, implementation, tests, and other evidence[cite: estefan2008mbse]. These connections help engineers examine the design choices made to meet the requirement and find the evidence needed to evaluate the implementation. When a requirement or design changes, the links help identify which parts of the implementation and which checks need to be reconsidered. Designing enforcement requires a separate judgment about how the evaluation should affect the work: should its finding inform a later decision, or should a mechanism refuse an action or result that violates the obligation?

<!-- point: traceability-connects-intent-realization-evidence-without-enforcement | Traceability can relate an obligation, its realization, and its evidence without any mechanism enforcing the obligation — a requirement can trace cleanly to a test whose failure is merely reported, and a permission boundary can enforce firmly without a rich traceability model. | terms: independent-obligation, traceability, alignment-principle -->

Traceability can connect an obligation, its realization, and its evidence without any mechanism enforcing the obligation. A requirement may trace cleanly to a test whose failure is merely reported. Conversely, a permission boundary may enforce firmly without a rich traceability model. Alignment therefore separates the relation among intent, realization, and evidence from the decision to enforce the resulting judgment.

<!-- point: tolerance-independence-comes-from-intent-not-realization | Where an obligation permits variation, the acceptable region must come from governing intent, not from the realization being judged. | terms: independent-obligation, tolerance -->

Where an obligation permits variation, the acceptable region must likewise come from the engineering intent that governs the work. A measured implementation can establish what latency it achieved or what dependencies it contains; whether those values are acceptable requires an independent engineering obligation.

<!-- point: those-requirements-produce-the-two-lessons-agreement-not-correctness-and-placement-follows-semantics | Two lessons: agreement is not correctness, and placement must follow the meaning of the obligation. | terms: agreement-vs-correctness, semantic-gap -->

A description and the implementation it describes can therefore agree while both violate the intended property. Placement matters as well: if an obligation is enforced at a boundary too small to make it legible, the checker may inspect the wrong thing.

## Agreement Is Not Correctness

<!-- section-terms: agreement-vs-correctness, independent-obligation -->

<!-- point: a-drift-check-establishes-agreement-not-that-the-correspondence-is-right | A drift check establishes agreement between two artifacts, not that the correspondence expresses the right intent. | terms: agreement-vs-correctness -->

<!-- index-def: agreement-vs-correctness -->

<!-- index-def: mirror-vs-spec -->

<!-- index-def: drift-caveat -->

A drift check can establish a declared correspondence between two artifacts. It cannot establish that the correspondence expresses the right intent. Suppose an endpoint is required to be authenticated. If an independently authored policy says so, a check can compare the implementation against that obligation and flag an open endpoint. If instead the representation is derived only from the implementation, it may faithfully record the endpoint as open. Model and code agree; the security obligation is still violated.

<!-- point: the-distinction-is-description-versus-independent-obligation | The distinction is not derived versus authored; it is description versus independent obligation. | terms: independent-obligation, agreement-vs-correctness -->

<!-- index-def: independent-obligation -->

The distinction is between description and independent obligation. A model extracted from code can supply the current *is*; an independently authored invariant can supply the governing *ought*. Authorship alone establishes neither role: a hand-authored model may merely repeat the implementation's assumptions, while a derived model can faithfully describe its present behavior. Enforcement requires an obligation against which the work can actually be wrong.

<!-- point: drift-checking-has-a-precise-job-preserve-the-correspondence-the-model-claims | Drift checking has a precise job: preserve a declared correspondence between model and implementation; correctness needs another claim. | terms: agreement-vs-correctness, independent-obligation -->

Drift checking therefore has a precise job: preserve a declared correspondence between model and implementation. If code and model diverge, surface the disagreement. Correctness requires another claim—the policy, invariant, requirement, or property that must hold. Once that obligation exists, a validator can compare evidence against it and a gate can decide what follows.

<!-- point: proof-strength-applies-to-the-proposition-proved-not-to-its-adequacy | Proofs and model checks evaluate stated properties under their assumptions; they do not establish that the chosen properties capture the engineering requirement. | terms: agreement-vs-correctness, independent-obligation -->

Stronger verification does not remove this distinction. A proof assistant such as Lean checks proofs that formally stated propositions follow from their assumptions[cite: lean-reference]. TLA+ lets engineers specify system behavior; the TLC model checker can explore a model to check whether it satisfies stated properties[cite: lamport2002]. Neither approach establishes that the chosen property captures the engineering requirement. If an agent chooses a property merely because its implementation satisfies it, a valid proof or successful model check may leave the intended obligation untested. Engineers can ask agents to help formalize requirements and develop proofs or checks while retaining responsibility for whether those requirements express what the system must preserve.

<!-- point: docable-requested-temporal-checking-for-an-obligation-its-models-already-carried | Fix the required behavior before choosing an analysis; independence concerns the source of the obligation and the implementation judged against it. | terms: independent-obligation, agreement-vs-correctness -->

For example, an engineer can require a queueing service to bring every accepted job to a terminal state, then ask an agent to develop an analysis of that requirement. The agent may help formalize the obligation and construct the checker. What matters is that the obligation comes from what the service must do, rather than being chosen merely because the current implementation satisfies it.

### Example — Siemens {other}

The Siemens reconstruction describes models, implementation, and engineering analyses kept in correspondence across a digital thread without making every disagreement a build-blocking event. In that account, a human confirms the bill of materials while physics solvers carry parts of the evaluation. The example is useful precisely because correspondence and gating are separate design choices: the coupling can hold without a hard gate deciding the instant the two diverge.

<!-- worked-examples-end -->

An interface offers a simple way to distinguish these relations. Two services’ contract tests can establish that their represented message formats agree. A separate validator can compare an emitted message with an independently specified protocol. The receiving service can still reject a conforming message because the sender lacks permission to submit it. Agreement between representations, conformance to a requirement, and acceptance by a particular environment are different claims.

> #### Worked inset — DocAble: A Model That Directs Work and a View That Describes It {#inset-docable-correspondence}
>
> DocAble repairs accessibility problems in Office and PDF documents through a sequence of computations. A DOCX table-header repair, for example, establishes information needed by a later table-structure check. The scheduler must preserve that dependency even though the two operations have separate implementations. An explicit computation model records such relationships. The scheduler reads the model to determine what is ready to run, while each computation’s implementation determines how it performs its work.[^static-graph]
>
> A separate analytical graph makes that composition available for queries and checks. For Office formats, its dependency edges are projected from registry read/write metadata also used by scheduling. For PDF, the analytical relations come from typed declarations of what computations produce and consume, with blocking parity checks maintaining correspondence to the executable declarations. The view helps engineers inspect the pipeline, but the scheduler does not use that view to run it. [ref:fidelity-vs-authority] separates these two uses of the system knowledge.
>
> <!-- label: fidelity-vs-authority -->
> <!-- figure: assets/fidelity-vs-authority.svg | *Governing role and correspondence.* Execution consumes the computation model. >
> The checks also have a coverage limit. In the examined Office implementation, one live pass still ran outside the registration surface that the parity checks could enumerate. The checks reported agreement for the represented surface, but remained audit-only. Making them blocking would not have closed that gap: the same green result would still have omitted the unregistered pass. The missing correspondence first had to be understood and addressed before the check could justify a broader acceptance claim.
>

A gate can make that agreement a condition of admission, but cannot enlarge the evidence the check supplies. Keeping those decisions separate lets engineers strengthen each relation without mistaking one for another.

## The Semantic Gap

<!-- section-terms: semantic-gap, earliest-legible-boundary, model-drift -->

<!-- point: an-obligation-can-be-perfectly-stated-and-unenforceable-at-the-boundary-you-check | An obligation can be stated clearly and still be unenforceable at the boundary where you try to check it. | terms: semantic-gap, earliest-legible-boundary -->

A second problem is placement. **An obligation can be stated clearly and still be unenforceable at the boundary where you try to check it.** If the property exists only over a whole task, no single commit holds enough meaning to decide it. If data exfiltration appears only as a pattern across calls, no individual system call carries the whole property. A **semantic gap** occurs when an obligation requires meaning that the chosen boundary does not expose. [ref:semantic-gap] shows the fix: lift the check to where the meaning is legible.

<!-- sidenote -->

> *A footnote on where the placement rule comes from.* It echoes the end-to-end argument in system design: a function that needs end-to-end knowledge cannot in general be implemented completely at a lower layer that lacks that knowledge[cite: saltzerreedclark1984e2e]. Their setting was where to place *functionality* in a distributed system; the setting here is where to place *enforcement* over engineering work. The design instinct is the same — put the deciding mechanism where the semantics needed to decide the property are available.

<!-- label: semantic-gap -->
<!-- figure: assets/semantic-gap-levels.svg | *The semantic gap.* A property that spans several events cannot be decided from any event alone. Move the mechanism to the earliest boundary where the evidence required by the obligation is assembled. -->

<!-- point: a-deployment-can-pass-every-check-and-still-serve-a-blank-page | Test the served page in a browser at the earliest boundary where the relevant composition can be exercised; a successful HTTP fetch alone does not establish correct rendering. | terms: semantic-gap, earliest-legible-boundary -->

A deployment can pass twenty checks and still serve a blank page. Those checks may have examined source files, build outputs, and emitted data without testing how the deployed application combines them. If the obligation is that the served page displays the expected data, a smoke test should load that page in a browser and assert that the expected content appears. The check belongs at the earliest boundary where the relevant composition can be exercised, which may be a preview or staging deployment. A successful HTTP fetch alone does not establish that the page rendered correctly.

<!-- point: model-drift-at-the-commit-is-the-wrong-granularity | Checking model drift at the commit is the wrong granularity; the property belongs to the completed unit of work. | terms: model-drift, semantic-gap -->

<!-- index-def: semantic-gap -->

<!-- index-example: model-drift -->

The same gap appears inside a single task. Take a concrete obligation: *if this task substantially changes the call graph, the corresponding architectural model must still agree when the task closes.* A task may pass through several intermediate commits in which temporary disagreement is legitimate. A pre-commit check would then act too early. At task return, the accumulated code changes and model updates can be assessed together against the completion obligation. The check belongs to the completed unit of work, rather than every intermediate commit.

## The Earliest Decidable Boundary

<!-- section-terms: earliest-legible-boundary, semantic-gap -->

<!-- point: place-enforcement-at-the-earliest-boundary-where-the-obligation-is-legible-and-decidable | The design rule: place enforcement at the earliest boundary where the obligation is both legible and decidable. | terms: earliest-legible-boundary, semantic-gap -->

<!-- index-def: earliest-legible-boundary -->

The agent-return check illustrates a general rule for placing enforcement:

> **Place enforcement at the earliest boundary where the obligation is both legible and decidable.**

*Earliest* matters because delay makes a failure more expensive to repair. *Legible* matters because moving the check earlier than the property permits produces false refusals or meaningless checks. The rule explains the whole mechanism menu at a glance:

- **Static type checks** evaluate expressions against declared types before execution, once the relevant code and type information are available.
- **Action hooks** act at one tool call, one command — where a single move is legible.
- **Task-completion checks** act at the work-unit boundary — where "done" first becomes readable across a body of commits.
- **Deployment gates** act after broader evidence has accumulated — the last boundary before the artifact is accepted.

And some properties stay illegible until later still: a leak visible only across ten calls, a cost overrun visible only across a season of jobs. No boundary makes them readable sooner. The correct placement is not the earliest available boundary; it is the earliest boundary that can actually decide the obligation.

<!-- point: the-part-two-rhyme-coarsest-representation-earliest-boundary | The rhyme: Modeling chooses the reduction that answers the question without carrying unnecessary detail; Alignment chooses the earliest boundary where the obligation is legible. | terms: semantic-gap, earliest-legible-boundary, modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->

{{chapter:modeling}} and {{chapter:alignment}} therefore use companion selection rules:

<!-- inline-quote -->

> **Modeling** — *choose the reduction that makes the engineering **question** answerable without carrying unnecessary detail.*
>
> **Alignment** — *choose the earliest boundary at which the **obligation** becomes legible and decidable.*

Both rules keep irrelevant detail from driving the engineering decision.

- Modeling avoids carrying detail the question does not need.
- Alignment avoids enforcing an obligation before the necessary evidence exists.

Choose the wrong abstraction in {{chapter:modeling}} and the required property is not represented. Choose the wrong boundary here and the required evidence is not available.

<!-- point: real-boundaries-are-sometimes-approximate-a-roughly-right-hook-beats-the-perfect-one | Real boundaries are sometimes approximate: a hook that fires early enough to leave budget for the work beats the theoretically ideal hook. | terms: earliest-legible-boundary, semantic-gap -->

Real boundaries sometimes require a margin for the mechanism to act. Suppose an agent must preserve a durable hand-off before its working context is compacted. Waiting until no room remains may leave too little capacity to write it. A practical trigger can act earlier, when context is becoming full and the durable record is stale. The trigger approximates the desired boundary while preserving enough time and capacity to complete the required action.

<!-- point: the-chapter-supplied-both-requirements-next-question-is-what-the-mechanism-does | The section has supplied both requirements for enforcement; the next question is what the mechanism at that boundary actually does. | terms: independent-obligation, semantic-gap -->

With an independent obligation and a boundary where it becomes legible, the next question is what the mechanism at that boundary actually does.
