# From Intent to Enforceable Obligation

<!-- section-terms: independent-obligation, semantic-gap, agreement-vs-correctness -->

<!-- point: authority-needs-an-independent-obligation-and-a-legible-boundary | Authority needs an obligation independent of the work being judged, and a boundary at which that obligation is legible. | terms: independent-obligation, semantic-gap -->
Authority needs two things: **an obligation independent of the work being judged, and a boundary at which
that obligation is legible.** Without the first, a checker may only confirm that the system agrees with
itself. Without the second, a perfectly good obligation is being enforced where the evidence needed to
evaluate it does not yet exist.

<!-- point: an-obligation-may-live-in-a-model-or-directly-in-a-type-or-policy | An obligation may live in a model, or directly in a type, permission, schema, test, or policy; Alignment does not care which. | terms: independent-obligation, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
Part 2 supplied many ways to make obligations explicit. Some live in models: permitted service edges, legal
state transitions, ownership relations, operational bounds. Others live directly in types, permissions,
schemas, tests, or policy. Alignment does not care that every obligation arrived through the same
representation. It cares that the obligation can be told apart from the thing being judged, and that an
authoritative mechanism can act on the result.

<!-- point: those-requirements-produce-the-two-lessons-agreement-not-correctness-and-placement-follows-semantics | Those requirements produce two lessons: agreement is not correctness, and placement follows semantics. | terms: agreement-vs-correctness, semantic-gap -->
Those two requirements produce the two lessons of this chapter. First, **agreement is not correctness**: a
description and the implementation it describes can agree while both violate the intended property. Second,
**placement follows semantics**: enforce an obligation at a boundary too small to make it legible, and the
checker will confidently inspect the wrong thing.

## Agreement is not correctness

<!-- section-terms: agreement-vs-correctness, independent-obligation -->

<!-- point: a-drift-check-establishes-agreement-not-that-the-correspondence-is-right | A drift check establishes agreement between two artifacts, not that the correspondence expresses the right intent. | terms: agreement-vs-correctness -->
<!-- index-def: agreement-vs-correctness -->
<!-- index-def: mirror-vs-spec -->
<!-- index-def: drift-caveat -->
A drift check can establish a declared correspondence between two artifacts. It cannot establish that the
correspondence expresses the right intent. Suppose an endpoint is required to be authenticated. If an
independently authored policy says so, a check can compare the implementation against that obligation and
flag an open endpoint. If instead the representation is derived only from the implementation, it may
faithfully record the endpoint as open. Model and code agree; the security obligation is still violated.

<!-- point: the-distinction-is-description-versus-independent-obligation | The distinction is not derived versus authored; it is description versus independent obligation. | terms: independent-obligation, agreement-vs-correctness -->
<!-- index-def: independent-obligation -->
The distinction is not **derived model versus authored model.** Derived models are useful throughout MAGE. The
distinction is **description versus independent obligation.** A model extracted from code can supply the
current *is*; an independently authored invariant supplies the *ought*. A hand-authored model can be just as
wrong when it merely repeats the implementation's assumptions. Authority requires something against which
the work can actually be wrong.

<!-- point: drift-checking-has-a-precise-job-preserve-the-correspondence-the-model-claims | Drift checking has a precise job: preserve the correspondence the model claims to maintain; correctness needs another claim. | terms: agreement-vs-correctness, independent-obligation -->
Drift checking therefore has a precise job: preserve the correspondence the model claims to maintain. If
code and model diverge, surface the disagreement. Correctness needs another claim — the policy, invariant,
requirement, or property whose satisfaction matters. Once that obligation exists, a validator can compare
evidence against it and a gate can decide what follows.

<!-- worked-examples: binds-model-to-territory -->
### Example — Siemens {other}
Siemens keeps its models, implementation, and engineering analyses in correspondence across a digital
thread without making every disagreement a build-blocking event. A human confirms the bill of materials;
physics solvers carry parts of the authority. The example is useful precisely because correspondence and
gating are separate design choices — the coupling can hold without a hard gate deciding the instant the two
diverge.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble wires each model to a build-time drift-and-parity check where the modeled correspondence is meant to
stay exact. A mismatch fails the relevant check immediately, and the disagreement surfaces the instant it
appears rather than the instant it bites. That proves the declared map and territory disagree; the
independent invariant is what decides why the disagreement matters.

<!-- takeaway -->
Correspondence makes a representation trustworthy over the surface it claims to describe. An independent
obligation gives the system something meaningful to be wrong against. Authority needs both a judgment and a
mechanism — and neither implies that every disagreement deserves the same gate.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

## The semantic gap

<!-- section-terms: semantic-gap, earliest-legible-boundary, model-drift -->

<!-- point: an-obligation-can-be-perfectly-stated-and-unenforceable-at-the-boundary-you-check | An obligation can be perfectly stated and still be unenforceable at the boundary where you try to check it. | terms: semantic-gap, earliest-legible-boundary -->
The second failure is placement. **An obligation can be perfectly stated and still be unenforceable at the
boundary where you try to check it.** If the property exists only over a whole task, no single commit holds
enough meaning to decide it. If data exfiltration appears only as a pattern across calls, no individual
system call carries the whole property. The mismatch between the semantics an obligation *requires* and the
semantics a boundary *exposes* is the **semantic gap**. [ref:semantic-gap] shows the fix — lift the check to
where the meaning is legible.

> *A footnote on where the placement rule comes from.* It echoes the end-to-end argument in system design:
> a function that needs end-to-end knowledge cannot in general be implemented completely at a lower layer
> that lacks that knowledge [cite:saltzerreedclark1984e2e]. Their setting was where to place *function* in a
> distributed system; the setting here is where to place *authority* over engineering work. The design
> instinct is the same — put the deciding mechanism where the semantics needed to decide the property are
> available.

<!-- label: semantic-gap -->
<!-- figure: assets/semantic-gap-levels.svg | *The Semantic Gap.* Bottom row: small events — commit, commit, call, call, turn — where the property spreads across many and is invisible in any one, so the events do not yet carry enough semantics to decide it. Top row: the whole unit of work, where the obligation becomes legible. Lift the mechanism to the earliest boundary where the evidence the obligation needs is assembled. -->

<!-- point: model-drift-at-the-commit-is-the-wrong-granularity | Checking model drift at the commit is the wrong granularity; the property belongs to the completed unit of work. | terms: model-drift, semantic-gap -->
<!-- index-def: semantic-gap -->
<!-- index-example: model-drift -->
Take a concrete obligation: *if this task substantially changes the call graph, the corresponding
architectural model must still agree when the task closes.* A real task runs through several intermediate
commits, and temporary disagreement during those commits can be legitimate. A pre-commit check therefore
acts too early — it sees a fragment while the property belongs to the completed unit of work. Enforce at the
commit and you are checking a sentence for grammar before the paragraph is written. Operating-system
security lives with the same shape constantly: the OS sees individual system calls, but "this program is
exfiltrating data" is a *pattern across* calls, invisible in any one. At task return, the accumulated
changes, model updates, tests, and other completion evidence can be weighed together — which is exactly
where the companion repository places its definition-of-done audit, over the whole unit of work rather than
any single commit inside it.

<!-- gloss: Epic | A tracked unit of work larger than one commit: a feature or effort with its own file, plan, and definition of done, carried out over several agent dispatches. -->

<!-- gloss: Definition of done (DoD) | The explicit list of conditions a unit of work must meet to count as finished — tests present, docs updated, the model in sync — checked at the end rather than assumed. -->

<!-- point: place-authority-at-the-earliest-boundary-where-the-obligation-is-legible-and-enforceable | The design rule: place authority at the earliest boundary where the obligation is both legible and enforceable. | terms: earliest-legible-boundary, semantic-gap -->
<!-- index-def: earliest-legible-boundary -->
The agent-return check is one instance of a rule that runs under the whole of this Part:

> **Place authority at the earliest boundary where the obligation is both legible and enforceable.**

*Earliest* matters because delay makes a failure more expensive to repair. *Legible* matters because moving
earlier than the semantics permit produces false refusals or meaningless checks. The rule explains the whole
mechanism menu at a glance:

- **Types** act as soon as an expression is formed — a shape is legible the instant it is written.
- **Action hooks** act at one tool call, one command — where a single move is legible.
- **Task-completion checks** act at the work-unit boundary — where "done" first becomes readable across a body of commits.
- **Deployment gates** act after broader evidence has accumulated — the last boundary before the artifact is accepted.

And some properties stay illegible until later still — a leak visible only across ten calls, a cost overrun
visible only across a season of jobs. No boundary makes them readable sooner. The correct placement is not
the earliest boundary *available*; it is the earliest boundary that can actually decide the obligation.

<!-- point: the-part-two-rhyme-coarsest-representation-earliest-boundary | The rhyme: Modeling chooses the coarsest representation where the question is answerable; Alignment chooses the earliest boundary where the obligation is legible. | terms: semantic-gap, earliest-legible-boundary, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
Part 2 and Part 3 therefore carry companion selection rules, one discipline read twice:

> **Modeling** — *choose the coarsest representation at which the engineering **question** becomes answerable.*
>
> **Alignment** — *choose the earliest boundary at which the **obligation** becomes legible and enforceable.*

Both rules reject accidental detail. Modeling avoids reasoning over detail the question does not need;
Alignment avoids binding a decision before the evidence needed to make it exists. Choose the grain wrong in
Part 2 and the reasoner has nothing to hold; choose the boundary wrong here and the gate has nothing to read.

<!-- point: real-boundaries-are-sometimes-approximate-a-roughly-right-hook-beats-the-perfect-one | Real boundaries are sometimes approximate: semantic placement is an engineering trade-off, not a demand for a theoretically perfect hook. | terms: earliest-legible-boundary, semantic-gap -->
Real boundaries are sometimes approximate. In DocAble's own orchestration loop, the ideal moment to
preserve durable session state would be the instant before a lossy compaction, but waiting for that exact
hook leaves too little budget to write the hand-off comfortably. So the implemented mechanism fires earlier,
when context is becoming full *and* the durable record has gone stale. The example is a useful warning:
semantic placement is an engineering trade-off, not a demand for a theoretically perfect boundary.
