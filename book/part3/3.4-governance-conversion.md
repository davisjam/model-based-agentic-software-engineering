# Governance Conversion

<!-- point: a-governed-environment-has-an-ex-ante-and-an-ex-post-half | A governed environment has an ex-ante and ex-post half. | terms: ex-ante-governance, ex-post-governance -->
One rule runs this whole chapter: **an ex-post failure becomes an ex-ante control when judgment
classifies the recurrence and encodes it in the environment.** That is the move that turns a mistake
you caught once into a check the environment enforces on every agent after. You cannot write a
trustworthy environment down in advance; the question is how trust grows from the failures no
specification predicted.

Both halves give intent authority; they differ only in when the intent is known. The ex-ante
half installs explicit invariants up front — the properties you can name before the first line
of code, made binding as checks. The ex-post half is the spine's last station, *improving the
environment from what happened*: a failure classified by judgment enriches either the
representation or the controls, so the correction outlives the incident. Represented intent
becomes authority whether the intent was written down in advance or learned from a wall the
loop kept hitting.

It has two halves, because governance does. Some things you know in advance and write into a
specification up front. But real engineering rarely works that way — you discover what you are
building by building part of it. So a governed environment carries what you can decide *before* you
start and what you can only learn *along the way*, and the promotion rule is the hinge between them.

That an agentic team needs governance of its own is beginning to draw formal treatment. Recent
academic work proposes a domain-specific language for governance policies across mixed human-agent
projects [cite: ait2025governance]. MAGE converges with that direction at a different grain: not a
single policy language, but a repertoire of governance mechanisms drawn from a production case.

## Ex-ante: everything you can decide up front

<!-- section-terms: ex-ante-governance, specification-driven-development -->

<!-- point: ex-ante-write-down-what-you-know-and-determinize-it-with-cheap-checks | Write down what you know; determinize it with cheap checks. | terms: ex-ante-governance, specification-driven-development -->
<!-- index-def: ex-ante-governance -->
<!-- index-def: specification-driven-development -->
Some things you genuinely know from the beginning, and you should write every one of them down.
This is specification-driven development, and where you can do it, you should. A coding style guide
is the everyday example — Google has one, Airbnb has one — full of rules like "every function carries
a comment" or "we never call `exec` outside this one approved place." Give the agent those rules, then
write *deterministic checks* for them, or better, have the agent write the checker. If a program's
output has a required format, the checker that confirms it is cheap and the agent implements it.
Deciding the right policy and maintaining its fit are not cheap — a cost this Part later proves.

## Ex-post: everything you learn by building

<!-- section-terms: ex-post-governance, governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: ex-post-you-build-governance-as-failures-reveal-themselves | You build governance ex-post, as failures reveal themselves. | terms: ex-post-governance -->
<!-- index-def: ex-post-governance -->
But you will not know everything, and pretending otherwise is the mistake. You will discover that
performance is a problem and you have no performance model, that a model version has a tic — every
LLM's fondness for the em-dash is the running joke — and that the tics move when you upgrade. So you
build governance *ex-post*: after the fact, iteratively, strapping on new mechanisms as failures
reveal themselves and the software's real properties emerge. You define what you can up front, and
stay willing to discover the rest.

<!-- point: ex-post-is-where-most-real-mechanisms-come-from-not-a-fallback | Ex-post is where most real mechanisms come from. | terms: ex-post-governance, governance-conversion -->
The honest version of the loop is less tidy than the ex-ante story makes it sound. You set up the
governance you can imagine in advance, launch the work, and then **watch it burn and try to fix it.**
The failures you did not foresee teach you which mechanism to build next. Ex-post governance is not a
fallback for a weak plan; it is where most of the real mechanisms come from, because most of the real
failures are the ones no specification predicted.

<!-- point: the-engine-is-to-hold-a-quality-goal-with-a-mechanism-placed-up-front-or-after-a-failure-seen-twice | Hold a quality goal with a mechanism that keeps it. | terms: governance-conversion, constraints-and-sensors -->
<!-- index-def: governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: constraints-and-sensors -->
The engine of the whole discipline is what you do to hold a quality goal: **you set up a mechanism
that keeps it.** You place some mechanisms up front, from what you know about the domain, and add more
in response to a recurring or consequential failure — a mistake caught in yesterday's manual audit
becomes today's automatic check, enforced on every agent thereafter instead of re-caught by
inspection. That conversion runs the printer loop's own shape (introduced in
[Engineering the Loop](1.3-loops-and-models.html)), escalated: when the loop keeps hitting one wall,
judgment turns the failure class into a durable mechanism ([ref:governance-conversion-loop]). The
payoff is an environment left more capable of absorbing the next change. Conversions accrue — the
positive dual of churn, a dynamic Part 6 names and bounds.

None of this is new as an instinct. Manufacturing stops the line at a defect and reshapes the work so
it cannot recur; resilience engineering treats an incident as a signal about the system, not a verdict
on the operator [cite: hollnagel2006resilience]; site reliability practice turns each outage into a
blameless postmortem whose action items become durable work [cite: beyer2016sre]; Shingo built
mistake-proofing into the fixture itself [cite: shingo1986zqc]. MAGE inherits that family. The changed
ingredient is velocity: the fleet surfaces structural gaps fast enough that the environment accumulates
reliability one class at a time.

## The controls accumulate {#control-growth}

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: the-control-substrate-grew-from-zero | The control substrate grew from zero. | terms: governance-conversion -->
Run the promotion rule for a season and the environment fills with controls — nearly all discovered
ex-post, the obligations no up-front specification would have named. The case project shows the shape:
[ref:control-growth-curve] counts its two hardest control surfaces — project-specific lint files and
gate scripts — at the same four windows the build passed through.

<!-- label: control-growth-curve -->
<!-- figure: assets/control-growth.svg | The controls accumulate across four windows — lint files (0 to 336 to 595 to 747) and gate scripts (0 to 20 to 76 to 102). Both start at literal zero: the substrate is post-prototype. The climb is steepest through mechanization and rises after, one class at a time. -->

The curve is the accumulation, not its cause. Those lint files carry 993 registered lint specs at the
final window, each a policy the environment now enforces on every agent. What the count cannot separate
is which controls a failure drove and which the domain suggested up front — the two modes interleave by
design, and no commit trail cleanly splits them. But the discipline that converts a failure is
documented and named: a paired fix-and-lint tag marks the commits that ship a code fix beside the check
that forbids its recurrence, appearing in 208 commits, and 27 lints name a specific dated incident in
their own text, spot-checked as genuine conversions. Read the curve as a documented, growing
discipline, then — not a measured causal rate. [data: control-growth]

Conventional wisdom offers a rough rule for how large the environment should grow — about one line of
tests per line of production — and [The timeline and the work](5.3-the-timeline-and-the-work.html)
measures the ratio MAGE reaches against it. Either way, a mechanism takes one of two forms, and the
difference between them is the difference between a firewall and a smoke detector.

Before the synthesis, watch the conversion run outside one project — the failure that motivates it is
mine, the pattern that answers it is not.

> **Field note — the pipeline that ran open-loop.**
> One of our document-remediation pipelines had every mark of a clean design: a single driver, one
> phase taxonomy, a typed contract on every pass, a startup sort that checked the order. It had drifted
> anyway. The declared order and the actual order had come apart at seven places, and the seven were
> not scattered — they sat exactly where the accessibility-AI loop churned hardest, each new trick wired
> straight into the run path, skipping the registry. The registry was a constraint that held for the
> passes routed through it; the inline additions routed around it, and my one guard only ever looked at
> registered passes. The repair was the missing sensor: a parity check that compares the passes that
> actually run against the declared graph and fails when an unregistered one appears. That is the
> conversion in miniature — a failure I could not afford to catch by hand became a check the environment
> now runs on every build.

<!-- worked-examples: governance-conversion -->
### Example — Docker
Docker's exploratory agents filed an issue for every anomaly they found, and the humans reading those
issues stopped listening. Rather than re-tune the prompt, the team routed every discovering skill
through a project-manager that deduplicates and confirms before a report is filed, so the noisy sensor
became a governed one. A parallel miss — self-evaluation kept passing its own work — became a rule that
a separate reviewer grades every change, because generation is not evaluation.

### Example — Shopify
Shopify treats each recurring failure as a reason to change the environment, not to re-instruct the
agent. Private sessions taught no one twice, so the coding agent was made public-channel-only and every
session became a searchable artifact mined into skills and defaults; free-roaming generation over
millions of lines proved unreliable and was replaced by declarative, reproducible workflows. The
converted artifact is often soft — a skill, a default — yet the loop holds: a failure seen twice becomes
a fixture the next agent inherits.

### Example — Zenseact
Zenseact's agents do knowledge work over the company's own systems, and their sharpest failure is a
routing miss: the skill a task needs exists, but the retrieval layer fails to surface it. The miss is
detected and recorded as telemetry, an automated pipeline analyzes it, and the disclosure mechanism is
updated so later agents get the right context. The failure is diagnosed as an environmental one — the
environment failed to present the right representation — and the repair targets the environment, not
the model.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble runs the conversion as a standing discipline. A defect caught once in a manual audit is encoded
the same day as an automatic check the environment enforces on every later agent, and the two commits —
the fix and the lint that forbids its recurrence — ship together under one paired tag. That tag marks
two hundred-odd commits, and the control substrate it grew is the curve shown earlier: from zero to
hundreds of registered checks, each one a failure that will not return.

<!-- takeaway -->
The shared move is *governance conversion*: a failure caught once is promoted into a standing mechanism
the environment enforces on every later agent, so the correction outlives the incident that taught it.

## What it adds up to

<!-- point: the-governed-environment-is-determinize-what-you-can-then-convert-every-surprise-into-a-constraint-or-sensor | Determinize what you can; convert every surprise into a mechanism. | terms: governed-environment, constraints-and-sensors -->
<!-- index-def: governed-environment -->
Step back and this is what you are really doing: describing policies for how the engineering work
should proceed, and letting those policies produce the rules of the road the agents operate within.
That combination — determinize what you can, then convert every recurring surprise into a constraint or
a sensor — *is* the **governed engineering environment**.

<!-- point: governance-extends-the-reasoning-horizon-by-holding-the-obligation-in-the-environment | Governance extends the reasoning horizon: the environment holds the obligation. | terms: governed-environment, reasoning-horizon, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->
<!-- index-example: reasoning-horizon -->
One frame ties this chapter to the last Part. A model extends the reasoning horizon by externalizing
*knowledge*: the fleet reasons through a compact picture instead of rebuilding the whole system in
working state. A governed environment extends the same horizon by externalizing *judgment*: the
engineer decides once that some invariant must hold, encodes it as a constraint or a sensor, and the
environment carries that obligation afterward. Neither agent nor engineer keeps "don't break invariant
X" in working memory, because the ground they stand on keeps it. Modeling externalizes knowledge and
alignment externalizes judgment; both buy back the same scarce thing — how much of a system a bounded
reasoner can hold coherent at once.

## The residual: goals no mechanism reaches

<!-- section-terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->

<!-- point: some-goals-split-into-neither-because-the-failure-is-an-absence-nobody-specified | Some goals split into neither, being an absence nobody specified. | terms: residual -->
<!-- index-def: residual -->
The synthesis reads as if it settles everything. It does not: some goals split into **neither**. No
cheap sensor can see the failure and no constraint can scope it away, because the failure is an
**absence nobody specified**, and you cannot watch for the violation of a rule no one wrote or forbid
an action the design never mentioned.

<!-- point: the-residual-is-a-human-job-that-sets-the-throughput-ceiling | The residual is a human job setting the throughput ceiling. | terms: residual, judgment-is-the-scarce-resource -->
Security remediation is the clean case. A missing authorization check has **no failing test — by
definition.** If a test caught it, the check was specified; if it was specified, it would not be
missing. The gap is invisible because nothing ever named it. So the goal stays a **human** job:
someone has to look at the system and *know* the check should be there. Call this the **residual** —
the third outcome beside prevent and detect. Which of your goals land in it sets your real throughput
ceiling: the mechanized goals run at fleet speed, and these run at yours.

<!-- point: the-residual-is-the-complement-of-the-two-moves-not-a-third-value-on-the-soft-hard-axis | The residual is the complement of both moves, not soft. | terms: residual, soft-vs-hard-governance -->
This is **not** the soft end of the soft-versus-hard axis you just met. A soft mechanism — a
structured model that aims the agent, a convention that reminds — is still a *mechanism*: a named
artifact that raises the odds and that you can point to. The residual has none. There is nothing to
name, nothing to tag as a constraint or a sensor, nothing to add to the census. It is the *complement*
of the two moves, not a third value on the axis.

<!-- point: naming-the-residual-is-the-honest-edge-that-makes-the-mechanized-core-credible | Naming the residual is the honest edge that earns trust. | terms: residual -->
Naming the residual costs the method nothing; a method that claims to mechanize everything is the one
to distrust. The residual is the honest edge that makes the mechanized core credible — it says plainly
what the catalogue does *not* cover, and that admission is what lets you trust the coverage it does claim.

<!-- point: the-residual-is-not-a-fixed-wall-authoring-the-missing-spec-pulls-a-goal-out | The residual is not fixed; authoring the spec shrinks it. | terms: residual, mirror-vs-spec -->
Nor is the residual a fixed wall. You can sometimes pull a goal out of it by **authoring the missing
spec.** The same absence that defeats a sensor defeats a *derived* model: a model read off the code
records the missing check as correctly absent, and the drift gate agrees with the bug — a mirror
cannot see what was never specified (the same absence, restated for models, 2.2). A journey test that
asserts only "the flow ran" greens while the user's task is broken, until a human authors the terminal
task-closure assertion that says what *done* means (3.5). Everything in this chapter governed the code
the fleet writes. But the fleet does not only write code — it operates a system, and the operating
judgment an agent lacks is its own residual; the next chapter captures as much of it as a runbook can hold.
