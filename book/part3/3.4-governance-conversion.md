# Growing the Governed Environment

<!-- point: a-governed-environment-grows-ex-ante-and-ex-post-with-conversion-the-hinge | A governed environment grows ex ante and ex post; governance conversion is the ex post route. | terms: ex-ante-governance, ex-post-governance, governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: governed-environment -->
No engineering team begins with a complete catalogue of everything its environment will eventually need
to govern. Some obligations are known in advance: an API must require authentication, a state transition
is forbidden, a format mutation must cross a particular seam. Others become visible only when the work
exposes a failure nobody represented or controlled. A governed environment acquires durable structure in
two ways: **ex ante from obligations already known, and ex post from failures and surprises.**

<!-- index-def: governance-conversion -->
The ex post route is **governance conversion**. A failure is observed; engineering judgment
identifies the recurring class behind it; the missing representation, obligation, evidence, evaluation, or
consequence is made durable; subsequent work inherits the result. The repair fixes this instance. Conversion changes
the environment.

## Ex ante: encode what you already know

<!-- section-terms: ex-ante-governance, specification-driven-development -->

<!-- point: ex-ante-encode-obligations-you-can-state-before-the-work-begins | Ex ante: encode the obligations you can state up front, and stop paying judgment for them. | terms: ex-ante-governance, specification-driven-development -->
<!-- index-def: ex-ante-governance -->
<!-- index-def: specification-driven-development -->
Start with obligations you can state before the work begins and whose recurrence justifies making them
durable. A required output shape may become a schema; a forbidden dependency may become an architectural
relation plus a validator; a service boundary may become a permission or narrow API; a legal lifecycle
may become a closed transition table. Encode recurring obligations when the environment can carry them
more cheaply and reliably than repeated judgment.

Part IV turns this into a method for starting and migrating systems; here the distinction is simply that
these obligations are known before failure forces the issue.

## Ex post: convert what the work teaches

<!-- section-terms: ex-post-governance, governance-conversion, constraints-and-sensors -->

<!-- point: ex-post-diagnose-what-was-missing-do-not-assume-every-failure-wants-a-gate | Ex post: diagnose what was missing; not every failure wants another gate. | terms: ex-post-governance, governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: ex-post-governance -->
The harder half begins when the system surprises you. A recurring failure may reveal that the environment
lacks a representation, that an obligation was never stated, that the relevant evidence is not observable,
that a validator is too weak, or that a judgment exists but carries no authority. **Do not assume every
failure wants another gate. Diagnose what was missing.**

<!-- point: the-conversion-lands-in-one-of-five-places | The conversion lands in one of five places: model, invariant, sensor, validator, or authority. | terms: governance-conversion, constraints-and-sensors -->
The conversion can therefore land in several places:

1. **Missing representation / semantics → improve the model.** Make the fact or relation durable at the
   abstraction where the question becomes answerable.
2. **Missing obligation → state the invariant or policy.** Give future work something independent to be
   wrong against.
3. **Missing evidence → add a sensor or trace.** Make the relevant state observable.
4. **Missing evaluation → strengthen the validator.** Turn evidence into a repeatable judgment.
5. **Missing consequence → add or strengthen a constraint or gate.**

<!-- point: modeling-and-alignment-form-a-feedback-loop-without-one-being-a-prerequisite | Modeling and Alignment form a feedback loop in practice without one being a prerequisite for the other. | terms: modeling-principle, alignment-principle, governance-conversion -->
This is why Modeling and Alignment form a feedback loop in practice without one being a logical
prerequisite for the other. Alignment failures can expose missing representation; better representation
can expose new properties worth aligning. The environment improves by moving recurring work out of
episodic judgment and into durable structure. [ref:governance-conversion-fig] draws the diagnosis.

<!-- label: governance-conversion-fig -->
<!-- figure: assets/governance-conversion.svg | *Governance conversion.* When a recurring failure exposes missing representation, obligation, evidence, evaluation, or consequence, encode the missing structure at the appropriate layer. Future work inherits the result as engineering capital when the durable structure continues to lower future cost or uncertainty. -->

## Correct Judgment Without Consequence

<!-- section-terms: governance-conversion, ex-post-governance -->

<!-- point: judgment-without-consequence-governance-conversion-can-delete-a-capability | Governance conversion can add a mechanism; it can also delete a capability the environment should never have offered. | terms: governance-conversion, ex-post-governance -->
A merge automation drained a backlog of agent worktrees into the mainline each tick. Most rebased cleanly;
a fraction conflicted, and a single convenience option resolved those conflicts by taking one side
automatically. On overlapping edits it silently dropped content. An audit already understood the danger: a
guard detected when a conflict touched substrate-class files, judged the resolution unsafe, and logged it
correctly — as a warning only. The operation proceeded, and the tree lost content.
[ref:judgment-without-consequence] traces the cascade.

<!-- label: judgment-without-consequence -->
<!-- figure: assets/judgment-without-consequence.svg | *Correct judgment without consequence.* The system observed the dangerous condition and evaluated it correctly, but no authoritative mechanism acted on the verdict. The durable repair removed the unsafe capability rather than making the warning louder. -->

The failure was not principally missing evidence. The system had evidence. Nor was it missing evaluation:
the audit correctly identified the dangerous condition. What it lacked was consequence. The warning
described a condition the environment still permitted.

The durable repair did not make the warning louder. It removed the dangerous auto-resolution fallback and
escalated ambiguous merges to a human instead. **Governance conversion can add a mechanism. It can also
delete a capability the environment should never have offered.** Where a warning marks a known-dangerous
condition that should eventually bind, its temporary status should be explicit, with an owner and
resolution condition.

## Engineering Capital

<!-- section-terms: engineering-capital, governance-conversion, modeling-principle, alignment-principle -->

<!-- point: governance-conversion-forms-engineering-capital-the-positive-stock | Governance conversion forms engineering capital — the concrete positive stock software engineering has lacked a name for. | terms: engineering-capital, governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: engineering-capital -->
Governance conversion motivates a useful accounting concept: **engineering capital**. The field has a
mature metaphor for the opposite condition. Technical
debt is a liability carried forward: an expedient decision today imposes interest on future change.
Engineering capital is the stock of durable engineering assets that lowers the future cost or uncertainty
of change.

<!-- point: the-asset-is-something-you-can-point-at | The asset is not "the team learned" — it is something you can point at. | terms: engineering-capital, constraints-and-sensors -->
The asset is not an abstract claim that "the team learned." It is something you can point to. Engineering
capital is concrete: a model removes repeated reconstruction; a constraint removes a failure class; a
sensor makes hidden state observable; a validator turns repeated judgment into a repeatable decision; a
gate gives the decision consequence; traceability preserves correspondence; a skill or runbook packages
judgment for reuse. Each changes the starting conditions inherited by future work. Engineering capital is
therefore broader than "more gates": the conversion of a recurring failure can land in a model, in a
packaged procedure, or in an authoritative mechanism, and each can contribute to the same stock of reusable engineering capacity.

<!-- point: capital-earns-a-return-lower-marginal-engineering-cost | Capital earns a return: lower marginal engineering cost. Conversion is capital formation. | terms: engineering-capital, governance-conversion -->
Engineering capital earns a return by lowering the marginal cost of future work: fewer facts
rediscovered, fewer failures repeated, fewer reviews spent on mechanically decidable questions, and less
rework after violations. Governance conversion is therefore more than learning from failure. It turns an
episode of engineering judgment into productive structure that continues to pay after the episode ends.

<!-- point: capital-depreciates-so-accumulation-cannot-be-the-objective | Capital depreciates; maintenance, reconciliation, and retirement manage it, so accumulation cannot be the objective. | terms: engineering-capital, governed-environment -->
The engineered environment evolves with the system it governs.[^lehman] Capital also **depreciates**. A model can drift from the system it represents. A validator can encode an
assumption the architecture has outgrown. A sensor can become noisy; a gate can impose more delay than
the failure it prevents; a once-important control can survive long after its obligation disappears.
Maintenance preserves useful capital; reconciliation repairs assets that no longer match the system; retirement removes assets
whose carrying cost now exceeds their return. This is why accumulation cannot be the objective. A governed
environment with more mechanisms is not necessarily richer than one with fewer. What matters is the future
engineering value those mechanisms continue to produce.

[^lehman]: Lehman's work on software evolution made the analogous observation about software itself: software used in a changing real-world environment must continually adapt, and its complexity tends to increase unless effort is spent maintaining or reducing it. MAGE extends that caution to the engineered environment surrounding the software. Models, validators, constraints, and procedures evolve too — and can become liabilities when they no longer fit what they govern. See Lehman & Ramil 2003 [cite: lehman2003evolution].

<!-- point: debt-and-capital-are-symmetric-stocks-that-compound | Technical debt and engineering capital are symmetric stocks — both accumulate, both change the economics of later change, both compound. | terms: engineering-capital -->
Debt makes future work pay again for today's expedience; capital lets future work inherit today's
judgment. Governance conversion is one way engineering effort stops being consumed once and becomes
reusable structure.

## Where Authority Stops

<!-- section-terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->

<!-- point: where-a-goal-lands-turns-on-three-questions-starting-with-explicit-in-usable-form | Where a goal lands turns on three questions; the first is whether the obligation is explicit in a form the environment can use. | terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->
Not every quality goal should end in a deterministic gate. A consequential concern may remain
unrepresented, require judgment, be mechanically evaluated without authoritative consequence, or be
governed authoritatively. [ref:honest-gaps] shows the four possible stopping points.

<!-- label: honest-gaps -->
<!-- figure: assets/residual-decision-tree.svg | *Where authority stops.* A concern may remain unrepresented, require judgment, be evaluated without authoritative consequence, or be governed. These are design choices, not maturity levels; different obligations should stop at different points. -->

<!-- point: the-residual-is-an-obligation-nobody-made-explicit-enough-for-the-environment-to-use | The residual is an obligation nobody made explicit enough for the environment to use. | terms: residual, judgment-is-the-scarce-resource -->
<!-- index-def: residual -->
**Residual.** The obligation is not explicit enough for the environment to use, so an engineer or agent
must reconstruct the missing semantics.

<!-- point: an-explicit-obligation-may-still-resist-cheap-mechanical-evaluation | An explicit obligation may still resist cheap mechanical evaluation — an honest boundary, not failed Alignment. | terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->
**Judgment required.** The obligation is explicit, but available mechanisms cannot evaluate it adequately.
Architecture may be represented while a design trade-off still requires expert judgment.

<!-- point: observed-only-is-explicit-and-evaluated-but-un-gated-because-a-gate-is-a-commitment | Evaluated only: explicit and evaluated, yet un-gated — because a gate is a commitment. | terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->
**Evaluated only.** The environment can produce and evaluate relevant evidence, but no authoritative
consequence follows.

<!-- point: a-governed-obligation-carries-an-authoritative-consequence-appropriate-to-its-risk | A governed obligation carries an authoritative consequence appropriate to its risk; the four states are a design space, not a ladder. | terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->
**Governed.** The obligation carries an authoritative consequence appropriate to its risk: the action is
constrained, the violation is refused, or admission depends on the verdict.

<!-- point: three-different-reasons-non-enforcement-is-sometimes-the-engineered-result | An obligation can stop short of a gate for three different reasons; non-enforcement is sometimes the engineered result. | terms: residual, governance-conversion -->
DocAble contains examples of each non-governed stopping point, each for a different reason. Some cost authority is
deliberately dormant because whether to impose a hard daily budget is a policy choice. A
governance-conversion ledger stays an instrument rather than a gate because turning a conversion rate into
a target would invite gaming. Some accessibility judgments remain outside deterministic enforcement because
the available representation does not contain enough semantics to evaluate them reliably. **Non-enforcement
is sometimes the engineered result.**

<!-- point: the-mechanism-that-discovers-need-not-carry-the-authority | The mechanism that discovers an obligation need not be the one that carries its authority; Part IV follows that migration. | terms: sensor, governance-conversion -->
The mechanism that discovers an obligation need not be the mechanism that ultimately carries its authority.
DocAble's primitive-density lint remained an audit signal because high density was only a smell. The
migration it provoked produced more specific types, boundaries, and drift controls whose predicates could
justify stronger consequences. Part IV follows that migration in detail.

<!-- point: the-point-is-not-the-rightmost-leaf-it-is-knowing-where-each-obligation-sits-and-why | The point is not to drive every obligation to the rightmost leaf; it is to know where each sits and why — and conversion has now created an estate to govern. | terms: governance-conversion, residual, governed-environment -->
Different obligations should stop in different places. Governance conversion closes recurring gaps when
doing so is worth the cost. It also creates the next problem: the environment now contains an expanding
portfolio of controls, and those controls can themselves become inconsistent, redundant, or orphaned.
