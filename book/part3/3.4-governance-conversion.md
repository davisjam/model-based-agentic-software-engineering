# Growing the Governed Environment

<!-- point: a-governed-environment-grows-ex-ante-and-ex-post-with-conversion-the-hinge | A governed environment grows ex ante and ex post; governance conversion is the hinge. | terms: ex-ante-governance, ex-post-governance, governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: governed-environment -->
No engineering team begins with a complete catalogue of everything its environment will eventually need
to govern. Some obligations are known in advance: an API must require authentication, a state transition
is forbidden, a format mutation must cross a particular seam. Others become visible only when the work
exposes a failure nobody represented or controlled. A governed environment therefore grows in two
directions: **ex ante from what engineering already knows, and ex post from what experience teaches.**

<!-- index-def: governance-conversion -->
The hinge between them is **governance conversion**. A failure is observed; engineering judgment
identifies the recurring class behind it; the missing knowledge, obligation, evidence, or authority is
made durable; subsequent work inherits the result. The repair fixes this instance. Conversion changes
the environment.

## Ex ante: encode what you already know

<!-- section-terms: ex-ante-governance, specification-driven-development -->

<!-- point: ex-ante-encode-obligations-you-can-state-before-the-work-begins | Ex ante: encode the obligations you can state up front, and stop paying judgment for them. | terms: ex-ante-governance, specification-driven-development -->
<!-- index-def: ex-ante-governance -->
<!-- index-def: specification-driven-development -->
Start with obligations you can state before the work begins and whose recurrence justifies making them
durable. A required output shape may become a schema; a forbidden dependency may become an architectural
relation plus a validator; a service boundary may become a permission or narrow API; a legal lifecycle
may become a closed transition table. The point is not to mechanize every preference. It is to avoid
repeatedly paying human or probabilistic judgment for rules the environment can already carry cheaply.

Part IV turns this into a method for starting and migrating systems. Here the important distinction is
temporal: these are obligations the engineer can install before a failure forces the issue.

## Ex post: convert what the work teaches

<!-- section-terms: ex-post-governance, governance-conversion, constraints-and-sensors -->

<!-- point: ex-post-diagnose-what-was-missing-do-not-assume-every-failure-wants-a-gate | Ex post: diagnose what was missing; not every failure wants another gate. | terms: ex-post-governance, governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: ex-post-governance -->
The harder half begins when the system surprises you. A recurring failure may reveal that the environment
lacks a representation, that an obligation was never stated, that the relevant evidence is not observable,
that a validator is too weak, or that a judgment exists but carries no authority. **Do not assume every
failure wants another gate. Diagnose what was missing.**

<!-- point: the-conversion-lands-in-one-of-five-places | The conversion lands in one of five places: model, invariant, sensor, validator, or authority. | terms: governance-conversion, constraints-and-sensors -->
The conversion can therefore land in several places:

1. **Missing knowledge or semantics → improve the model.** Make the fact or relation durable at the
   abstraction where the question becomes answerable.
2. **Missing obligation → state the invariant or policy.** Give future work something independent to be
   wrong against.
3. **Missing evidence → add a sensor or trace.** Make the relevant state observable.
4. **Missing evaluation → strengthen the validator.** Turn evidence into a repeatable judgment.
5. **Missing consequence → add or strengthen authority.** Constrain the action or attach an appropriate
   gate.

<!-- point: modeling-and-alignment-form-a-feedback-loop-without-one-being-a-prerequisite | Modeling and Alignment form a feedback loop in practice without one being a prerequisite for the other. | terms: thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment, governance-conversion -->
This is why Modeling and Alignment form a feedback loop in practice without one being a logical
prerequisite for the other. Alignment failures can expose missing representation; better representation
can expose new properties worth aligning. The environment improves by moving recurring work out of
episodic judgment and into durable structure. [ref:governance-conversion-fig] draws the diagnosis.

<!-- label: governance-conversion-fig -->
<!-- figure: assets/governance-conversion.svg | *Governance Conversion.* A recurring failure is not automatically a request for another gate. Diagnose what the environment lacked — knowledge, an obligation, evidence, a judgment, or a consequence — encode the missing representation or control, and let future work inherit the result. The inherited structure is engineering capital. -->

Read the branches as a diagnosis, not a reflex: a failure surfaces, you ask *what was missing*, and the
answer routes to a model, an invariant, a sensor, a validator, or a constraint or gate. Whichever branch
you take, the output is the same in kind — durable engineering structure that future work inherits
without rediscovering the lesson.

> **Field note — the pipeline that ran open-loop.**
> A document-remediation pipeline had a declared phase registry and a startup check, yet seven production
> passes had been wired directly into the run path around the registry. The existing constraint held only
> for work that already crossed the modeled seam. The repair was a parity sensor: compare the passes that
> actually run with the declared graph and fail when one is unregistered. The incident changed the
> diagnosis from "remember to use the registry" to "the environment cannot see bypasses." One repeated
> judgment became a durable check.

## Engineering capital

<!-- section-terms: engineering-capital, governance-conversion, thesis-modeling, thesis-alignment -->

<!-- point: governance-conversion-forms-engineering-capital-the-positive-stock | Governance conversion forms engineering capital — the concrete positive stock software engineering has lacked a name for. | terms: engineering-capital, governance-conversion -->
<!-- index-def: engineering-capital -->
Governance conversion gives software engineering something it has long had difficulty naming: a concrete
form of **engineering capital**. The field has a mature metaphor for the opposite condition. Technical
debt is a liability carried forward: an expedient decision today imposes interest on future change.
Engineering capital is the positive stock. It is engineering work performed once that reduces the cost,
uncertainty, or judgment required by work that follows.

<!-- point: the-asset-is-something-you-can-point-at | The asset is not "the team learned" — it is something you can point at. | terms: engineering-capital, constraints-and-sensors -->
The asset is not an abstract claim that "the team learned." It is something you can point to. A structural
model saves later engineers and agents from reconstructing the same architecture from code. A constraint
removes a failure class from the available action space. A sensor makes a previously invisible condition
observable. A validator turns recurring judgment into a repeatable decision. A gate gives that decision
consequence. Traceability preserves the relationship between the representation and the thing it
describes. Each mechanism changes the starting conditions inherited by the next task.

<!-- point: capital-earns-a-return-lower-marginal-engineering-cost | Capital earns a return: lower marginal engineering cost. Conversion is capital formation. | terms: engineering-capital, governance-conversion -->
Capital earns a return. The return appears as lower marginal engineering cost: fewer facts rediscovered,
fewer failures repeated, fewer reviews spent on mechanically decidable questions, less rework after
violations, and larger units of autonomous work that can proceed without surrendering assurance.
Governance conversion is therefore not merely "learning from failure." It is **capital formation**:
turning an episode of engineering judgment into productive structure that continues to pay after the
episode is over.

<!-- point: capital-depreciates-so-accumulation-cannot-be-the-objective | Capital depreciates; maintenance, reconciliation, and retirement manage it, so accumulation cannot be the objective. | terms: engineering-capital, governed-environment -->
The engineering environment evolves with the system it governs.[^lehman] Capital also **depreciates**. A model can drift from the system it represents. A validator can encode an
assumption the architecture has outgrown. A sensor can become noisy; a gate can impose more delay than
the failure it prevents; a once-important control can survive long after its obligation disappears.
Maintenance preserves useful capital; reconciliation repairs impaired capital; retirement removes assets
whose carrying cost now exceeds their return. This is why accumulation cannot be the objective. A governed
environment with more mechanisms is not necessarily richer than one with fewer. What matters is the future
engineering value those mechanisms continue to produce.

[^lehman]: Lehman's work on software evolution made the analogous observation about software itself: software used in a changing real-world environment must continually adapt, and its complexity tends to increase unless effort is spent maintaining or reducing it. MAGE extends that caution to the engineering environment surrounding the software. Models, validators, constraints, and procedures evolve too — and can become liabilities when they no longer fit what they govern. See Lehman & Ramil 2003 [cite: lehman2003evolution].

<!-- point: debt-and-capital-are-symmetric-stocks-that-compound | Technical debt and engineering capital are symmetric stocks — both accumulate, both change the economics of later change, both compound. | terms: engineering-capital -->
This gives technical debt and engineering capital a useful symmetry. Debt makes future work pay again for
today's expedience. Capital lets future work inherit today's judgment. Both are stocks accumulated over
time, both affect the economics of later change, and both can compound. The governance-conversion loop is
the mechanism by which engineering effort moves from being consumed once to becoming an asset the
environment can reuse.

## The Honest Gaps

<!-- section-terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->

<!-- point: where-a-goal-lands-turns-on-three-questions-starting-with-explicit-in-usable-form | Where a goal lands turns on three questions; the first is whether the obligation is explicit in a form the environment can use. | terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->
Not every quality goal should end in a deterministic gate. Where a goal lands depends on three questions —
is the obligation explicit in a form the environment can use, can it be mechanically evaluated adequately,
and does authority act on the result — and [ref:honest-gaps] walks the three questions and the four
outcomes.

<!-- label: honest-gaps -->
<!-- figure: assets/residual-decision-tree.svg | *The Honest Gaps.* An obligation may remain implicit (the RESIDUAL — a human or agent must reconstruct it), may be explicit but resist cheap mechanical evaluation (JUDGMENT REQUIRED), may be mechanically evaluated with no gate acting on the result (OBSERVED ONLY), or may carry an authoritative response (GOVERNED). Modeling can move an obligation rightward by making its semantics explicit; Alignment decides what authority the environment attaches once the obligation can be acted on. The progression is a design space, not a maturity ladder. -->

<!-- point: the-residual-is-an-obligation-nobody-made-explicit-enough-for-the-environment-to-use | The residual is an obligation nobody made explicit enough for the environment to use. | terms: residual, judgment-is-the-scarce-resource -->
<!-- index-def: residual -->
Not every quality goal should end in a deterministic gate. The first gap is the **residual**: an
obligation nobody has made explicit enough for the environment to use. The system may still depend on it,
but an engineer, reviewer, or agent has to reconstruct the missing judgment. This is one place where weak
representation turns directly into human work.

<!-- point: an-explicit-obligation-may-still-resist-cheap-mechanical-evaluation | An explicit obligation may still resist cheap mechanical evaluation — an honest boundary, not failed Alignment. | terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->
An explicit obligation may still resist cheap mechanical evaluation. Architecture may be represented while
a design trade-off remains contextual; a requirement may be clear while satisfaction still requires expert
judgment. That is not failed Alignment. It is an honest boundary on what the available mechanism can
establish.

<!-- point: observed-only-is-explicit-and-evaluated-but-un-gated-because-a-gate-is-a-commitment | Observed only: explicit and evaluated, yet un-gated — because a gate is a commitment. | terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->
A third state is **observed only**. The obligation is explicit, the environment can produce and evaluate
relevant evidence, but no gate acts on the result. A production cost ceiling is deliberately in this state
in one of the running examples: the system measures and reports cost without refusing production work.
That may be the right design. **A gate is a commitment**, and a poorly calibrated validator given blocking
authority converts uncertainty into outages.

<!-- point: a-governed-obligation-carries-an-authoritative-consequence-appropriate-to-its-risk | A governed obligation carries an authoritative consequence appropriate to its risk; the four states are a design space, not a ladder. | terms: residual, constraints-and-sensors -->
Finally, a governed obligation has an authoritative consequence appropriate to its risk: the action is
constrained, the violation is refused, or admission depends on the validator's result. The progression
from implicit to explicit to observable to binding is a design space, not a maturity ladder. Different
obligations should stop in different places.

<!-- point: a-missing-authorization-requirement-gives-the-shape-of-the-residual | A missing authorization requirement gives the shape: with no requirement stated, a derived model faithfully records the missing check as absent. | terms: residual -->
A missing authorization requirement gives the shape. If no system-specific requirement, invariant, or
general policy makes the obligation visible to the environment, a derived model may faithfully record the
missing check as absent and no local test need fail. Someone still has to know that authorization belongs
there. Once that obligation is stated independently, the gap can move into the mechanically governed
region.

<!-- point: the-point-is-not-the-rightmost-leaf-it-is-knowing-where-each-obligation-sits-and-why | The point is not to drive every obligation to the rightmost leaf; it is to know where each sits and why — and conversion has now created an estate to govern. | terms: governance-conversion, residual, governed-environment -->
The point is not to drive every obligation to the rightmost leaf. It is to know where each one sits and
why. Some residuals justify better models; some judgment should remain human; some observed properties
have not earned a gate. Governance conversion closes recurring gaps when doing so is worth the cost. It
also creates the next problem: the environment now contains an expanding portfolio of controls, and those
controls can themselves become inconsistent, redundant, or orphaned.
