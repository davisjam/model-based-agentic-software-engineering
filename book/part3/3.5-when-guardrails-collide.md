# When Controls Become a System

<!-- gloss-only: Governance graph | A structured model whose nodes are the fleet's own governance mechanisms (each tagged by the event it fires on and the shared resources it reads, writes, or locks) and whose edges are the conflicts between them over a shared resource, in a closed taxonomy of contradiction, contention, ordering, and soft-versus-hard; the dynamic dual of a static catalogue of controls, holding the interaction dimension a list omits. -->

<!-- point: every-control-reasonable-alone-the-system-of-controls-still-wrong | Every control can be reasonable in isolation while the control machinery as a whole remains inconsistent, incomplete, or obsolete. | terms: governance-graph, constraint -->

As governance mechanisms accumulate, they become control machinery: the constraints, sensors, validators, gates, permissions, and other mechanisms through which the environment enforces its obligations. The machinery can fail even when every individual control is reasonable. Two controls can impose incompatible demands; their execution order can be ambiguous; an obsolete control can survive after its purpose disappears; an important obligation can remain uncovered. None of these failures is visible by reading one mechanism's code.

<!-- point: successful-environments-accumulate-machinery-so-the-estate-is-a-portfolio-to-manage | Governance conversion and up-front design accumulate control machinery; that machinery becomes an engineering object to maintain, reconcile, and sometimes retire. | terms: governance-graph, engineering-capital -->

<!-- point: alignment-turned-on-itself-a-model-of-the-controls-made-binding-on-the-controls | Alignment turned on itself: a model of the controls, enforced on the controls. | terms: governance-graph -->

These interactions require engineers to maintain the controls as a system. MAGE asks three questions: **What controls exist? How do they interact? Which obligations do they cover?** These questions produce three useful views: a catalogue, an interaction graph, and a coverage relation. Represent that machinery explicitly, so engineers and tools can reason about how its controls compose and what obligations they cover—and, once the machinery materially affects engineering work, hold it to obligations of its own.

## Catalogue the Controls

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph -->

<!-- point: the-catalogue-gives-each-control-a-stable-identity | Catalogue each control: a stable identity, the boundary it acts at, the resources it touches, the obligation it serves, and its governance role. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->

Start by giving each control a stable identity: the event or boundary at which it acts, the resources it reads or changes, the obligation or failure class that justifies it, and its governance role or roles. The catalogue supplies stable identities for the interaction and coverage views, so engineers and tools need not rediscover controls from scripts, hooks, CI files, and institutional memory.

<!-- point: a-catalogue-alone-cannot-show-conflict-or-a-missing-control | A catalogue can describe each control individually, but it cannot reveal conflicts between them; conflicts appear in the relationships among controls. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->

A catalogue can describe each control individually, but it cannot reveal conflicts between them. Those conflicts appear in the relationships among controls.

## Model Interactions Between Controls

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->

<!-- point: each-collision-lives-in-the-space-between-two-controls-drawn-nowhere | A collision lives between two controls, drawn nowhere until the graph draws it. | terms: shared-resource, governance-graph -->

Two collisions from the case study make it concrete. In the first, one control required a squashed commit while another treated the resulting broad diff as unauthorized scope expansion. Each was reasonable alone; together they imposed contradictory demands on the same commit set. In the second, two hooks fired on the same turn-end with no order declared between them, each able to block, and their composition was left implicit. Neither collision lives in any one control's code. Each lives in the space *between* two controls. That relation had not been represented. [ref:collision-edge] draws it.

<!-- label: collision-edge -->
<!-- figure: assets/collision-edge.svg | *The conflict is the edge.* Two controls may be individually valid yet impose incompatible demands on the same resource. The interaction belongs to the relation among the controls and shared resource, not necessarily to either control's implementation. -->

<!-- point: the-interaction-graph-joins-controls-by-shared-resource | The interaction graph uses controls as nodes and shared resources as the principal join key; controls need not call one another to collide. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->

The interaction graph uses controls as nodes and shared resources as the principal join key. A resource may be ordinary — a lock, a file, a commit-set, a deployment slot — or operational, such as a lifecycle event or a context budget. Controls need not call one another to interact; they only need to make potentially incompatible demands on the same modeled resource.

<!-- point: docable-implemented-governance-graph-is-intentionally-partial | DocAble's implemented governance graph is intentionally partial — a small high-value slice of the larger machinery, not comprehensive coverage. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->

DocAble's implemented governance graph is intentionally partial. It models only the high-value interactions for which recurring questions justified explicit representation, including those around the orchestrator lifecycle.

<!-- point: shared-resource-is-a-useful-conservative-relation-not-a-proof-of-completeness | Shared resource is a useful conservative relation, not a proof that the graph captured every interaction. | terms: shared-resource, semantic-gap -->

A shared resource is a conservative signal of interaction, not proof that every conflict has been captured. Within the represented surface, it lets engineers and tools ask useful questions before the controls interact in production work: which controls fire on this event, which touch this resource, which pairs have no declared order, and which known combinations impose incompatible requirements.

<!-- point: decidable-edges-a-lint-settles-semantic-ones-a-validator-or-human-judges | Some candidate conflicts are mechanically decidable; others are semantic — a sensor produces evidence of the interaction, a validator or human judges whether it is a conflict. | terms: governance-graph, semantic-gap -->

Some candidate conflicts are mechanically decidable: a lock cycle, two exclusive writers, or two same-slot mechanisms with no permitted ordering. Others are semantic. A static check can **sense** that two controls touch the same commit-set; a validator — or a human reviewer — must decide whether their requirements actually conflict. Again the four roles matter: producing evidence of an interaction is not the same as judging the interaction invalid. In the motivating commit-set collision, the machine could identify the shared resource, but judgment was still required to recognize the requirements as contradictory. The graph lets the environment surface the interaction for evaluation before the controls collide during work.

<!-- point: diagnose-the-conflict-kind-before-weakening-an-obligation | Not every conflict between controls reflects a conflict between the obligations they enforce; incompatible obligations require revisiting requirements, while interfering mechanisms may be changed without weakening either obligation. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->

Not every conflict between controls reflects a conflict between the obligations they enforce. Two obligations may genuinely be incompatible, requiring engineers to revisit the requirements or specification. But compatible obligations can also be enforced by mechanisms that interfere with one another. In that case, changing the mechanisms or how they interact may resolve the conflict without weakening either obligation.

<!-- point: three-turn-end-hooks-one-shared-slot | Worked example: three turn-end hooks, each reasonable alone, all writing the turn's closing message; the governance graph records exactly these three conflict edges among twenty-five mechanisms. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->

DocAble now represents the second of these conflicts in an explicit governance model. Among the automations that run when an agent finishes a turn, three matter here: one discourages premature termination, another prompts reflection on failures, and a third checks for stranded work. All three write to the turn's closing message. The governance graph contains twenty-five mechanisms and three conflict edges, all between these hooks. [ref:turn-end-ordering] shows their shared resource and the modeled ordering.

<!-- label: turn-end-ordering -->
<!-- figure: assets/turn-end-ordering.svg | *Three turn-end hooks, one shared resource.* All three hooks write to the turn's closing message, producing three conflict edges. The governance model computes an execution order, but the generic runtime driver that would enforce it has not yet been implemented. -->

<!-- point: conflict-classes-and-resolutions-form-a-closed-vocabulary | The graph distinguishes four conflict kinds (contradiction, contention, ordering, soft-versus-hard) and five resolutions (merge, order, separate, accept, open); the goal is not a conflict-free graph but understood, appropriately resolved interactions. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->

The graph distinguishes four kinds of conflict: *contradiction*, when requirements are incompatible; *contention*, when mechanisms compete for a resource; *ordering*, when their execution sequence matters; and *soft versus hard*, when one mechanism can prevent another from achieving its purpose. Each conflict can be resolved by *merging* mechanisms, *ordering* them, *separating* their execution points, *accepting* the interaction, or leaving it *open* for later resolution. The goal is not a conflict-free graph; it is a graph whose consequential interactions are understood and appropriately resolved.

The three turn-end conflicts are ordering conflicts. Their pairwise constraints establish a sequence: the premature-termination guard runs first, reflection second, and the stranded-work check last. The model computes this order by topological sorting; a cycle produces an error rather than an arbitrary sequence.

<!-- point: what-the-model-enforces-today-is-detection-ordering-and-a-drift-report | What exists today: the model records conflicts and computes the resolution order, and a separate audit-only check reports model-vs-configuration discrepancies; the runtime driver is not yet built, so the order is a checkable design decision, not an enforced one. | terms: governance-graph -->

The model currently records conflicts and computes their resolution order. A separate check reports discrepancies between the model and the harness's configured mechanisms. The generic runtime driver that would execute hooks according to the computed order has not yet been built. The order is therefore an explicit, checkable design decision, not yet an automatically enforced one.

<!-- point: the-pileup-was-discovered-by-collision-the-model-reverses-that-direction | The original conflict was discovered when two hooks interfered during development; with the pair recorded, similar interactions can be examined as part of the design rather than discovered through failures. | terms: governance-graph -->

The original conflict was discovered only after two hooks interfered during development. The model records that pair and lets engineers identify similar interactions before installing another automation. Instead of discovering conflicts through failures, engineers can examine them as part of the design.

<!-- point: governance-interaction-graph-candidate-conflict-condition | Conflict detection follows a read/write criterion — same firing event, and one mechanism writes a resource the other accesses; classifying the conflict and resolving it are separate, later judgments. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource, semantic-gap -->

The governance model records conflicts according to a simple criterion. Each mechanism declares an execution event — its *slot* — and the sets of resources it reads (\(R\)) and writes (\(W\)). Two mechanisms have a **potential conflict** when they execute at the same event and at least one writes a resource the other accesses. Two readers do not conflict. The resulting graph \(G = (V, E)\) records these interactions as edges: an undirected edge joins distinct mechanisms \(u\) and \(v\) when

<!-- eq: \operatorname{slot}(u) = \operatorname{slot}(v) \land (W_u \cap R_v) \cup (R_u \cap W_v) \cup (W_u \cap W_v) \neq \varnothing -->

An edge identifies an interaction requiring attention; it does not establish that the underlying obligations are incompatible. The model separately classifies the conflict and records its resolution.

## Trace Obligations to Mechanisms

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph, governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: a-catalogue-and-a-graph-do-not-say-what-should-be-governed-but-is-not | A catalogue says what exists and a graph says where controls collide; neither says what should be governed but is not. | terms: governance-graph, governance-conversion -->

Interactions are only half of governing the control machinery. A catalogue tells you what controls exist; an interaction graph tells you where controls may collide. Neither tells you what *should* be governed but is not. For that, join the mechanisms to the obligations or failure classes that justify them.

<!-- point: coverage-is-a-requirements-traceability-problem-gap-vs-orphan | Coverage is a small requirements-traceability problem: an obligation with no adequate mechanism is a coverage gap; a mechanism with no obligation is an orphan. | terms: governance-graph, governance-conversion -->

The result is a small requirements-traceability problem. An important obligation with no adequate mechanism is a coverage gap. A mechanism with no current obligation, failure class, or documented purpose is an orphan. The useful question is not "how many controls do we have?" It is whether consequential obligations have appropriate coverage and whether every surviving control can still explain why it exists. [ref:census-trio] sets the three views side by side over the same control machinery.

<!-- label: census-trio -->
<!-- figure: assets/census-trio.svg | *Three views of the control machinery.* The catalogue identifies controls; the interaction view relates controls through shared resources; the coverage view relates controls to the obligations that justify them. Stable identities connect the three views while letting each answer a different engineering question. A missing mechanism appears as a coverage gap; an untraced mechanism appears as a candidate orphan. -->

<!-- point: gap-is-under-governance-orphan-is-governance-debt-neither-automatically-bad | A coverage gap is an uncovered obligation; an orphan is a mechanism with no current purpose. Neither is automatically bad — an orphan is a finding to reconcile, not proof to delete. | terms: governance-graph -->

Two findings do the diagnostic work, and neither verdict is automatic.

- **Coverage gap.** A consequential obligation for which the intended evidence or enforcement is not adequately supplied.
- **Orphan.** A mechanism that no longer traces to a current obligation, failure class, or documented purpose. An orphan is a finding to reconcile, not proof that the mechanism should be deleted.

## Add, Reconcile, and Retire Controls

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph, teetering-tower, engineering-capital -->

<!-- point: conversion-is-reactive-coverage-is-proactive-a-healthy-environment-needs-both | Governance conversion is reactive and coverage auditing is proactive; a healthy environment needs both. | terms: governance-graph, governance-conversion -->

Governance conversion and coverage auditing provide complementary pressures. Conversion adds durable structure when experience exposes a recurring gap; coverage auditing finds controls that are missing, redundant, or obsolete.

<!-- point: the-same-audit-pushes-against-permanent-accumulation-capital-is-not-the-number-of-controls | The same audit pushes against permanent accumulation: engineering capital is not the number of controls. | terms: engineering-capital, teetering-tower -->

The same audit also pushes against permanent accumulation. A control whose purpose has disappeared should be reconciled or retired; two controls that now overlap may be consolidated; a mechanism whose maintenance cost exceeds the failure it prevents may no longer be worth carrying. This echoes {{sec:governance-conversion}}: **engineering capital is not the number of controls.** A control that no longer produces value has depreciated into overhead; the task now is to reconcile or retire it.

<!-- point: the-meta-model-stays-connected-where-controls-are-modeled-explicitly | Where DocAble models controls explicitly, entries can be anchored to their implementations and drift-checked, so a mis-placed control fails loud. | terms: traceability, teetering-tower -->

The control model must remain connected to the controls it represents. Where DocAble models controls explicitly, entries can be anchored to their implementations and checked for drift, so a control the rule cannot place fails explicitly rather than dropping into a silent default bucket. This applies the broader rule from {{chapter:modeling}}: if engineers and agents are going to reason through a model of the control machinery, the model must stay connected to the territory it claims to describe.

<!-- point: stop-the-recursion-unless-experience-earns-more-model-the-estate-that-exists | Stop the recursion unless experience earns more: a governance model can itself become bureaucracy. | terms: governance-graph, teetering-tower -->

Do not recursively govern every relation merely because it can be modeled. Extend the control model only when a recurring engineering question or observed failure justifies the added machinery.

The control-machinery model is grounded primarily in the originating case.[^estate]

<!-- worked-examples: governs-accumulated-governance -->

### Example — Two Safety Rules {other}

A building's fire system unlocks doors when it detects smoke; its security system may lock those same doors under an intrusion policy. Each rule is sensible in isolation while the pair demands incompatible states of the same resource. The contradiction belongs to the interaction, not to either rule's own logic.

### Example — DocAble

DocAble models its governance mechanisms as a system rather than only as individual scripts. Controls are anchored to their implementations, interaction relations identify shared resources, and coverage traces controls back to the obligations or failures that justify them. The model does not make every possible conflict mechanically decidable. It exposes enough structure to ask, before the next collision, which mechanisms interact and which important obligations have no adequate coverage.

<!-- takeaway -->

Governance conversion adds durable engineering structure; governing the control machinery keeps that structure coherent. Catalogue the controls, expose their interactions, trace them to the obligations they serve, and retire machinery that no longer earns its cost.

<!-- point: alignment-distinguishes-what-must-be-governed-from-what-may-remain-open | Alignment must preserve two complementary freedoms — freedom of implementation (which acceptable realizations remain available) and freedom to extend the environment (whether additional controls can coexist); the realization-space model answers the first, the governance graph the second. | terms: governance-graph, alignment-principle -->

Alignment must preserve two kinds of freedom. The first is freedom of *implementation*: which acceptable realizations remain available after obligations are enforced. The second is freedom to *extend the engineering environment*: whether additional controls can coexist with those already installed. The realization-space model in {{sec:constraints-sensors-validators-gates}} addresses the first question; the governance graph addresses the second.

<!-- point: governing-the-governors-is-second-order-not-a-license-for-unrestricted-agents | Modeling and Alignment extend to the machinery that performs engineering work; the result is not unrestricted agent autonomy — agents may explore without being authorized to commit, and engineers still decide which freedoms to delegate. | terms: governance-graph -->

This extends Modeling and Alignment to the machinery that performs engineering work. Product obligations motivate controls; controls interact; and those interactions must themselves be modeled and governed. The result is not unrestricted agent autonomy. Agents may be permitted to explore alternatives without being authorized to merge, deploy, or otherwise commit them. Engineers must still decide which freedoms can be delegated and which require stronger controls.

<!-- point: part-3-treated-alignment-as-a-separate-discipline-part-2-enlarges-the-scale | Alignment does not require comprehensive Modeling; permissions, types, APIs, and local gates enforce local obligations directly, and explicit models expand the semantic reach of that enforcement. | -->

Alignment does not require comprehensive Modeling: permissions, types, APIs, and local gates can enforce local obligations directly. Explicit models expand the semantic reach of that enforcement.

{{chapter:alignment}} developed Alignment in five moves: locate the boundary where the obligation can be enforced; state an independent obligation, including its tolerance where acceptable variation matters; distinguish constraints, sensors, validators, and gates; convert recurring failures and judgments into durable structure; and govern the resulting control machinery as a system in its own right.

<!-- point: modeling-and-alignment-are-both-on-the-table-but-that-is-not-yet-a-method | Modeling and Alignment are both on the table, but that is not yet a method; Chapter 4 turns those decisions into method. | -->

Modeling and Alignment are now both on the table. That still is not a method. The remaining problem is judgment: when a question deserves a model, when an obligation deserves enforcement, how work should move through the environment, and what to do when the available representation or control is insufficient. {{chapter:one-problem-many-models}} turns those decisions into method.
