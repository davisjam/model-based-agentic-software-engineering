# Governing the Governance

<!-- gloss-only: Governance graph | A structured model whose nodes are the fleet's own governance mechanisms (each tagged by the event it fires on and the shared resources it reads, writes, or locks) and whose edges are the conflicts between them over a shared resource, in a closed taxonomy of contradiction, contention, ordering, and precedence; the dynamic dual of a static catalogue of controls, holding the interaction dimension a list omits. -->

<!-- point: a-system-of-controls-has-the-one-failure-a-list-cannot-show | Every control correct, the system of controls still wrong. | terms: agent-stack, constraint -->
Every control in a system can be correct and the system of controls still be wrong. The stack gives
you four layers to steer at, and a governed fleet fills them — a hook at the harness firing at
turn-end, a lint at the application gating a commit, a skill aiming a task, a mediator rationing a
lock. Each earns its place alone. But the more mechanisms you install, the more pairs of them touch
the same thing, and a system of controls carries the one failure a list of controls cannot show:
two of them making **incompatible demands on the same thing at the same moment**. This is the
Part's question in its last form, turned recursive: how do you govern the governance itself?

This is Alignment turned on itself. The Part has spent its chapters giving represented intent
authority over the artifact; now the pile of controls is itself an artifact, and the same move
applies — a model of the controls, made binding on the controls. The environment that enforces
the models is held to a represented account of its own guardrails.

<!-- point: each-collision-lives-in-the-space-between-two-controls-drawn-nowhere | A collision lives between two controls, drawn nowhere. | terms: shared-resource -->
Two collisions from the case study make it concrete. In the first, one mechanism required several
commits to be collapsed into a single squashed one, while another read the resulting combined diff
as unauthorized scope expansion and flagged it — two controls, one commit-set, contradictory demands,
caught only when a real commit tripped both. In the second, two hooks fired on the *same* turn-end
with no order declared between them, each able to block, and the pipeline simply hoped they composed.
Neither collision lives in any one control's code. Each lives in the space *between* two controls, and that space was
drawn nowhere — until [ref:collision-edge] draws it.

<!-- label: collision-edge -->
<!-- figure: assets/collision-edge.svg | *The Conflict Is the Edge.* Read Control A alone and it is correct; read Control B alone and it is correct. What they demand of the one resource they share collides — and that collision is drawn nowhere until the graph draws it. -->

Answering it takes three views of the same set of controls: a catalogue of what exists, an
interaction graph of what collides, and a coverage census of what sits un-watched. This chapter
builds them in turn.

## Drawing what lies between the controls

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph -->

<!-- point: the-governance-graph-nodes-are-mechanisms-edges-are-conflicts-joined-by-shared-resource | Nodes are mechanisms; edges are conflicts over a shared resource. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->
The Governance Graph draws it. The nodes are the mechanisms — each tagged with the event it
fires on and the resources it reads, writes, or locks. The edges are the conflicts: two controls
colliding over one resource, in a small closed set of ways to collide. The exact value set is
reference material — it lives with the mechanism, not here. What the chapter needs is the join key,
and the join key is the shared resource. Two controls that never call each other still collide if
they both touch the turn-end slot, so the resource — not the call — is what makes them comparable.
Type the turn-end slot and the context budget as resources too, and hook-pileup and injection-pressure
fall under the same conflict machinery as a contended lock. One graph represents all of it.

<!-- point: the-shared-resource-is-the-only-cross-lifecycle-coupling-surface-and-keeps-the-graph-sparse | The shared resource is the only cross-lifecycle coupling surface. | terms: shared-resource, semantic-gap -->
The shared resource is also what lets the graph reason *across lifecycles*. Most collisions are
local — two controls in one lifecycle touching one nearby resource. But a hook that fires at turn-end
and a check that fires at commit live in different lifecycles, and they still collide if they both reach
for one typed resource. The resource is the *only* coupling surface between lifecycles: controls in
different lifecycles that share no resource cannot conflict, which is what keeps the graph sparse. And
this is the [semantic gap](3.2-models-and-the-semantic-gap.html) again — the collision is a property of the
*interaction*, invisible in either control's own code, legible only at the level where both are drawn.
The graph lifts the check to that level.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [Governance Graph](appendix-b-governance-graph.html).

<!-- point: the-graph-is-the-catalogues-dynamic-dual-splitting-decidable-edges-from-judgment-ones | The graph is the catalogue's dynamic dual. | terms: governance-graph, soft-vs-hard-governance -->
A catalogue lists the controls; it can't show which two will crash into each other. The graph
draws exactly those crashes: which pairs collide, and over what. It is the edges a list of nodes
omits — the static catalogue's dynamic dual. And the two halves of that answer split cleanly. Some
collisions are **mechanically decidable** — a same-slot pair with no declared order, a lock cycle; a
lint settles those. Others need **judgment** — are two constraints on a commit-set truly incompatible,
or do they compose? No lint decides that; a reader must. So each edge carries its nature, and the
decidable edges route to a sensor while the semantic ones route to a human prompt. This is where the
motivating commit-set collision was really lost: the machine could flag that two controls touched the
commit-set, but only judgment could call the contradiction. The graph makes both the flag and the
prompt land *before* the control ships rather than at the collision.

<!-- point: the-graph-makes-the-implicit-claim-that-mechanisms-compose-explicit-and-checkable | The graph makes "mechanisms compose" explicit and checkable. | terms: governance-graph -->
The payoff is operational. A governed environment is not just a pile of running mechanisms; it is a
claim that those mechanisms compose. The claim was implicit, checked by collision. The graph makes it
explicit and checkable: ask *what fires on this event, what touches this resource, is this new control
consistent with the ones already wired* — and get an answer at authoring time. The fleet stops merely
running its guardrails and starts modeling how they interact.

## The other question a list can't answer

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph -->

<!-- point: a-list-shows-what-exists-the-graph-what-collides-neither-shows-the-un-watched | A list shows what exists; neither shows what is un-watched. | terms: governance-graph -->
The graph settled one question a flat list could not: which two controls collide. A second question
hides in the same blind spot. A list names every control the fleet installed, and the graph draws
every pair that can crash — but neither says which *target* has nothing watching it at all. A governed
estate grows toward the last painful failure, piling controls onto the target that just hurt while
another accretes nothing, and that imbalance is a property of the whole set, invisible in any single
control's code.

<!-- point: the-census-is-a-third-view-of-one-graph-coverage-per-target | The census is a third view: coverage per target. | terms: governance-graph -->
So the same control node-set carries a **third view**. The list is the controls that exist; the
interaction graph is how two of them collide; the **coverage census** is how many watch each governance
target, and which target has none. Classify every control by the target it guards, roll the set up per
target, and a target with zero controls — or with only soft aims and no hard hold — becomes a first-class
finding. The empty cell is not an absence nobody noticed. It is the estate's own blind spot, named, and
pointing at where the next control should go. [ref:census-trio] sets the three views side by side over
one control set.

<!-- label: census-trio -->
<!-- figure: assets/census-trio.svg | *Catalogue, Graph, Census.* Three views ride on one set of controls. The catalogue lists what exists; the interaction graph draws which two collide over a shared resource; the coverage census bins the same controls by the target each guards and surfaces the target with none. The move that matters is that the graph and the census are **re-views, not new data** — the identical node-set, asked a different question each time. An empty census cell is not an absence nobody noticed; it is the estate's own blind spot, named, and pointing at where the next control should go. -->

<!-- point: the-census-is-a-model-whose-instances-are-the-controls | The census is a model whose instances are the controls. | terms: governance-graph -->
This is a model of the controls themselves — its instances are the controls, keyed by the target each
guards. It turns the book's own method on the governance system: the governance-conversion loop turns
each recurring failure into a control; the census asks whether the portfolio that results is balanced
across everything that needs governing. Governance audits governance. Run first on the real fleet, the
census sorted the controls into the three targets a mechanism can guard — the **agent fleet** that
produces the work, the **models** the fleet reasons through, and the **shipped product** — and found the
agent target fully populated while the other two sat at zero, entirely un-watched until the roll-up made
the gap queryable.

## Growing controls, and auditing the growth

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph, teetering-tower -->

<!-- point: the-fleet-grows-controls-reactively-and-that-growth-is-biased | The fleet grows controls reactively, and that growth is biased. | terms: governance-graph -->
The census has a counterpart, and the two are true opposites over one graph. The fleet grows its controls
the reactive way this book argues for: let velocity expose a failure, convert each recurring one into a
durable guardrail. That reflex *extends* the graph — toward the targets that already failed. A target that
has never bitten draws no new control, so the reactive discipline, left to itself, leaves whole targets
structurally un-watched. It is a feed-back loop with a systematic blind spot.

<!-- point: the-census-is-the-proactive-audit-that-corrects-the-bias | The census is the proactive audit that corrects the bias. | terms: governance-graph -->
The census is the proactive audit that catches exactly that. Governance conversion grows the estate
reactively, failure by failure; the census audits its balance proactively, target by target, and names
the target the reflex never reached. One side grows the estate; the other keeps the growth balanced across
every target rather than only the ones that hurt.

<!-- point: static-analyses-keep-the-census-equal-to-the-territory | Static analyses keep the census equal to the territory. | terms: governance-graph, teetering-tower -->
And the census holds *itself* equal to the territory by the same discipline it enforces on everything else.
Each control's target is **derived from its code anchor**, never hand-declared, so the map cannot drift
from the controls it counts. A control the rule cannot place **fails loud** rather than dropping into a
silent default bucket that would mis-credit the coverage. The roll-up never blocks a commit — it aims the
next control, it does not gate — yet the honesty backstop beneath it is hard. The census even files its own
anchor-drift guard, so the control set censuses the very governor that measures it.

## Keeping the model honest

<!-- section-terms: traceability, teetering-tower -->

<!-- point: keep-the-graph-honest-by-symbol-anchoring-and-a-descriptive-not-aspirational-model | Keep the graph symbol-anchored and descriptive, not aspirational. | terms: traceability, teetering-tower -->
Two disciplines keep the model honest, and both are the book's. Anchor each node to the code
that implements its control by symbol, never by line, so a drift lint re-resolves the anchor on
every build and a control wired but unmodeled reddens the gate: the map stays equal to the
territory, or the graph lies about which collisions are covered. And keep the model
**descriptive** — it draws the controls that exist and checks proposed ones on request; it does
not invent conflict-lints for collisions that have never happened. A governance graph is itself
a governance mechanism, and governance can recurse into bureaucracy: controls spawning controls
until the overhead outweighs what they protect. This graph models the controls that exist and the
conflict classes actually observed — not speculative controls for every imaginable interaction.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [symbol-anchored traceability graph](appendix-b-symbol-anchored-traceability-graph.html).

No external report in the sample governs its governance this way, so the case stands alone here — but the
shape it answers to is ordinary, and a toy draws it before the real machinery does.

<!-- worked-examples: governs-accumulated-governance -->
### Example — Two safety rules {other}
A building's fire system unlocks every door the instant it senses smoke, so no one is trapped. Its
security system seals every door the instant it senses an intruder. Each rule is correct alone. Let both
fire on the same door in the same moment and they demand incompatible states of one lock — a
contradiction nothing in either system's own logic can see, because it lives only in the pair.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble turns its own control set into a model and audits it. The governance graph makes each control a
node tagged by the resource it touches and each collision an edge over a shared resource, so a real
guardrail like the quota gate — which rations the compute the whole fleet draws on — becomes checkable
for conflict against anything else reaching for that resource. The coverage census then bins the same
controls by the target each guards — the agent fleet, the models, the shipped product — and names the
target with nothing watching it. Governance audits governance: the estate that grew one failure at a
time is held to a model of itself.

<!-- takeaway -->
The shared move is to *govern the accumulated governance*: treat the pile of controls as a system in its
own right and model both the collisions between them and the targets none of them watch, so the estate
that grew one failure at a time is audited for the failure a flat list of controls can never show.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

<!-- point: part-3-built-the-environment-over-part-2-models | The environment enforces the models Part 2 built. | -->
This Part gave represented intent authority over the artifact — the layers you reach into, the
obligations a demand answers to, the constraints, sensors, validators, and gates that hold those
obligations, the conversion loop that grows the estate one failure at a time, and the graph that keeps
the accumulated guardrails from colliding. Every one of those checks the code against something it
assumes already sits there: a schema, an invariant, a map of the controls. The environment enforces; it
does not supply what it enforces against. That is the model — the world Part 2 built, which this Part
gave its authority.

Modeling gave the fleet a world it could reason through. Alignment made that world binding. But a
governed environment is not yet a method. Someone still has to decide what to model, where to place
authority, how to run a change through the environment, and what to do when the environment itself
proves incomplete. That is the work of Part IV.
