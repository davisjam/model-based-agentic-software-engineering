# When Controls Become a System

<!-- gloss-only: Governance graph | A structured model whose nodes are the fleet's own governance mechanisms (each tagged by the event it fires on and the shared resources it reads, writes, or locks) and whose edges are the conflicts between them over a shared resource, in a closed taxonomy of contradiction, contention, ordering, and soft-versus-hard; the dynamic dual of a static catalogue of controls, holding the interaction dimension a list omits. -->

<!-- point: every-control-reasonable-alone-the-system-of-controls-still-wrong | Every control can be reasonable in isolation while the control machinery as a whole remains inconsistent, incomplete, or obsolete. | terms: governance-graph, constraint -->
As governance mechanisms accumulate, they become control machinery: the constraints, sensors, validators,
gates, permissions, and other mechanisms through which the environment enforces its obligations. The machinery
can fail even when every individual control is reasonable. Two controls can impose incompatible demands;
their execution order can be ambiguous; an obsolete control can survive after its purpose disappears; an
important obligation can remain uncovered. None of these failures is visible by reading one mechanism's code.

<!-- point: successful-environments-accumulate-machinery-so-the-estate-is-a-portfolio-to-manage | Governance conversion and ex ante design accumulate control machinery; that machinery becomes an engineering object to maintain, reconcile, and sometimes retire. | terms: governance-graph, engineering-capital -->
<!-- point: alignment-turned-on-itself-a-model-of-the-controls-made-binding-on-the-controls | Alignment turned on itself: a model of the controls, enforced on the controls. | terms: governance-graph -->
At that point, the controls themselves have become an engineering system. They must be understood and
maintained as one. MAGE asks three questions: **What controls exist? How do they interact? Which obligations
do they cover?** These questions produce three useful views: a catalogue, an interaction graph, and a
coverage relation. Represent that machinery explicitly, so engineers and tools can reason about how its
controls compose and what obligations they cover—and, once the machinery materially affects engineering work, hold it to obligations of its own.

## Catalogue the Controls

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph -->

<!-- point: the-catalogue-gives-each-control-a-stable-identity | Catalogue each control: a stable identity, the boundary it acts at, the resources it touches, the obligation it serves, and its governance role. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->
Start by giving each control a stable identity: the event or boundary at which it
acts, the resources it reads or changes, the obligation or failure class that justifies it, and its
governance role or roles. The catalogue gives the next two views stable identities to work from instead of
forcing engineers and tools to rediscover controls from scripts, hooks, CI files, and institutional
memory.

<!-- point: a-catalogue-alone-cannot-show-conflict-or-a-missing-control | A catalogue alone cannot tell you whether two sensible controls conflict; that is a relational question, living on the edges between controls. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->
A catalogue alone cannot tell you whether two individually sensible controls conflict. That is a
relational question, and it lives on the edges between controls.

## Model Interactions Between Controls

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->

<!-- point: each-collision-lives-in-the-space-between-two-controls-drawn-nowhere | A collision lives between two controls, drawn nowhere until the graph draws it. | terms: shared-resource, governance-graph -->
Two collisions from the case study make it concrete. In the first, one control required a squashed commit
while another treated the resulting broad diff as unauthorized scope expansion. Each was reasonable alone;
together they imposed contradictory demands on the same commit set. In the second, two hooks fired on the same turn-end with no order declared between
them, each able to block, and their composition was left implicit. Neither collision lives in any one
control's code. Each lives in the space *between* two controls. That relation had not been represented.
[ref:collision-edge] draws it.

<!-- label: collision-edge -->
<!-- figure: assets/collision-edge.svg | *The conflict is the edge.* Two controls may be individually valid yet impose incompatible demands on the same resource. The interaction belongs to the relation among the controls and shared resource, not necessarily to either control's implementation. -->

<!-- point: the-interaction-graph-joins-controls-by-shared-resource | The interaction graph uses controls as nodes and shared resources as the principal join key; controls need not call one another to collide. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->
The interaction graph uses controls as nodes and shared resources as the principal join key. A resource
may be ordinary — a lock, a file, a commit-set, a deployment slot — or operational, such as a lifecycle
event or a context budget. Controls need not call one another to interact; they only need to make
potentially incompatible demands on the same modeled resource.

<!-- point: docable-implemented-governance-graph-is-intentionally-partial | DocAble's implemented governance graph is intentionally partial — a small high-value slice of the larger machinery, not comprehensive coverage. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->
DocAble's implemented governance graph is intentionally partial. It models only the high-value interactions
for which recurring questions justified explicit representation, including those around the orchestrator
lifecycle.

<!-- point: shared-resource-is-a-useful-conservative-relation-not-a-proof-of-completeness | Shared resource is a useful conservative relation, not a proof that the graph captured every interaction. | terms: shared-resource, semantic-gap -->
A shared resource is a conservative signal of interaction, not proof that every conflict has been captured.
Within the represented surface, it lets engineers and tools ask useful questions before the controls
interact in production work: which controls fire on this event, which touch this resource, which pairs have
no declared order, and which known combinations impose incompatible requirements.

<!-- point: decidable-edges-a-lint-settles-semantic-ones-a-validator-or-human-judges | Some candidate conflicts are mechanically decidable; others are semantic — a sensor produces evidence of the interaction, a validator or human judges whether it is a conflict. | terms: governance-graph, semantic-gap -->
Some candidate conflicts are mechanically decidable: a lock cycle, two exclusive writers, or two same-slot
mechanisms with no permitted ordering. Others are semantic. A static check can **sense** that two controls
touch the same commit-set; a validator — or a human reviewer — must decide whether their requirements
actually conflict. Again the four roles matter: producing evidence of an interaction is not the same as
judging the interaction invalid. In the motivating commit-set collision, the machine could identify the
shared resource, but judgment was still required to recognize the requirements as contradictory. The graph
lets the environment surface the interaction for evaluation before the controls collide during work.

<!-- point: diagnose-the-conflict-kind-before-weakening-an-obligation | Distinguish a genuine conflict among obligations from a conflict introduced by the enforcing mechanisms; the first may require revisiting requirements, the second may admit a different mechanism. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->
When controls collide, diagnose before weakening. A genuine conflict among obligations means the
requirements themselves cannot all hold, and resolving it may require revisiting the requirements or the
specification. A conflict introduced by the mechanisms chosen to enforce them is different: the obligations
are compatible, and a different enforcement mechanism — or a declared composition — can dissolve the
collision. Engineers should not immediately weaken an obligation; they should first ask which kind of
conflict they have.

<!-- point: three-turn-end-hooks-one-shared-slot | Worked example: three turn-end hooks, each reasonable alone, all writing the turn's closing message; DocAble's governance graph holds exactly these three conflict edges among twenty-five mechanisms. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->
The second collision above repays a closer look, because DocAble now models it. Several automations run
when an agent finishes a turn. Three are relevant here: one discourages premature termination, one prompts
reflection on failures, and one checks for stranded work. Each purpose is reasonable; the interaction is
the problem, because all three act at the same turn-ending event and write the same logical resource — the
turn's closing message. In DocAble's typed graph of the environment's governance mechanisms, twenty-five
mechanisms are represented, and exactly three conflict edges appear — all among these hooks.
[ref:turn-end-ordering] draws the three hooks and the slot they share.

<!-- label: turn-end-ordering -->
<!-- figure: assets/turn-end-ordering.svg | *Three turn-end hooks, one shared slot.* Three automations fire when an agent ends a turn and all write its closing message — the only three conflict edges among twenty-five modeled mechanisms. All are ordering conflicts on one resource, resolved by an order computed from the model. The order is what a generic runtime driver would run; the driver is a later phase, so nothing enforces it at runtime yet. -->

<!-- point: conflict-classes-and-resolutions-form-a-closed-vocabulary | The model classifies each conflict (contradiction, contention, ordering, soft-versus-hard) and declares its resolution (merge, order, separate, accept, open); the hook edges are ordering conflicts composing to a total order. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource -->
The model classifies each edge within a closed vocabulary: a *contradiction*, where the requirements are
incompatible; *contention*, where both compete for one resource; an *ordering* problem, where both can act
but the sequence matters; or *soft versus hard*, where a hard mechanism can prevent a softer one from
achieving its purpose. Resolutions are equally closed: *merge* the mechanisms, impose an *order*,
*separate* their execution points, *accept* the interaction as harmless, or leave it *open*. All three
hook edges are ordering conflicts resolved by an order, and the pairwise constraints compose to a total
order: the premature-termination guard first, reflection second, the stranded-work check last. The order
is obtained by topologically sorting the modeled constraints; a cycle produces an error rather than an
arbitrary order.

<!-- point: the-pileup-was-discovered-by-collision-the-model-reverses-that-direction | One edge is annotated as the original pileup pair, discovered when the hooks collided; with the graph in place a conflict can be identified before the next automation is installed. | terms: governance-graph -->
One edge carries an annotation naming it the original pileup pair. That problem was discovered the
expensive way, when the hooks collided in production work; the edge was modeled afterward. The reversal is
the point. With the graph in place, the question "which existing mechanisms act at this event, on this
resource?" can be asked — and a conflict identified — *before* another automation is installed, rather
than after the collision.

<!-- point: what-the-model-enforces-today-is-detection-ordering-and-a-drift-report | What exists today: the model, conflict detection, the computed order, and a check that reports model-vs-configuration drift; the generic runtime driver is a deliberately deferred later phase. | terms: governance-graph -->
What exists today is deliberately partial. The model holds the mechanisms and their conflict edges; a
query computes the order a generic driver *would* run the hooks in; and a check reports drift between the
governance model and the harness's wired configuration. The runtime driver that would arbitrate the hooks
from the model is a later phase, not yet built. The modeled order is a design decision made explicit and
checkable — not yet a mechanism that enforces itself.

<!-- point: governance-interaction-graph-candidate-conflict-condition | Formally, a governance-interaction graph pairs mechanisms by shared firing slot and overlapping resources; the condition yields a candidate conflict, not a verdict. | terms: governance-graph, shared-resource, semantic-gap -->
The example generalizes to a compact formal object. Let \(G = (V, E)\) be a governance-interaction graph:
each \(v \in V\) a governance mechanism, each edge a potential conflict. Each mechanism carries a firing
event — its *slot* — a set of resources \(R_v\), and typed requirements on them: reading, writing,
requiring, constraining. A candidate conflict exists when two mechanisms act at the same firing event with
potentially incompatible requirements on overlapping resources:

<!-- eq: \operatorname{slot}(u) = \operatorname{slot}(v) \land \operatorname{overlap}(R_u, R_v) -->

The condition is a candidate test, not a sufficient one. Two mechanisms can safely read the same resource;
the typed requirements decide whether an overlap is problematic. This is the sensing-versus-judging split
drawn above, restated as structure: the graph surfaces the pair, and the requirement types — or a human —
judge the conflict.

## Trace Obligations to Mechanisms

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph, governance-conversion -->

<!-- point: a-catalogue-and-a-graph-do-not-say-what-should-be-governed-but-is-not | A catalogue says what exists and a graph says where controls collide; neither says what should be governed but is not. | terms: governance-graph, governance-conversion -->
Interactions are only half of governing the control machinery. A catalogue tells you what controls exist; an interaction
graph tells you where controls may collide. Neither tells you what *should* be governed but is not. For
that, join the mechanisms to the obligations or failure classes that justify them.

<!-- point: coverage-is-a-requirements-traceability-problem-gap-vs-orphan | Coverage is a small requirements-traceability problem: an obligation with no adequate mechanism is a coverage gap; a mechanism with no obligation is an orphan. | terms: governance-graph, governance-conversion -->
The result is a small requirements-traceability problem. An important obligation with no adequate mechanism
is a coverage gap. A mechanism with no current obligation, failure class, or documented purpose is an
orphan. The useful question is not "how many controls do we have?" It is
whether consequential obligations have appropriate coverage and whether every surviving control can still
explain why it exists. [ref:census-trio] sets the three views side by side over the same control machinery.

<!-- label: census-trio -->
<!-- figure: assets/census-trio.svg | *Three views of the control machinery.* The catalogue identifies controls; the interaction view relates controls through shared resources; the coverage view relates controls to the obligations that justify them. Stable identities connect the three views while letting each answer a different engineering question. A missing mechanism appears as a coverage gap; an untraced mechanism appears as a candidate orphan. -->

<!-- point: gap-is-under-governance-orphan-is-governance-debt-neither-automatically-bad | A coverage gap is an uncovered obligation; an orphan is a mechanism with no current purpose. Neither is automatically bad — an orphan is a finding to reconcile, not proof to delete. | terms: governance-graph -->
Two findings do the diagnostic work, and neither verdict is automatic.

- **Coverage gap.** A consequential obligation for which the intended evidence or enforcement is
  not adequately supplied.
- **Orphan.** A mechanism that no longer traces to a current obligation, failure class,
  or documented purpose. An orphan is a finding to reconcile, not proof that the mechanism should be deleted.

## Add, Reconcile, and Retire Controls

<!-- section-terms: governance-graph, teetering-tower, engineering-capital -->

<!-- point: conversion-is-reactive-coverage-is-proactive-a-healthy-environment-needs-both | Governance conversion is reactive and coverage auditing is proactive; a healthy environment needs both. | terms: governance-graph, governance-conversion -->
Governance conversion and coverage auditing provide complementary pressures. Conversion adds durable
structure when experience exposes a recurring gap; coverage auditing finds controls that are missing,
redundant, or obsolete.

<!-- point: the-same-audit-pushes-against-permanent-accumulation-capital-is-not-the-number-of-controls | The same audit pushes against permanent accumulation: engineering capital is not the number of controls. | terms: engineering-capital, teetering-tower -->
The same audit also pushes against permanent accumulation. A control whose purpose has disappeared should
be reconciled or retired; two controls that now overlap may be consolidated; a mechanism whose maintenance
cost exceeds the failure it prevents may no longer be worth carrying. This echoes {{sec:governance-conversion}}: **engineering capital is not the
number of controls.** A control that no longer produces value has depreciated into overhead; the task now
is to reconcile or retire it.

<!-- point: the-meta-model-stays-connected-where-controls-are-modeled-explicitly | Where DocAble models controls explicitly, entries can be anchored to their implementations and drift-checked, so a mis-placed control fails loud. | terms: traceability, teetering-tower -->
The control model must remain connected to the controls it represents. Where DocAble models controls
explicitly, entries can be anchored to their implementations and checked for drift, so a control the rule
cannot place fails explicitly rather than dropping into a silent default bucket. This applies the broader
rule from {{chapter:modeling}}: if engineers and agents are going to reason through a model of the
control machinery, the model must stay connected to the territory it claims to describe.

<!-- point: stop-the-recursion-unless-experience-earns-more-model-the-estate-that-exists | Stop the recursion unless experience earns more: a governance model can itself become bureaucracy. | terms: governance-graph, teetering-tower -->
Do not recursively govern every relation merely because it can be modeled. Extend the control model only
when a recurring engineering question or observed failure justifies the added machinery.

The control-machinery model is grounded primarily in the originating case.[^estate]

[^estate]: I found no comparable control-machinery model in the external systems reconstructed for {{chapter:software-factories}}.

<!-- worked-examples: governs-accumulated-governance -->
### Example — Two Safety Rules {other}
A building's fire system unlocks doors when it detects smoke; its security system may lock those same
doors under an intrusion policy. Each rule is sensible in isolation while the pair demands incompatible
states of the same resource. The contradiction belongs to the interaction, not to either rule's own
logic.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble models its governance mechanisms as a system rather than only as individual scripts. Controls are
anchored to their implementations, interaction relations identify shared resources, and coverage traces
controls back to the obligations or failures that justify them. The model does not make every possible
conflict mechanically decidable. It exposes enough structure to ask, before the
next collision, which mechanisms interact and which important obligations have no adequate coverage.

<!-- takeaway -->
Governance conversion adds durable engineering structure; governing the control machinery keeps that structure coherent.
Catalogue the controls, expose their interactions, trace them to the obligations they serve, and retire
machinery that no longer earns its cost.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

<!-- point: alignment-distinguishes-what-must-be-governed-from-what-may-remain-open | The purpose of Alignment is not to minimize freedom but to distinguish the choices that must be governed from those that may safely remain open; two complementary freedoms are at stake — freedom of implementation and freedom to extend the environment. | terms: governance-graph, alignment-principle -->
The purpose of Alignment is not to minimize freedom. It is to distinguish the choices that must be governed
from those that may safely remain open. Controls that exclude unacceptable outcomes can permit considerable
freedom within the remaining realization space, and that freedom has value: agents can investigate
alternatives, implementation can reveal information, and engineers can defer commitments until they have
reason to make them. Two kinds of freedom are at issue, complementary rather than identical. Freedom of
*implementation* is the realization-space question from {{sec:constraints-sensors-validators-gates}}: which
choices stay available under the obligations. Freedom to *extend the engineering environment* is the
conflict-graph question: whether the mechanisms constraining those choices can themselves coexist. When
controls collide, the engineering task is therefore not merely to make them consistent, but to preserve as
much useful freedom as the obligations permit.

<!-- point: governing-the-governors-is-second-order-not-a-license-for-unrestricted-agents | The chapter escalates from product obligations through engineering controls to governance of the controls — a second-order application of Modeling and Alignment; the authority to explore is distinct from the authority to commit. | terms: governance-graph -->
The progression of {{chapter:alignment}} has been escalating: obligations on the product, controls in the
engineering environment, and now governance of the controls themselves. This chapter is a second-order
application of Modeling and Alignment — the environment that governs the agents must also govern its own
governance mechanisms. None of it argues for unrestricted agents. Verification may be incomplete, and
exploration can consume resources or cause irreversible consequences; the authority to *explore* must be
distinguished from the authority to merge, deploy, or otherwise commit.

<!-- point: part-3-treated-alignment-as-a-separate-discipline-part-2-enlarges-the-scale | Alignment does not require comprehensive Modeling; permissions, types, APIs, and local gates enforce local obligations directly, and explicit models expand the semantic reach of that enforcement. | -->
Alignment does not require comprehensive Modeling: permissions, types, APIs, and local gates can enforce
local obligations directly. Explicit models expand the semantic reach of that enforcement.

<!-- point: the-part-developed-the-discipline-in-five-moves | The chapter developed the discipline in five moves: boundary, obligation, roles, conversion, meta-governance. | -->
{{chapter:alignment}} developed Alignment in five moves: locate the boundary where the obligation can be enforced; state an
independent obligation, including its tolerance where acceptable variation matters; distinguish
constraints, sensors, validators, and gates; convert recurring failures and judgments into durable
structure; and govern the resulting control machinery as a system in its own right.

<!-- point: modeling-and-alignment-are-both-on-the-table-but-that-is-not-yet-a-method | Modeling and Alignment are both on the table, but that is not yet a method; Chapter 4 turns those decisions into method. | -->
Modeling and Alignment are now both on the table. That still is not a method. The remaining problem is
judgment: when a question deserves a model, when an obligation deserves enforcement, how work should move
through the environment, and what to do when the available representation or control is insufficient. {{chapter:one-problem-many-models}}
turns those decisions into method.
