# Lifecycles and Runbooks

<!-- point: agent-knows-git-not-your-environments-lifecycles-so-write-them-down | The agent lacks your operating context; write it down. | terms: lifecycle, runbook -->
You know what to do when the disk fills up, when two branches conflict, when an incident
pages you at 2 a.m. You know it so well you barely notice you know it. The agent knows
none of it. Out of the box it knows how to commit to Git; what it does not know
is the life cycle of *your* engineered environment. The disk fills, and here is what to do
when the disk fills — that instruction will never be in the agent's context unless you put
it there. It has no operating context, no sense of "how we do things around here," and the
more of that judgment your operation runs on unwritten, the more helpless the agent is the
moment the healthy path ends. So a governed environment for real work writes the knowledge
down — as lifecycles, and as the runbooks that ride on top of them. This chapter captures the
operational judgment you carry in your head so an agent can act on it.

Written down, that judgment is operational intent made explicit — and this chapter's move is to
give it authority. A lifecycle names the healthy path and the way out of each failure; a runbook
encodes each prohibition as a mechanism at the right level where it can. That is the ladder's top
half applied to operations: intent stated as an invariant, then held by the environment rather
than left to whoever is awake at 2 a.m. Represented operational intent becomes authority over the
fleet.

## Lifecycles first, then the failure map

<!-- section-terms: lifecycle, failure-mode-effects-analysis -->

<!-- point: a-lifecycle-is-the-healthy-path-then-a-failure-map-off-each-node | A lifecycle: write the healthy path, then map failures. | terms: lifecycle -->

<!-- index-def: lifecycle -->
Start with the lifecycles, because everything else hangs off them. A lifecycle is the
correct operation of one recurring activity: how a bug report moves from filed to fixed,
how a feature moves from design to shipped. Write the healthy path first. Then, from each
node on that path, map the failure states — the ways this step can go wrong — and for
each failure, the action to take. Think of it as a grid: every node crossed with every
symptom, and in each cell, what to do.

<!-- point: fmea-names-the-way-out-and-the-prohibition-because-the-agent-knows-neither | FMEA-inspired: name each failure's way out and prohibition. | terms: failure-mode-effects-analysis -->
<!-- index-def: failure-mode-effects-analysis -->
This grid is **FMEA-inspired**, after *failure mode and effects analysis* — the reliability
practice that enumerates how a thing can fail and what each failure costs. Full FMEA weighs more
than the book borrows: each failure's causes, its severity, how often it occurs, how readily it is
detected, and the priority ranking that falls out. The book takes the enumeration and adds the part
people skip — how to get *out* of each failure again. You know how to get out. If two branches
conflict, you read both sides, work out what each was trying to accomplish, and reconcile them. If
the disk fills, you find the fat part of the system — the cache, usually — and wipe it. The agent
knows none of this. It cannot find each commit's intent during a merge conflict, and it does not
know the ground rules for your system. So you coach it: where the hot points are, and, in every
failure path, what is *not allowed*. Encode the prohibition as a mechanism at the right level where
you can — a Git hook, an agent hook — and where you cannot, tell the agent plainly.

> *A footnote on the disk-fills failure.* Left to itself, an agent that needs to free a full
> disk may reach for the biggest lever it can name — `rm -rf /` will certainly free the space.
> That is not a hypothetical; agents really do it. It is exactly why you coach the hot points
> *and* the prohibitions, rather than trusting it to guess which fat is safe to trim.

<!-- point: name-the-sin-and-the-way-out-not-the-prohibition-alone | Pair each prohibition with its sanctioned way out. | terms: pink-elephant -->

> **Principle — Pair every prohibition with a sanctioned recovery path.** Naming a forbidden act
> raises an honest worry: does saying "don't" plant the very idea, the way telling someone not to
> picture a pink elephant paints one? In practice the naming helped — but only when the escape was
> named alongside the ban. My orchestrator kept reaching for a destructive Git command to bail out of
> a stuck merge, the one that resets the branch and silently discards the changes already applied, and
> twice lost committed work that way. A bare "never abort the merge" leaves the agent stuck with
> nowhere to go. What worked was pairing the forbidden command with the sanctioned one that clears the
> stuck state *without* dropping the applied work, so the rule reads "not that lever, this one." Name
> the sin alone and you may plant the temptation; name the sin *and the way out*, and you replace it.

## Runbooks: split the deterministic from the judgment

<!-- section-terms: runbook, deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->

<!-- point: a-runbook-splits-into-deterministic-and-judgment-steps-governed-differently | A runbook splits into deterministic and judgment steps. | terms: runbook, deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->

<!-- index-def: runbook -->
Once the healthy path and the failure map are written, the thing you hand the agent is a
**runbook** (or playbook): the steps to take, whether recovering from a failure or
working a fresh ticket. The one discipline that makes a runbook work is to split it into
two kinds of step — the deterministic and the judgment-laden — because the two are governed
completely differently. [ref:runbook-split] fences the one judgment step between two deterministic ends.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [runbook](appendix-a-operational-playbooks.html).

### The deterministic parts get tools

<!-- point: deterministic-parts-get-tools-not-improvisation | Deterministic steps get executable tools, not improvisation. | terms: deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->
<!-- index-def: deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->
Anything that has a single correct outcome should
be an executable script the agent runs, not a procedure it improvises. Leave it to
improvisation and it will get it right most of the time — and you will wish you had
automated it the one time it does not.

<!-- point: determinizing-a-step-buys-a-trace-with-two-consumers | Determinizing buys a trace with two consumers. | terms: reasoning-trace, deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->
Determinizing a step buys you more than a reliable outcome — it buys an **inspectable execution
trace**. An improvised step leaves nothing behind: its run is smoke, gone the moment it finishes.
Pin the step to an executable procedure and the run becomes a record — the inputs it read, the branch
it took, the value it produced — in a fixed shape you can replay and compare.

Be precise about what that trace is. An execution trace is evidence of *what ran* — inputs, branches,
outputs — not the *reasoning* for why a branch was the right one. That rationale is a separate record,
logged around the judgment step when it makes the call. The deterministic ends give you a trace to
replay; the fenced judgment step gives you a decision rationale to audit. Between them they cover the
run.

The trace earns its keep through two consumers. The first is **the next turn of the loop**: last run's
trace is this run's input, so the agent starts from what was already established instead of re-deriving
it, and the loop converges instead of wandering. The second is **a checker that reads the trace
itself** — a reviewer, human or agent, that never watched the step run but can audit the path it took
and flag a run that went where it should not. A bare judgment step gives you a verdict; a determinized
step gives you a trace to hold that verdict to account.

### The judgment parts: the roughest algorithm that still fits

<!-- section-terms: deterministic-vs-judgment-split, rubric -->

<!-- point: judgment-parts-get-the-roughest-algorithm-that-still-fits | Judgment steps get the roughest algorithm that fits. | terms: conditioning-the-search -->

**The judgment parts get the roughest algorithm that still fits.** For a well-constrained
decision, write the algorithm down and let the agent do the glue. For an open-ended one —
a cyber threat hunt, where the premise is that the attacker did something you never
anticipated — do not over-constrain: give heuristics and strategies to explore, and stop
there. Remember that you are biasing the search: steer it down the
wrong path and it will never reach the place you wanted. So if you do not know how to do
the thing, do not write as though you did — give hints. If you truly do not know, say
"solve this open-endedly," and accept the trade: the looser the guidance, the more tokens
the agent spends, and the less likely it is to land.

<!-- point: a-judgment-that-is-a-measurement-gets-a-rubric | A judgment that is a measurement gets a rubric. | terms: rubric -->
<!-- index-def: rubric -->
When a judgment step is really a *measurement*, give it a **rubric**. You cannot write a checker for
"is this painting good" or "is this student essay good," but you can break the question into parts a
reader can assess: how are the topic sentences, how is the technique, are there grammatical errors,
are there blotches from bad storage? Against a rubric the agent makes real judgments — grading each
dimension and returning feedback good enough to act on. What stays with a person is the *final verdict
on taste*, the whole-work call the rubric deliberately declines to reduce to a threshold.

<!-- point: the-pre-canned-brief-is-the-strongest-form-templating-delegation-itself | The pre-canned brief templates delegation itself. | terms: pre-canned-brief -->
<!-- index-def: pre-canned-brief -->
The strongest form of this is the **pre-canned brief**. When an orchestrator agent hands work to a
sub-agent, you do not want it inventing, each time, how to explain the task. You want a template it
adapts — the work already articulated in writing. The companion repository leans on exactly this:
every dispatch is composed from a template that pre-places the required instructions, and a
brief-linter checks the template is intact before the agent launches. The delegation itself becomes a
governed step, not a fresh improvisation each time.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [brief-linting](appendix-a-brief-linting.html).

<!-- point: typing-each-runbook-step-makes-the-deterministic-judgment-split-mechanical | Typing each step makes the deterministic-judgment split mechanical. | terms: deterministic-vs-judgment-split, runbook -->
The split has a mechanical form once you write runbooks at scale. Type every step as one of
three kinds, and let the type decide how the step is governed. A deterministic step is
*runnable*: it carries the exact tool line to run. The judgment steps divide by whether an
agent can take them. A *judgment-automatable* step carries a pre-canned brief the orchestrator
dispatches to a strong model, the missing middle between "run this" and "you decide." A
*judgment-irreducible* step carries the words to surface to the person, because the call must
stay theirs. [ref:runbook-step-kinds] gives the three kinds and what each must carry. Typing
the step makes the shape checkable: a tool line on a judgment step, or a dispatched brief on a
runnable one, is a contradiction a linter catches before the runbook ships. The failure map
from the last section joins the same way, each symptom routed to its runbook by its class
rather than its exact error text, so a fresh failure that rhymes with a known one still finds
the way out.

| Step kind | What it is | What it must carry | How it is governed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runnable | A deterministic step with one correct outcome | The exact tool line to run | The tool runs it; its logged trace is what a second checker reads |
| Judgment-automatable | Reasoning no script can do, but a strong-model dispatch can — the missing middle | A pre-canned brief the orchestrator dispatches | A brief-linter checks the brief is intact before launch |
| Judgment-irreducible | Reasoning that must stay with the person | The words to surface, and why | Surfaced to the operator, never dispatched |

The split is no DocAble idiosyncrasy. Systems that share no code and answer to different regulators
reach for the same fence between the step a machine can decide and the step that needs a mind.

<!-- worked-examples: determinization -->
### Example — Siemens
Siemens draws the same fence as a clean division of labor across a safety-critical toolchain:
probabilistic semantic reasoning upstream, deterministic physics and verification downstream. The
decidable obligation — does the design satisfy its specified constraints — lands on a deterministic
mechanism, while the open-ended reading of intent stays with the model. The division is the design, not
an afterthought bolted on once the model proved unreliable.

### Example — Zenseact
Zenseact hardened three judgments that other sites leave probabilistic. Execution runs through
deterministic tools, authority is settled by an explicit permission table rather than the agent's
discretion, and context selection is decided by a TF-IDF router instead of a guess. Each was a step
that could have been improvised and was instead pinned to a repeatable mechanism, so the same input
takes the same path every run.

### Example — Docker {counter-example}
Docker is where the pattern fits awkwardly, and the awkwardness teaches the boundary. Its agents
determinize what carries hard evidence — the tests run as deterministic gates — but the admission
decision is kept deliberately probabilistic, an independent reviewer plus a human, because collapsing
that call to a threshold would forfeit the very judgment it exists to weigh. Docker does not
disconfirm the rule; it determinizes the tests and *declines* to determinize admission, marking by
choice the line the discipline itself draws between the two kinds of step.

### Example — DocAble
DocAble's accessibility pipeline is built on the split. The structural work — reading a document's tag
tree, checking reading order, rewriting the parts that have one correct shape — runs as deterministic
tools that leave a replayable trace. The judgment-laden work — describing an image, naming what a
figure is *for* — routes to a model against a rubric, because no script can write the alt text. Each
step is governed by its kind, not run as one undifferentiated remediation pass.

<!-- takeaway -->
The shared move is *determinization*: separate the steps with a single correct outcome — which earn an
executable tool and a replayable trace — from the steps that need judgment, which earn the roughest
algorithm that still fits, and govern each by its kind.
<!-- worked-examples-end -->

<!-- point: operating-judgment-becomes-something-the-environment-holds | Written down, operating judgment becomes something the environment holds. | terms: lifecycle, runbook, deterministic-vs-judgment-split -->
Step back and the chapter is one instruction wearing three shapes: a lifecycle writes down the healthy
path and the way out of each failure, a runbook rides on top and splits every step by its kind, and the
pre-canned brief hands that split to the next agent intact. What used to live only in your head — how
the work is done here, and what must never be done — becomes something the environment holds and can
enforce. Delegate the operation, not just the code, and it stops depending on who happens to be awake
at 2 a.m.

One thing the runbook still needs, the lifecycle cannot supply: something to steer by. Every loop it
drives is blind without an honest reading of its own state — a metric, which the next chapter develops.
