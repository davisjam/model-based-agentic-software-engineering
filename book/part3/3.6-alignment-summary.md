# Summary

Modeling makes consequential properties and intended obligations explicit; Alignment determines how selected obligations will be upheld as agents work. An implementation can agree with a model without satisfying the intended obligation, and a correct finding need not control what happens next. Engineers must establish what should hold, choose trustworthy mechanisms, and place them where the necessary evidence is available. They must also revise those mechanisms as the product and the engineering environment change.

1.  **Distinguish guidance, evidence, and enforcement.** Guidance tells an agent how it should act. Correspondence establishes agreement over selected relationships; conformance concerns an independent obligation. Acceptance decides whether work may proceed. Evidence for one does not establish the others.

2.  **Choose mechanisms suited to the obligation.** Constraints restrict available actions or states, sensors produce evidence, and gates evaluate conditions and enforce decisions. Evaluate the machinery itself, and preserve acceptable implementation choices unless their restriction has an engineering justification.

3.  **Enforce at the earliest decidable boundary.** An action can be refused as soon as the relevant facts are available. An obligation spanning several changes or concerning integrated behavior may need evidence that appears later. Placement and enforcement strength are separate decisions.

4.  **Convert recurring judgment into durable structure.** When a failure or repeated decision reveals something future work should preserve, record it in a model, check, or control. That structure becomes engineering capital when subsequent work benefits from it.

5.  **Govern the governance machinery.** Controls can leave an obligation uncovered, interfere with one another, or become too costly to maintain. Engineers need to know which controls exist, how they interact, and which obligations they govern.

> ### Alignment Principle {#alignment-principle}
>
> Make selected engineering obligations enforceable through constraints that restrict actions or states, sensors that produce evidence, and gates that evaluate evidence and control whether work may proceed.

Alignment makes selected obligations dependable without prescribing every choice involved in satisfying them. Agents can investigate, design, and implement within the freedom those obligations preserve; engineers remain responsible for the resulting system. They decide what must hold and whether the available evidence justifies acceptance. Mechanization is valuable where it reduces repeated judgment or prevents known failures. Beyond those boundaries, reasoning remains necessary.
