# The Four Mechanism Roles

<!-- point: four-roles-separate-prevention-observation-judgment-admission | Four roles — constrain, sense, validate, gate — separate prevention, observation, judgment, admission. | terms: governance-mechanism, constraint, sensor, validator, gate -->
MAGE uses four roles to describe how environmental authority operates. A mechanism may **constrain** an
action, **sense** the state or result of work, **validate** the resulting evidence against an obligation,
or **gate** whether the work may advance. One artifact often plays several at once; the four stay useful
because they separate the distinct moves — prevention, observation, judgment, admission — that a single
tool blurs together.

<!-- index-def: governance-mechanism -->
> **Governance mechanism.** A repeatable structure in the engineering environment that carries an
> engineering policy by constraining work, producing relevant evidence, evaluating that evidence against
> an obligation, or controlling admission.

The four roles are:

- **Constraint** — removes or narrows an action or state the system is allowed to express.
- **Sensor** — observes the work or system and produces evidence.
- **Validator** — evaluates evidence against an obligation.
- **Gate** — controls whether work may cross a boundary.

Implementations often bundle several roles. The vocabulary names the functions inside the bundle:
prevention, observation, judgment, and admission.

<!-- point: two-roles-act-on-the-work-two-act-at-the-door | Two roles act on the work; two act at the door it must pass. | terms: constraint, sensor, validator, gate -->
The four split into two pairs. A **constraint** and a **sensor** act on the work as the agent does it:
one prevents the wrong move, the other observes what happened after it. A **validator** and a **gate**
act at the door the work must pass: one weighs the evidence a change carries, the other decides whether
it may advance.

## Prevention and observation

<!-- section-terms: constraints-and-sensors, constraint, sensor -->

<!-- point: a-mechanism-is-one-of-two-moves-prevent-or-observe | A mechanism is one of two moves: prevent, or observe. | terms: constraint, sensor, constraints-and-sensors -->
<!-- index-def: constraints-and-sensors -->
Inside the loop a mechanism has one of two moves — prevent the mistake, or observe it — and
[ref:constraint-vs-sensor] draws the split. Constraint and sensor are the complementary pair: the
constraint works before the fact and the sensor after, and a mature environment carries both, one for
each failure it can prevent and each it can only watch for.

<!-- label: constraint-vs-sensor -->
<!-- figure: assets/constraint-vs-sensor.svg | *Constraint or Sensor.* A constraint narrows the action space before a mistake occurs; a sensor observes the result and produces evidence afterward. Prevention avoids the failed iteration; observation requires later interpretation and response. Prefer a constraint where the bad state can be scoped away; otherwise sense the drift. -->

<!-- point: a-soft-instruction-becomes-alignment-only-when-the-environment-can-act-on-it | A soft instruction becomes Alignment only when the environment can act on it. | terms: constraint -->
A soft instruction becomes Alignment only when the environment can act on it. If a brief must contain a
required section, check for that section before launch and refuse the launch when it is absent.

<!-- point: a-sensor-observes-and-produces-evidence-and-costs-iterations | A sensor observes and produces evidence — actual = observe(system); it does not judge, and acting on it costs an iteration. | terms: sensor -->
<!-- index-def: sensor -->
<!-- index-example: observability -->
<!-- gloss-only: Sensor | A governance mechanism that observes the work or system and produces evidence — actual = observe(system): a log, a trace, a metric emitter, a scan that reads what the code actually did. It produces evidence; it does not judge it, and it does not by itself determine that a violation occurred — that is the validator's job. Acting on the evidence fails the loop iteration, so the agent must run again; a sensor costs iterations. -->
**A sensor observes and produces evidence.** It lets the move happen and reads the evidence back out
after the fact — `actual = observe(system)`. A raw sensor only *produces* that evidence: a log line, a
trace, a metric emitter, a scan that reads the actual calls out of the code. It does not decide whether
what it saw is good or bad — that judgment belongs to the validator. Because the evidence arrives after
the move, acting on it costs an iteration — the agent runs again to fix what the sensor surfaced — and
that cost is the reason you do not reach for one first.

<!-- point: a-ci-suite-bundles-three-roles-without-collapsing-the-distinction | A CI suite bundles sensor, validator, and gate without collapsing the distinction among them. | terms: sensor, validator, gate, governance-package -->
A CI test suite often bundles three roles: executing the code produces evidence, checking the result
validates it, and failing the build gates progress. One artifact can perform several roles without
collapsing the distinction among them.

<!-- point: a-sensor-is-only-as-good-as-its-observability | A sensor is only as good as its observability — the semantic gap from the other side. | terms: sensor, observability -->
<!-- index-def: observability -->
A sensor is only as good as the signal it can read. **Observability** is the property that a running
system exposes enough signal to explain what it did and surface what went wrong. It is the wiring under
every sensor: the event a gate fires on, the log a checker reads, the trace that turns "the build
failed" into "the build failed *here, for this reason.*" If the evidence an obligation needs does not
exist, no downstream validator recovers it by authority alone. When it does not, the environment must
first instrument the relevant state or event.

<!-- point: a-constraint-prevents-drift-within-the-iteration-and-costs-no-iteration | A constraint prevents drift within the iteration, costing none. | terms: constraint, typed-enum -->
<!-- index-def: constraint -->
<!-- gloss-only: Constraint | A governance mechanism that prevents drift by scoping the agent's action space so the wrong move is unavailable within a loop iteration (a typed enum instead of a string); the prevented mistake costs no failed iteration. -->
**A constraint prevents drift.** Where a sensor only produces evidence of the mistake after the fact, a
constraint makes the whole class impossible: it scopes the agent's action space *within* the iteration,
so the wrong move is never available to pick. Closed representations are the cleanest example. A
lifecycle encoded as arbitrary strings admits values the design never intended, and a mismatch surfaces
only an iteration later, when the test suite crashes. Encode it as a closed enumeration instead and the
compiler rejects any value outside the declared set on the spot. The enum does not watch for the illegal
state later — it removes that representation from well-typed code, and no iteration is spent catching it.
This is why MAGE prefers closed representations where practical.

Programming-language theory made this move systematic: choose a representation or type discipline in which some invalid programs cannot be written as well-typed terms.[note: Benjamin C. Pierce characterizes type systems as syntactic methods for classifying program phrases so that classes of erroneous behaviors can be ruled out automatically [cite: pierce2002].] MAGE generalizes the engineering preference rather than the formalism: where practical, restrict the action or representation space so the bad state cannot be produced, instead of detecting it again on every later iteration.

<!-- point: constraint-and-sensor-echo-control-engineering | Constraint and sensor echo control engineering — analogy, not identity. | terms: constraint, sensor, constraints-and-sensors -->

> *A footnote on control engineering.* The language deliberately echoes control engineering: constrain
> the plant's admissible behavior, observe its state through sensors, compare the evidence against a
> desired condition, and act on the result. The inheritance is an analogy, not an identity — a fleet of
> probabilistic machines is not a linear control system, and MAGE does not claim it is one. What carries
> over is the closed-loop posture: constrain admissible behavior, observe state, compare evidence against
> a desired condition, and act on the result. Leveson's systems-theoretic safety work provides a nearby
> engineering precedent [cite: leveson2011].

<!-- point: human-process-instantiates-the-same-roles-logging-senses-reviewer-validates-approver-gates | Human process instantiates the same roles: logging senses, the reviewer validates, the approver gates. | terms: sensor, validator, gate, constraint -->
Human process can instantiate the same roles: logging senses, review validates, and approval gates. The
opportunity is not to eliminate human judgment, but to recognize repeated judgments that can be replaced
by durable structure.

<!-- point: the-constraint-is-poka-yoke-jidoka-is-a-sensor-validator-gate-bundle | The constraint is poka-yoke; jidoka is closer to a sensor/validator/gate bundle. | terms: constraint, sensor -->

> *A footnote on the factory floor.* [The MAGE Workflow](4.1-the-mage-workflow.html) gives Toyota's
> neighbouring names for these moves. *Poka-yoke* is close to the constraint: design the process so a
> mistake is difficult or impossible to make. *Jidoka* pairs detecting an abnormal condition with
> stopping the line — closer to a sensor, a validator, and a gate acting together than to a sensor
> alone.

<!-- point: ownership-is-the-purest-constraint-and-sensor-pair | Ownership shows the pair at its sharpest: the compare-and-set is a pure constraint, the stale-sweep a pure sensor. | terms: constraint, sensor, constraints-and-sensors -->
The sharpest instance of the pair is ownership of a unit of work. A **lease** makes one live claim
visible to peers, and an atomic **compare-and-set** on the claim decides which worker may perform the
side effect: competing claimants cannot both acquire the same permission, so at most one performs it.
That is a pure constraint — the wrong move is unavailable, and no iteration is spent on a collision that
cannot occur. Its paired **sensor** handles the one failure the constraint cannot prevent: an owner that
dies mid-work. A stale-lease sweep scans the in-flight leases and **produces evidence** that an owner has
been abandoned and recovery is required, so a lease that will never be acknowledged is surfaced and
routed to failure rather than left to strand the job. Keep recovery and permission separate: the lease
lets the work become available again; the atomic claim stops two owners from acquiring the authority to
run it at once.

## Validators and gates

<!-- section-terms: validator, gate, governance-mechanism -->

<!-- point: a-validator-turns-evidence-into-a-judgment | A validator turns evidence into a judgment. | terms: validator, gate -->
<!-- index-def: validator -->
<!-- gloss-only: Validator | A governance mechanism that evaluates the evidence a change carries against the obligation it must meet — a provenance stamp, a re-derived test result, a completion re-checked rather than self-reported. It turns evidence into a judgment; its authority depends on what the environment does with the result. -->
The other two roles act at the loop's closing door. A **validator** turns evidence into a judgment: does
the observed call graph contain only permitted edges, did this journey establish its post-condition,
does the provenance record account for every mutation? It reads the evidence a sensor produced — or
derives it straight from the artifact — and asks whether it satisfies the obligation. A validator may be
deterministic, probabilistic, or human; its authority comes not from itself but from what the
environment does with its verdict.

<!-- point: a-gate-supplies-the-consequence-and-a-gate-is-a-commitment | A gate supplies the consequence — and a gate is a commitment. | terms: gate, validator -->
<!-- index-def: gate -->
A **gate** supplies that consequence. It decides whether the work may cross a boundary: commit, merge,
deploy, execute, close, or draw on a scarce resource. Validation and gating are separate design
decisions. A cost ceiling can be measured and evaluated without blocking production; a security
invariant may deserve immediate refusal. **A gate is a commitment.** Give an uncertain validator blocking
authority and you convert its uncertainty into outages.

<!-- point: cheap-evidence-early-full-re-derivation-late-the-thing-asking-to-pass-does-not-define-passing | Cheap evidence early, full re-derivation late; the thing asking to pass does not define passing. | terms: validator, gate -->
The strongest loops do not trust the producing agent's self-report when the evidence can be re-derived.
Cheap checks run early for fast feedback and bind their result to an exact tree; a later, more expensive
boundary re-derives the evidence admission needs rather than reading a marker. The definition of done
recomputes the full evidence at close instead of trusting a report. The principle is simple: the thing
asking to pass does not get to define what counts as having passed.

## The four roles, across the models

<!-- section-terms: constraint, sensor, validator, gate -->

<!-- point: the-service-flow-model-runs-the-complete-four-role-loop | The service-flow model runs the complete loop: a typed seam constrains, a scan senses, a diff validates, a gate blocks. | terms: constraint, sensor, validator, gate -->
The service-flow model shows the complete loop. The model states which services may communicate; a typed
seam constrains permitted calls, a scan observes actual calls, a diff validates them against declared
wires, and a gate blocks an undeclared edge before it ships.

Other models emphasize different subsets of the four roles. [ref:role-taxonomy-matrix] shows the
variation.

## What counts as a mechanism

<!-- section-terms: governance-mechanism, constraints-and-sensors -->

<!-- point: keep-policy-guidance-and-governance-mechanism-separate | Keep policy, guidance, and governance mechanism separate; authority begins when something acts. | terms: governance-mechanism, model-as-map -->
Keep **policy**, **guidance**, and **governance mechanism** separate. A model file can state policy; a
playbook can guide an agent; neither becomes an authoritative mechanism merely by existing or by
appearing in an agent's context. Authority begins when something in the environment actually constrains
the work, produces evidence, evaluates that evidence, or controls admission. The model supplies the
policy; the consuming machinery supplies the holding.

<!-- point: the-same-artifact-can-play-several-roles | The same artifact can play several roles; the model is the representation, the machinery the authority. | terms: governance-mechanism, model-as-map -->
The same artifact can participate several ways. A structured model injected into context guides
reasoning. A validator reading that same model uses it as the obligation against which observed code is
judged. A generated permission table turns the same modeled relation into a constraint. The model is the
representation; the consuming machinery supplies the authority.

<!-- point: implementations-bundle-roles-name-them-do-not-stretch-the-terms | Implementations bundle roles; name them, don't stretch the terms. | terms: governance-package, constraints-and-sensors -->
<!-- gloss-only: Governance mechanism | A repeatable structure in the engineering environment that carries an engineering policy by constraining work, producing evidence, evaluating that evidence against an obligation, or controlling admission — four roles: constraint, sensor, validator, gate. The umbrella that replaces the ambiguous word "control"; a model joins governance only when something reads or enforces it. -->
<!-- index-def: governance-package -->
<!-- gloss-only: Governance package | A mechanism that bundles several roles — a structured model that aims the agent shipped with the lints and drift-parity gates that hold what the model only aims at. Name the roles inside the bundle rather than stretching one term to cover them all. -->
Implementations routinely bundle roles. A test runner may sense, validate, and gate; a narrow interface
may constrain while emitting provenance. Name the functions inside the bundle rather than stretching
every influence into a "soft constraint" or "soft sensor."

<!-- point: represented-intent-becomes-authoritative-through-one-of-four-roles | Leave-with: intent becomes authority through one of four roles; next, where the mechanisms come from. | terms: governance-mechanism, thesis-alignment -->
Represented intent becomes authoritative when a mechanism constrains an action, produces the evidence of
a violation, evaluates that evidence, or controls admission. Those four roles are the vocabulary the
rest of this Part builds on. With them defined, one question remains: where do the obligations and the
mechanisms that hold them come from? Some can be designed before the first autonomous change. Others are
visible only after the environment fails — including modeled properties, like the cost ceiling just
named, that are sensed but not yet governed. That is the conversion problem, and it is the next chapter.
