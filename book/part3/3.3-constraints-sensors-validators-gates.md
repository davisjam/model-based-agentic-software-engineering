# Constraints, Sensors, Validators, and Gates

<!-- point: four-roles-are-the-taxonomy-not-another-ontology | Four roles are the taxonomy: constrain, sense, validate, gate. | terms: governance-mechanism, constraints-and-sensors -->
The Alignment Thesis names four verbs, and they are the whole taxonomy of this Part. A mechanism
**constrains** an action, **senses** a violation, **validates** evidence, or **gates** admission. Every
guardrail a governed environment carries plays one of those four roles. Resist the pull to invent a
fifth. The value of a small, closed set is that a reader can place any new mechanism in it on sight,
and a term that stretches to cover everything governs nothing. Bound the word first:

<!-- index-def: governance-mechanism -->
> **Governance mechanism.** A repeatable environmental structure that encodes an engineering policy
> and either constrains an action, senses a violation, requires evidence, or controls admission.

<!-- point: two-roles-act-on-the-work-two-act-at-the-door | Two roles act on the work; two act at the door it must pass. | terms: constraint, sensor, validator, gate -->
The four split into two pairs. A **constraint** and a **sensor** act on the work as the agent does it:
one prevents the wrong move, the other catches it after the fact. A **validator** and a **gate** act at
the door the work must pass: one weighs the evidence a change carries, the other decides whether it may
advance. Most of the craft lives in the first pair, so start there.

## Constraints and sensors

<!-- section-terms: constraints-and-sensors, constraint, sensor -->

<!-- point: a-mechanism-is-one-of-two-moves-prevent-or-catch | A mechanism is one of two moves: prevent, or catch. | terms: constraint, sensor, constraints-and-sensors -->
<!-- index-def: constraints-and-sensors -->
Inside the loop a mechanism has one of two moves — prevent the mistake, or catch it — and
[ref:constraint-vs-sensor] draws the split. Constraint and sensor are the complementary pair: the
constraint works before the fact and the sensor after, and a mature environment carries both, one for
each failure it can prevent and each it can only detect.

<!-- label: constraint-vs-sensor -->
<!-- figure: assets/constraint-vs-sensor.svg | *Constraint or Sensor.* The two moves and their asymmetric cost. A constraint prevents the mistake inside the iteration, so it costs no failed loop; a sensor catches it after the fact, so it costs at least one. Prefer the constraint where you can build one; add a sensor for the drift you cannot scope away. -->

<!-- point: words-alone-are-a-probability-flip-so-determinize-them-into-a-gate | Words are a probability flip; determinize them into a check. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance, constraint -->
First, the argument for reaching for a mechanism at all. Words alone do not hold. Tell the agent "make
sure the briefs always follow this template" and it will say, *"Sure, boss"* — and then not reliably do
it, because it is a probability flip. A prompt raises the odds; it does not settle them. But you can
*determinize that*: before the agent launches, confirm every required part of the template is present,
and refuse the launch when it is not. The soft instruction becomes a hard check. That move — from a
probability you nudge to a condition you enforce — splits into the two forms below.

<!-- point: a-sensor-detects-drift-after-the-fact-and-costs-iterations | A sensor detects drift after the fact and costs iterations. | terms: sensor -->
<!-- index-def: sensor -->
<!-- index-example: observability -->
<!-- gloss-only: Sensor | A governance mechanism that detects drift after the fact and fails the loop iteration, so the agent must run again to fix what it caught (a lint, a test, a gate); it costs iterations. -->
**A sensor detects drift.** It lets the mistake happen and catches it after the fact, and because it
fires after, it fails the loop iteration: the agent runs again to fix what the sensor caught. Your test
suite in CI is a sensor, and so are your lints. It would be better if the model never wrote the bug; but
if it does, the sensor catches it before the code ships. The cost of a sensor is iterations — detect,
fail, re-iterate — and that cost is the reason you do not reach for one first.

<!-- point: a-sensor-is-only-as-good-as-its-observability | A sensor is only as good as its observability. | terms: sensor, observability -->
<!-- index-def: observability -->
A sensor is only as good as the signal it can read. **Observability** is the property that a running
system exposes enough signal to explain what it did and catch what went wrong. It is the wiring under
every sensor: the event a gate fires on, the log a checker reads, the trace that turns "the build
failed" into "the build failed *here, for this reason.*" Build the observability first and the sensor
has something to watch; skip it and it stares at a blank wall.

<!-- point: a-constraint-prevents-drift-within-the-iteration-and-costs-no-iteration | A constraint prevents drift within the iteration, costing none. | terms: constraint, typed-enum -->
<!-- index-def: constraint -->
<!-- gloss-only: Constraint | A governance mechanism that prevents drift by scoping the agent's action space so the wrong move is unavailable within a loop iteration (a typed enum instead of a string); the prevented mistake costs no failed iteration. -->
**A constraint prevents drift.** Where a sensor detects an instance of the mistake after the fact, a
constraint makes the whole class impossible: it scopes the agent's action space *within* the iteration,
so the wrong move is never available to pick. The cleanest example is types. Suppose the agent
represents a state with bare strings. Being a language model underneath, it will cheerfully reach for a
synonym next time, and the synonym silently fails to match — you learn that an iteration later, when the
test suite crashes. Tell it to use an enumeration instead, and the failure mode evaporates: it can no
longer invent an unlisted value, and if it tries, the compiler rejects it on the spot. The enum forbids
the bug rather than watching for it, and no iteration is spent catching it — which is why the catalogue
leans so hard on structured models and closed enumerations over free-form strings.

> Learn more about this governance mechanism: [closed enumerations over free-form strings](appendix-c-typed-categories.html).

<!-- point: constraint-and-sensor-are-control-engineerings-working-terms | Constraint and sensor come from control engineering. | terms: constraint, sensor, constraints-and-sensors -->

> *A footnote on control engineering.* Constraint and sensor are the working terms of control
> engineering. A governed plant — a reactor, an autopilot — carries constraints on the inputs and
> states it may reach and sensors on the outputs it produces, so the controller reads the error and
> corrects the next cycle. A fleet of probabilistic machines is a plant you cannot run open-loop; the
> governed environment is the loop you close around it. Engineers have run unruly physical processes
> this way for a century.
>
> Learn more: Nancy Leveson's *Engineering a Safer World* [cite: leveson2011] applies the same systems view to safety engineering.

> *A footnote on closing the loop in the wild.* Ericsson's EIAP closed loop pairs intent-based
> operation over curated, real-time network state with a controlled actuation surface, and decouples
> the model lifecycle from the application lifecycle: closed-loop autonomy needs both a state
> representation and a bounded actuation surface, or it is not operational autonomy [cite:ericsson2026closedloop].

<!-- point: a-purely-organizational-answer-is-all-sensors-and-never-removes-a-failure-class | A purely organizational answer is all sensors, removing no class. | terms: sensor, constraint -->
Seen from the outside, this is what a purely organizational answer leaves on the table. Faced with an
unreliable worker, the instinct is to wrap it in process: a review, an approval, an evidence trail, a
sign-off. Every one of those is a sensor — each lets the mistake happen and inspects the output
afterward. A discipline built only of sensors can raise the odds and shrink the blast radius, yet it
never removes a failure class, because it watches the action space instead of scoping it. The
constraint is the move that answer does not have: it works ex-ante, inside the iteration, so the wrong
move is never available to pick — reliability by construction, not inspection after the fact.

<!-- point: the-constraint-is-poka-yoke-the-sensors-stop-is-jidoka | The constraint is poka-yoke; the sensor's stop is jidoka. | terms: constraint, sensor -->

> *A footnote on the factory floor.* [The MAGE Workflow](4.1-the-mage-workflow.html) gives
> Toyota's names for these two moves: the constraint is *poka-yoke*, and the sensor's
> fail-the-iteration reflex is *jidoka*. The lesson Toyota drew is the one this section just argued —
> inspection at the end of the line cannot build quality in. A fleet of agents is a production line
> for code, and it relearns the same economics.

## Validators and gates

<!-- section-terms: validator, gate, governance-mechanism -->

<!-- point: a-validator-weighs-evidence-a-gate-decides-admission | A validator weighs evidence; a gate decides admission. | terms: validator, gate -->
<!-- index-def: validator -->
<!-- index-def: gate -->
<!-- gloss-only: Validator | A governance mechanism that evaluates the evidence a change carries against the obligation it must meet — a provenance stamp, a re-derived test result, a completion re-checked rather than self-reported. -->
The other two roles act at the loop's closing door. A **validator** evaluates the evidence a change
carries against the obligation it must meet: a provenance stamp, a re-derived test result, a completion
re-checked rather than self-reported. A **gate** decides whether the work may advance at all — the
admission rule of the engineered loop, holding the last door. Both are built out of constraints and
sensors underneath; they earn their own names from *where* they sit, at the point where the work asks
to pass.

<!-- point: cheap-evidence-early-full-re-derivation-late-never-a-trusted-report | Cheap evidence early, full re-derivation late, never a trusted report. | terms: validator, gate -->
The two compose into how a loop earns the right to advance. Cheap checks run early and bind their
result to an exact tree, so a later, more expensive stage *checks that binding* rather than *trusts* a
report. The definition of done recomputes the full evidence at close instead of reading a marker. Cheap
evidence early, full re-derivation late, never a trusted self-report.

## What counts as a mechanism

<!-- section-terms: governance-mechanism, constraints-and-sensors -->

<!-- point: a-model-is-not-a-mechanism-until-something-enforces-it | A model joins governance only when something enforces it. | terms: governance-mechanism, model-as-map -->
<!-- gloss-only: Governance mechanism | A repeatable environmental structure that encodes an engineering policy and either constrains an action, senses a violation, requires evidence, or controls admission — four roles: constraint, sensor, validator, gate. The umbrella that replaces the ambiguous word "control"; a model joins governance only when something reads or enforces it. -->
The bounded definition settles a boundary this book leans on. A model file is policy. **It becomes a
mechanism only when some agent, tool, or gate consumes it.** Injected into an agent's context, it
constrains the reasoning; read by a lint or a gate, it senses or admits. So a structured model can aim
the agent and still sit inert on disk, because "aims the agent" already names a consuming step the file
alone does not perform. The model supplies the policy; the consumption supplies the holding.

<!-- point: soft-mechanisms-influence-hard-mechanisms-hold | Soft mechanisms influence; hard mechanisms deterministically hold. | terms: soft-vs-hard-governance -->
One secondary distinction runs under all four roles, and it stays subordinate to them: **soft
mechanisms influence; hard mechanisms deterministically hold.** A soft constraint aims the agent's
reasoning; a hard one the compiler enforces. A soft sensor reminds; a hard one blocks. The role names
*what* the mechanism does; soft-versus-hard names *how firmly*.

<!-- point: most-mechanisms-bundle-a-constraint-with-a-sensor | Most mechanisms bundle a constraint with a sensor. | terms: governance-package, constraints-and-sensors -->
<!-- index-def: governance-package -->
<!-- gloss-only: Governance package | A mechanism that bundles several roles: a soft constraint (a structured model that aims the agent) shipped with hard sensors (the lints and drift-parity gates that catch what the constraint only aims at). -->
Few real mechanisms are pure. Most **bundle** more than one role — a soft constraint that aims the
agent shipped with the hard sensors that catch what it only aims at, a structured model carried with
its lints and drift-parity gates, a definition-of-done runbook wrapped in the checks that flag a
drifted step. Name the bundle, then tag its primary move: prefer a constraint where you can build one,
and add a sensor for the drift you cannot scope away.

<!-- point: represented-intent-becomes-authoritative-through-one-of-four-roles | Leave-with: intent becomes authority through one of four roles. | terms: governance-mechanism, thesis-alignment -->
Represented intent becomes authoritative when a mechanism constrains action, senses state, validates
evidence, or controls admission. Those four roles are the vocabulary the rest of this Part builds on.
The next chapter answers the question they leave open: where do the mechanisms that fill them come from?
