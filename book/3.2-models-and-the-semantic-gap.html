Part 3 — Alignment  ::  Chapter 20

3.2 Models and the Semantic Gap

The last chapter gave you four places to inject a mechanism. This one is about the most valuable thing you can put there — a model — and about a trap that catches people the moment they try to enforce one: pushing on the model at the wrong level, and watching it slip. A model is the sweet spot between prose too vague to bind and code too verbose to reason over. But a model only helps if you check it where checking makes sense. A trustworthy environment needs one description of intent that cannot silently lie — so the question sharpens from where to what, and to how you keep that what from rotting.

This chapter is the turn the whole Part rides on. It shows the model at its strongest — the Modeling Thesis at full strength — precisely so the next move can be made: wiring that model to a gate, so it stops advising and starts binding. On the capability ladder that is the step from rung 4 to rung 5: a model that says what shape the system should have becomes a model that acquires a checker, and stated intent stops being implicit. This is the first step of represented intent becomes authority — a model by itself has none, and this is where it gets one.

3.2.1 Words are soft, code is verbose, a model is the sweet spot

Your first governance mechanism is the prompt, because the agent is a language model and words are how you talk to it. But words are soft. They are ambiguous, and the more ambiguity you leave in, the less happy you will be. You can push the ambiguity down by writing documentation — describing the environment, the product, its goals, its policies — and if you were briefing a human team, that would be enough. A human team supplements the documentation with shared memory, social correction, accumulated context, and the plain fact that a person who ignores the rules gets fired; a loose document is enough because the team fills its gaps from all of that. A newly invoked agent begins without those supports unless the environment reconstructs them. The distinction is not human discipline against machine irresponsibility — humans forget rules and violate policies too. It is persistent organizational context against cold-started task context.

Code is the opposite failure. Code is operationally exact — it is exactly what will run — but it is semantically expensive to reason over in bulk, and it can still be ambiguous about what the behavior was meant to be. What you want sits between the two: something accurate and unambiguous, yet compact. The word for that thing is a model.

You already know models from physics. Think of F = ma, or y = mx + b. Neither is reality — F does not quite equal ma, and real data scatters around the line — but each is a cheap, honest approximation you can actually reason over, instead of wrestling the full empirical dataset or a nightmare of fluid equations. A circle approximated by tangents is not a circle, but it is good enough to compute with. Software has its own such models. UML was the famous attempt: a whole language of them, out of the 1990s, expected to be the future. It never became the universal development substrate its advocates expected, and why it did not is where this chapter goes next.

3.2.2 Why the models drifted — and why that changes now

The models are the map; the code is the territory. You draw the map, you build the territory from it — and then the territory drifts. Code changes constantly, and the more it changes, the more it costs to keep the map true. Worse, no single model captures everything, so teams either piled every concern into one unreadable mega-model, or split their concerns across so many views that a single code change meant updating ten diagrams. It is a big pain. So people gave up and drew box diagrams instead — which is what they still do 11. Sebastian Baltes and Stephan Diehl, “Sketches and Diagrams in Practice,” in “Proceedings of the 22nd ACM SIGSOFT International Symposium on Foundations of Software Engineering (FSE 2014),” special issue, Proceedings of the 22nd ACM SIGSOFT International Symposium on Foundations of Software Engineering (FSE 2014), 2014, 530–41, https://doi.org/10.1145/2635868.2635891..

Keeping the map equal to the territory is an old research program, not a new worry. The correspondence was always achievable; what never got cheap was holding it reliably while both sides moved — the same daily-labor cost that pushed everyone outside the regulated shops back onto the code.

That cost is what agents change, and this book measures the change rather than asserting it: the model-synchronization measurement in Part 2 asks whether an agent fleet can carry the recurring reconciliation labor a person used to carry by hand. The claim is economic, not historical — agents did not invent synchronization; they made a maintenance regime feasible that the Agile era could not afford. Synchronization cost is reduced and redistributed, not zero: what does not move to the fleet is deciding what the correspondence should mean. The rest of this section is that regime made operational — agents reasoning over the models, and a drift gate that turns a disagreement into a failed build.

Anyone who has worked in a shop that tried to keep models by hand knows the failure in its bones. A new hire opens a diagram and cannot tell whether it is describing the system or lying about it — whether the box that is missing means the code lost a component or the diagram lost a maintainer. The model was drawn years ago by someone who has since left, and nobody updated it, because updating it was always the task that could wait until after the release. The map and the territory drifted apart quietly, and the first person to trust the map paid for the gap. That lived failure — a hand-maintained model that rots the moment the person maintaining it stops — is why the practice fell out of favor. Only the industries where a mistake is catastrophic — Rolls-Royce, Boeing, who know just how bad it is to get it wrong — kept their models honest, because they could justify the cost. Everyone else chose agility and leaned on the code.

Agents flip the calculation. An agent can reason over local code, but not over a large system's raw implementation as one coherent object — there is too much of it to hold at once — whereas it can hold a model. So if you can find the models that usefully approximate your system and keep them in sync, your agents will always know where to go. They walk the docs, hit a pointer to the model — a data-flow diagram, a performance model, an inheritance hierarchy, a user journey — and if that model is linked to the relevant code, they can make the change with the whole picture held in a single window.

Which model the agent reaches for shifts with the move: the architecture to plan a feature, the module the model points at to write it, the user journey to ask whether the change still serves the product. No one sweet-spot model gives you that — a set of honest, linked models does, each the right map for a different moment. The Model Zoo in Part 2 is where that set, and the discipline of moving among its altitudes, gets its rules.

The model also lets the agent check its work: a test says the suite passed, but an invariant over a model says what must be true beyond the tests. This is how you stop architecture from decaying — the agent finds the architectural model, follows it, verifies against it, and when the territory genuinely moves, retrofits the map so the two stay equal. The companion catalogue's drift-and-parity gates are the mechanism that makes "the map equals the territory" a checked property rather than a hope: they fail the build the moment a model and the code it describes disagree.

Learn more about this governance mechanism: drift-and-parity gates.

Before the theory of why, note the plainer payoff the practice kept producing: modeling a subsystem surfaced real defects in it, not just a cleaner picture of it. Across a run of efforts that each built a structured model for a previously un-modeled part of the system, the act of writing the model — naming the states, the invariants, the seams — forced the kind of reading that finds bugs. It found a command-line seam whose exit codes were mislabeled, a resource leak on a cleanup path, an editing session that could lose an update because two writers were never fenced apart, and a queue-depth counter that drifted because it only ever moved one way. These were not style notes. They were live defects the tests had not caught, made visible because someone had to state precisely what the subsystem was supposed to do before they could model it.

Two honesties keep the claim the right size. Half of the defects were low-severity — the count is four real bugs, but two of them were minor — so read this as "modeling reliably surfaces something actionable," not "modeling finds a catastrophe every time." And the efforts that turned up no bug were not failures: each still produced an architectural strengthening, because modeling a clean subsystem still forces you to write down what "clean" meant. The mechanism at work is not luck. It is that you cannot author a spec — as opposed to mirroring the code — without deciding what the code ought to do, and that decision is exactly the reading under which a wrong "is" finally shows.

Sidebar — why agents don't reach for models on their own (a conjecture). Ask a coding agent for a change and it dives at the code; it never proposes a model. A conjecture, flagged as one: the public corpus an agent trained on is overwhelmingly the agile kind that gave up on models and leaned on the code, and what text there is about models is mostly negative — the obituaries for UML, the war stories about diagrams that rotted. So the agent is conditioned away twice: starved of positive examples, fed explicit priors that the approach is a mistake. If that is right, the reluctance is not evidence that models fail to help. It is an echo of the era that abandoned them — over the very cost, a human keeping the map in sync, that agents just made cheap.

3.2.3 A structured model layer binds the islands

Step back from drift for a moment and look at what you actually hand an agent to work from. It is never one thing. It is a hierarchy of descriptions, each aimed at the same system from a different angle. Prose docs at the top say what the thing is for; code comments say why a line reads the way it does; tests assert what must stay true; and schemas and typed records pin the shape of the data. Every one of these is a description of intent — and every one is partial, and every one drifts. The prose goes stale, the comment outlives the code it explained, the test pins yesterday's behavior, the schema lags the migration. This is the hierarchy the up-front half of governance climbs.

The usual agentic recipe gives you docs, code, and tests — and nothing binding them together. A change to the code does not touch the doc; a stale test does not know the schema moved; the prose swears the system does one thing while the code quietly does another. There is no glue, so there are no guardrails: nothing in the setup can tell you the three have fallen out of agreement, because nothing in the setup relates them at all. Drift, in this arrangement, is simply the default, and you find out only when it bites. Figure 3.2-1 draws the two arrangements side by side — the unbound islands, and the structured model that binds them.

The MAGE move adds the rung the three islands were missing: a structured model — a description precise enough that a machine can read it, and because it can, check the other descriptions against it. Not one universal master representation above everything; a model layer, per concern, that binds docs, code, and tests into one checkable whole. That is the property no amount of good prose can give you. And the link between that layer and the code is itself part of the material — an edge a tool can follow from a modeled element to the code it governs, and a check the build re-runs on every change — so the relationship, not just the model, is kept honest. The model becomes the single description you hold the docs, the code, and the tests up to: does the code still match the state machine the model declares? Does the schema still match the records? When the model and the territory disagree, a build-time check fails, and the disagreement surfaces the instant it appears instead of the instant it bites. This is the same drift-and-parity gate from the last section, seen from the other side: there it kept the map equal to the territory; here it is the glue the three-island picture was missing. It is the one edge that makes docs, code, and tests a bound system instead of three things that happen to describe the same repo.

That is the Modeling Thesis at full strength. Documentation matters, and agents have made thorough documentation cheap for the first time, so climb the hierarchy all the way up. The rung at the top is a structured model that binds intent to implementation and that a checker can prove things about. Everything below it — the prose, the comments, the tests — is honest and useful and still drifts; the structured model is the one description that cannot silently diverge from the implementation, because the gate stays red until the two agree again. This is why the companion catalogue gives the models-bridge its own role. The whole aim of the up-front half is to build the one description the machine can check the rest against — and building it well is a craft the Model Zoo in Part 2 teaches by example.

One school of thought comes most of the way here and then stops one rung short. It agrees that loose prose is too soft, and it pushes intent into a fixed shape — but it keeps that shape in constrained English: requirements written to a strict grammar, structured to read like acceptance criteria, with a real model reserved for the cases whose stakes justify the trouble. This is the old cost calculation wearing new clothes. Constrained English helps only to the extent that a parser assigns its structure explicit semantics a checker can enforce. Absent that, a machine can pattern-match the grammar but cannot hold the code up to it and prove the two agree — the distinction is between merely constrained prose and a formally interpreted representation. And reserving the model for where the stakes justify it is the same where-warranted hedge that once confined formal modeling to the high-stakes industries — a judgment that modeling is expensive, formed in a world where keeping the map current cost a person's standing labor. Agents cut that recurring cost sharply. When the map stays in sync almost for free, the structured model stops being the rung you climb to for the critical case and becomes the one you start from.

The sweet spot is easiest to see where independent systems already start from the model. Read these as the surface — the representation the environment is about to make binding; each is a model strong enough to be worth wiring to a gate, which is the move the next gallery shows.

Worked Examples

Siemens. Siemens builds model-first: SysML and UML, CAD, simulation, BOM, and digital twins are the primary artifact, and code is one of the things those models generate. The agents reason over the model, and physics — solvers, software-in-the-loop, virtual ECUs — stands where a lint would elsewhere to check it. The model is the compact object the work reasons through, not documentation kept beside the code.

Spotify. Spotify represents its estate as a Backstage catalog — an architecture model carrying dependency and endpoint lineage — that its migration agents navigate instead of grepping raw source. The model puts the right place in one window, so a change once spread across many teams is planned against the map. It is the sweet spot in practice: compact enough to hold, precise enough to act on.

DocAble. DocAble carries intent in a typed remediation IR: every fix is a typed node the fidelity validator can walk and the provenance layer can stamp. The IR sits between the loose prose of a requirement and the verbose raw document library — accurate, unambiguous, and small enough to reason over. The agent plans and checks its work against the model, not the file.

Takeaway. The shared abstraction is the model as the sweet spot: a representation compact enough to hold in one window yet precise enough for a machine to check, so the fleet reasons over the map instead of wrestling the territory.

3.2.4 A drift check proves agreement, not correctness

The claim I just made hides a trap, and the book's enthusiasm for drift gates can be misread into a dangerous half-truth. A model wired to a build-time drift check cannot rot — that much is real. But it can still be wrong. The gate proves one thing: the model and the code agree. It says nothing about whether either of them matches what you meant. Both can move together, in the same wrong direction, and the gate stays a contented green the whole way.

Picture a model that says an endpoint is protected, and code that leaves it open. A drift check catches that — but only because the model already carries the concept that the endpoint is meant to be protected. Now derive the model from the implementation instead. The derived model reads the open endpoint and records it, faithfully, as unprotected. The code is unprotected; the model says unprotected; the drift check agrees. The bug is invisible, the gate is green, and everything is "in sync."

The power, then, was never in modeling as such; it was in the model being authored independently of the code. A model derived from the code is a mirror: it reflects what is, and a mirror cannot tell you the thing it reflects is wrong. A model authored from intent is a spec: it says what ought to be, and only a spec catches a wrong "is." People will generate a model from their codebase and believe they hold the same thing as someone who wrote one from intent. They do not. The drift check looks identical in both cases. What differs is whether an independent claim exists for the code to be measured against, or only a reflection agreeing with itself. A derived descriptive model is not worthless — it becomes a specification the moment independent intent is attached to it, checked against a policy or invariant authored apart from the code. What a bare mirror lacks is that independent claim; supply one and the reflection acquires something to be wrong against.

This is the passage where the built bridge lands. A spec wired to a gate is intent given authority: represented intent becomes authority. In other words: the model stops being advice and starts becoming something the build, the deployment pipeline, or the runtime refuses to violate.

This does not weaken the drift gate; it tells you what the gate is for. Checking that the model and the code agree is mechanical, and the book automates it. Deciding what belongs in the model — which concepts it must carry, which "ought"s it must assert — does not automate. That is the essential residual Brooks warned would not yield, and under this method it moves one level up: from "write correct code" to "author the model that says what correct means." The gate is necessary. The authored model is what makes the gate mean anything.

That authored move has a name. Recall the Representation–Alignment Proposition from Part 2: as engineering intent becomes more explicit and structured, the set of obligations that can be enforced deterministically expands. That is the frontier the whole method rides, and on the Alignment side it is exactly what the gate cashes — the richer the spec, the more of the residual it pulls into the gate.

We met a corner of this already, without naming it. A done-check can confirm the model was updated but not that it is right — the same asymmetry surfaces below, in the semantic-gap passage on the definition-of-done audit. And it recurs in the test dimension: a journey test that asserts only that the flow ran is a mirror of execution, while a typed task-closure post-condition is the authored spec that says what "done" means — the Scenarios view draws that line for oracles.

What the gate adds, and where it stops, is sharpest against a system that binds without gating. Read these as the surface made binding — the same representation the last gallery showed, now wired to a check that fails on disagreement, so the model crosses from advising to holding.

Worked Examples

Siemens (near-miss). Siemens keeps its models and code coupled across a digital thread, but the coupling synchronizes rather than gates: a human confirms the BOM, and physics solvers stand in for a build-time drift check. The correspondence is maintained, yet nothing fails the build the instant the two diverge. It shows the binding without the hard gate — the near-miss that marks what the gate adds.

DocAble. DocAble wires each model to a build-time drift-and-parity gate: when the model and the code it describes disagree, the build fails, and the disagreement surfaces the instant it appears instead of the instant it bites. The gate makes "the map equals the territory" a checked property rather than a hope — the one edge that keeps docs, code, and tests a bound system.

Takeaway. The shared move is binding the model to the territory with a check that fails on disagreement — which proves the two agree, not that either is right: the gate keeps the map honest, but only an independently authored spec makes it worth keeping.

3.2.5 A model is a convenience, not a second copy of the truth

The other side of the mirror carries an over-reach. Name it before the enthusiasm for models runs away. Grant that the model is a spec, authored from intent, and that the drift gate holds it honest against the code. It is still not a second place the truth lives. The code already holds the truth: it is exactly what runs. A model that only restates what the code plainly says has bought you a maintenance bill and nothing else — a faithful copy you now have to keep faithful.

What a model earns its keep with is what it lets an agent do. It puts the right place in one window, so the fleet navigates to a change instead of grepping for it. It carries the claim across a boundary two files should honor, so a root-cause walk reads the seam instead of guessing at it. It gives a benchmark somewhere to point. Each of those is an operation the raw code could not afford at the fleet's speed, and each is the reason to draw the model. None of them is "record what the code says a second time." So the test before you model a surface is not "could this be modeled" — almost anything can — but "what will an agent do with the map that it cannot do against the territory." Model where an operation needs the map. Where none does, the code is already the truth, and a model only adds a thing to keep in sync.

Readers from knowledge representation will hear an echo here. One account holds that agentic systems need explicit ontologies — because language models generate probabilistically, they benefit from logical structure outside the model to check their outputs against: "probabilistic reasoning inside, logical guardrails outside" 55. Frank Coyle, “Why Agentic Systems Need Ontologies,” Medium, August 2026, https://medium.com/@coyle_41098/why-agentic-systems-need-ontologies-6361b54958e1.. The instinct matches this chapter's. But an ontology — a declared vocabulary of what exists and which arrangements are admissible — is one honest kind of model. The Modeling Thesis is wider: it commits to no single formalism and reaches for whichever model fits the move — architecture, state machine, schema, type system, user journey, or ontology. An ontology says what exists; it does not by itself say how the system is built, changed, deployed, or kept coherent.

3.2.6 Execute the model directly? A spectrum, not a verdict

One old idea says that if the structured model is where you now start, you should finish there too — skip the code and execute the model directly. Executable modeling lies on a spectrum: at one end no modeling (fast to change, no guarantee past the tests), at the other a model detailed enough to run and therefore as large and unforgiving as the code it replaces. Where you sit was always an economic call, set by the cost of keeping the map equal to the territory — and that cost is what agents just changed: they shift the optimum outward, further than the Agile era could afford, but they do not eliminate the essential complexity Brooks guards at the far end. Part 2 reopens the old verdict against executable models with the history and the discipline; this chapter only needs that the sweet spot moved, and is still moving.

3.2.7 The semantic gap

Enforce a property at the wrong level and it slips through; Figure 3.2-2 shows the fix — lift the check to where the meaning is legible.

Now the trap. Say you want to enforce "if the agent substantially changed the call graph but did not update the overlaid model, reject it." It sounds like a pre-commit hook. It is not. An agent carrying out a real task makes many commits toward one goal; the model may be legitimately out of sync in the middle and correct again by the end. Enforce at the commit and you are checking a sentence for grammar before the paragraph is written.

This is the semantic gap: the failure you get whenever you enforce a property at the wrong level of abstraction. Operating-system security lives with it constantly — the OS sees individual system calls, but "this program is exfiltrating data" is a pattern across calls, invisible in any single one; you have to stitch ten network calls together before the leak is legible. Model drift is the same shape. The commit is the wrong granularity.

The fix is to find the level where the property is actually legible, and enforce there. For model drift, that level is the agent returning from its task, because at that moment the entire feature and its plan are visible. You can see whether the work changed the model, and whether the body of commits includes the tests, the documentation, and everything else the task was supposed to carry. This is exactly where the companion repository places its definition-of-done audit: a check that runs when an agent finishes an Epic, over the whole unit of work, rather than at any single commit inside it. "Correct" is a hard word — you cannot fully prove the model is right — but you can check that it was updated and that the surrounding work is complete, and that is the right semantic level to do it.

Learn more about this governance mechanism: definition-of-done audit.

Epic. A tracked unit of work larger than one commit: a feature or effort with its own file, plan, and definition of done, carried out over several agent dispatches.

Definition of done (DoD). The explicit list of conditions a unit of work must meet to count as finished — tests present, docs updated, the model in sync — checked at the end rather than assumed.

3.2.8 A second worked example: the context-fullness bank-hook

The semantic gap shows up in the operator's own loop, not just the product. When a long session fills the harness's context window, the harness compacts: it summarizes the history and throws the raw record away to make room. That decision is a best-effort guess, because the harness sees tokens, not meaning — it cannot tell that the three paragraphs settling the architecture matter more than the forty walking a directory tree. It sits below the semantics of "what is worth keeping," so it can only approximate.

The property the operator actually cares about is not fullness. It is whether the durable record — the strategy document, the hand-off notes — is current, so that a compaction loses nothing important. The harness knows one number, how full the window is; it does not know whether those documents reflect the last hour of decisions or went stale ninety minutes ago. So move the check up to where the meaning is legible: a hook in the operator's loop that fires on two conditions together — the window getting full and the durable records having gone untouched while work continued. When both hold, it emits one reminder: bank the current state. A companion post-compaction hook then knows exactly where to rebuild context from, because the pre-work made those records the authoritative place to look.

Learn more about this governance mechanism: orchestrator lifecycle hooks.

Why both conditions, and not one? A full window is common; a stale record is common; either alone fires the reminder constantly, and a reminder that fires constantly trains the operator to stop and re-summarize the session instead of doing the work — self-summarization churn, the loop spending its turns banking its own banking. Both at once is the rare case where a compaction would actually lose something, and the only case worth the interruption. Fullness is not the semantic property; fullness plus stale durable state is.

One honest note, because the semantic-gap idea should make you notice it. The theoretically right moment to bank is the instant before compaction, and the harness even offers a pre-compact hook there. It is the wrong hook: it fires too late for a comfortable bank, and worse, writing the hand-off itself costs tokens, so a bank kicked off at the boundary can be the very thing that trips the compaction it was meant to precede. There is no clean rung here — so a hook at roughly the right level, earlier and on the dual condition, beats the theoretically-perfect one. That is the honest difference from the drift example, where agent-return genuinely is the level at which the property is legible and waiting for it loses nothing.

Part 2 shows how to author and maintain these models; the reusable rule here is to enforce a property at the level where it becomes observable.

A model wired to a drift gate holds you to what you knew to write down. But you do not know a whole system in advance — you discover what you are building by building it, and the sharpest failures are the ones no model predicted. That is the other half of governance: not the obligations you specify up front, but the ones a fast-moving agent reveals after the fact. The next chapter builds it, and gives the two forms every mechanism finally takes — the model and its gate among them.
