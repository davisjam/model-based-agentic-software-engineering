Part 2 — Modeling  ::  Chapter 10

2.7 Modeling Measurement and Evidence

A system can follow every declared transition and still be unusably slow, unexpectedly expensive, or unable to explain what it did. Behavior models state what executions are legal; measurement and evidence models state what the running system promises and what record it must leave behind. One turns quantities into explicit obligations. The other turns actions into an auditable account.

These models therefore answer two related questions: is the system operating inside the envelope we said it would? and does its account of its own work match what actually happened?

2.7.1 What the system promises about time and cost

Behavior says what the system does when it runs. It does not say what that run costs, how fast it answers, or whether the running system is still inside those bounds today. That is a measured question, and it needs its own model.

The execution policy in the previous chapter asks how hard a host may be pushed. This model asks whether the running system remains inside its promised throughput, latency, and cost bounds. One determines a plan; the other checks an observed condition.

A system that is never measured makes no promise it can keep. It is fast until it is slow, cheap until a runaway job costs a fortune, healthy until the queue backs up. Worse, the numbers that would explain a bad day were never recorded, so every incident is diagnosed from scratch. A configuration constant does not help here: a wall-clock kill at ten minutes tells you when a process will be stopped. It does not tell you whether the expected worst case is approaching ten minutes.

Operational-envelope model · Measurement

  • Engineering question — What does the system promise about latency, cost, and throughput — and is it inside those promises now?
  • Model — a typed statement of the measured quantities that must stay bounded: end-to-end latency, per-job wall-clock, per-call output cost, and queue depth by work-size class, each paired with the ceiling it may not cross.
  • Property — the quantities that define the operating envelope are explicitly named, measured, and compared with declared bounds; selected bounds drive admission or stop decisions, while others may remain report-only.
  • Quality attribute — performance, scalability, efficiency.

The measured envelope is itself modeled, not left to intuition. A small set of quantities carries the story: the warm end-to-end latency, tracked as measured rows the deploy smoke appends — around twenty-four seconds; a per-job wall-clock ceiling near ten minutes; a hard cap on how many tokens a single generation call may spend; a size classification of each incoming document; and a credit balance — not a per-file page cap — that throttles admission. Each is a promise; each has a bound. Figure 2.7-1 places the observed warm path against the predicted worst case and the budget the system promises to hold.

What the model lets you establish is whether the running system is leaving the portions of the envelope the instrumentation actually covers. It earns that through a sensor and a gate.

  • The sensor is instrumentation. The pipeline emits phase timings, per-segment timers, server-clock start/finish pairs, and worker heartbeats; a deploy-time budget smoke drives a fixture through the whole pipeline and appends the measured end-to-end row. The promised numbers are compared against measured ones, not hoped for.
  • The gate is admission. Before work begins, an admission gate sizes the job, classifies it, and debits the account's credit balance; a job whose account cannot cover it is refused at the door rather than started and abandoned mid-flight. A second gate refuses a different way: in the staging environment, a fidelity check fails an individual repair pass rather than shipping a regression — it emits a signal and exits with a distinct code the surrounding system reads as this pass did harm; stop.

One illustrative invariant carries the shape (Table 2.7-1):

One quantity has no production gate. The per-chunk cost model exists and is instrumented, but in production it only reports; it does not split or refuse work. The system's hard cost cap lives in an offline benchmark harness. This case separates representation from authority: the system can name and sense a quantity without giving it blocking power. Part III treats that as an explicit design decision rather than an automatic final step.

A measurement model names the quantities you promise to hold and the bound on each. Its authority is a gate that reads the sensed quantity and refuses to advance when the bound would break. The move: decide what the measurement must drive — an admission decision, a stop decision — and sense one level below the surface number so the data can act, not merely report.

The operational-envelope model measures the running system against resource bounds; drift checks — introduced in Making Models Executable — measure the model against the territory it claims to represent. One watches the system, the other the model.

This belongs to the service-level-objective, capacity-model, and admission-control tradition. The stronger form wires measurement to an engineering decision; the weaker form reports the quantity without giving it authority. DocAble's production cost cap currently occupies the latter rung. Three teams show the same move realized on materially different resources:

Worked Examples

Spotify. Spotify gates a merge on measured evidence. An automated merger admits a change only once its verifiers and monitoring have generated the evidence that it is safe, so the measurement is the admission criterion: the system reads a signal about the change and refuses to advance until the signal clears. The measured quantity is verification evidence; the ceiling is "not yet proven safe"; the gate is the merge itself. Same function as the envelope model — measure, then refuse to advance on a breach — realized as an evidence-gated merge.

Zenseact. Zenseact treats context as a scarce resource and rations it against a measured budget. A deterministic router measures how much context a task needs and holds it under a ceiling, cutting what each step carries by a large, measured fraction rather than a guessed one. The measured quantity is context size; the ceiling is the routing budget; the gate is the router that will not hand a step more than its share. A different resource, a different mechanism — but the same move: measure the envelope, ration against the bound.

DocAble. DocAble measures a latency-cost-throughput envelope and gates against it in two places. An admission gate refuses a job whose account cannot cover its measured size before any work starts; an in-pass fidelity gate fails a remediation that regresses quality rather than shipping it. The measured quantities are wall-clock, cost, and queue depth by size class; the ceilings are the job budget and the account balance; the gates are admission and per-pass fidelity. Same function again, realized as a resource-and-quality envelope enforced at the door and at each pass.

Takeaway. The same design pattern can govern different resources: name the quantity, state the bound, sense the current value, and decide whether a breach should merely report or should control admission.

Authority surface — The envelope names latency, cost, and queue-depth quantities and the bound on each; selected bounds already stop work at the door, while others are sensed but report-only. The model makes those obligations explicit; Part III asks which mechanism should hold each, and where a sensed-but-ungoverned bound should gain blocking power.

2.7.2 Keeping the explanation true to the change

Documentation is easy to dismiss as prose about the real system. That view becomes untenable when the documentation is evidence. A system that changes a customer's artifact returns not only the remediated document but a record of what it changed — and if implementation and explanation can drift independently, the record cannot support an audit.

Measurement and Documentation answer kindred questions: both are the system keeping an account of itself — one the quantities it must hold, the other the actions it took. A remediation inserts a description, retags a heading, repairs a reading order — then hands back an account of the work. Those are two claims, and they can disagree. When the explanation is written by hand, separately from the code that does the work, the two drift: the account omits half of what happened, or names a change the code never made. And when something goes wrong, "which step made this change?" becomes a multi-hop hunt across files — each hop cheap, the whole chain expensive.

Provenance-and-changelog model · Documentation

  • Engineering question — Does the human-facing explanation of what changed stay in sync with what the code actually did?
  • Model — a provenance model: every mutation stamped, every inserted artifact recorded, and the customer-facing changelog derived from those stamps rather than written by hand.
  • Property — every change is stamped, every inserted artifact is recorded and survives to the output, and the changelog is a loss-free projection of the stamps — so what the customer is told changed equals what the code did.
  • Quality attribute — understandability, maintainability, auditability.

The architecture reverses the usual relationship: rather than describe the work afterward, the code records it as it works, and the explanation is a view of that record. Three moves, in order:

  • Mutate, then stamp. Every verb that changes the document writes an entry — which verb, in which pass, visible or invisible — into a provenance registry embedded in the file. A change that ships without its stamp is the failure this move exists to prevent.
  • Insert, then record. Every artifact the tool adds is recorded with its locator and its placement, and it carries a reserved name-prefix so a later reader — and the validator — can tell an inserted artifact from one the author wrote.
  • Derive, don't author. The changelog the customer reads is projected from the registry, one row per stamped entry, loss-free. An empty registry yields an empty changelog — by construction, the document tells the truth about itself.

What the model buys is a single equality: what the customer is told changed equals what the code did. It earns that because the registry the code writes as it works is the only source the changelog reads. There is no second, hand-kept account to drift, so provenance collapses a four-hop forensic reconstruction into a registry lookup. Figure 2.7-2 draws that derivation, from a mutation's stamp to the changelog the customer reads.

The same move applies wherever a system acts on a user's artifact and must later explain or reverse what it did: derive the explanation from a record written as the action occurs, require that record mechanically, and generate the human-facing account from it. Audit logs, data-pipeline provenance, and regulatory audit trails are all this model; what separates the real ones from theater is whether emitting the record is mandatory.

Authority surface — Every change is stamped, every inserted artifact recorded, and the changelog is a loss-free projection of the stamps, so what the customer is told changed equals what the code did. The model makes that obligation explicit; Part III asks which mechanism should hold it and at which boundary — the mandatory-emission check that gives the record its authority.

---

Measurement tells the system whether its quantitative promises are holding. Provenance tells it whether its account of its own actions is faithful. Each answers one question in isolation. Real engineering tasks cross several such questions at once, which is why the final chapter joins the models rather than adding another class.
