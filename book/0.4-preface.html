Preface

Prefer to read offline? Download the PDF.

How do we safely grant autonomy to commodity intelligence?

Everything in this book is an answer to that question. Not just software. Not just coding agents. MAGE is one answer.

In this book, that question appears first as software engineering: when implementation becomes cheap enough that agents can build almost anything you can describe, what must the surrounding engineering environment do so they can act autonomously without making the system untrustworthy? The problem is autonomy, not code generation. The answer this book develops has two parts and a loop: make intent explicit in models the fleet reasons through, make the environment enforce the obligations that matter, and, when autonomy exposes a failure class nobody foresaw, convert that lesson into durable structure, so the next agent inherits a safer, more capable world. That the intelligence is now a commodity, abundant and cheap and general, is why the frame holds. As intelligence stops being the scarce thing, engineering reorganizes around what still is: judgment, representations, evidence, authority, and governance.

Why MAGE?

MAGE's contribution is not a list of practices. It is an account of how those practices fit together. The book develops that account from one production system studied deeply, then turns it outward. Independent engineering organizations reveal the same moves in very different forms: externalizing the world the intelligence must reason about, giving engineering intent authority, and converting recurring judgment into durable structure. Those comparisons both test the theory and show what the method looks like when no two organizations implement it the same way.

Those reports describe successful engineering practices; MAGE attempts something different. It tries to extract a coherent engineering theory from them — to relate the practices to one another, explain why they work, and name the engineering design spaces the individual reports leave implicit. That is worth doing because engineering advances when recurring structures become nameable. Once a failure pattern, an organizational investment, or a design tradeoff carries a shared name, engineers can compare systems, weigh alternatives, and deliberately improve them, instead of rediscovering the same ideas independently.

That is now the argument, and it has a shape. Parts 2 and 3 develop the two theses separately. Part 4 joins the two theses into the MAGE method and turns them into practical engineering work: the workflow, migration into existing systems, validation of change, and the encoding of recurring practice into reusable skills. Brief industrial examples show the same moves appearing elsewhere, without interrupting the method. Part 5 opens the originating case, DocAble, end to end, showing where the method came from and how it evolved under pressure. Part 6 steps back from both method and case: it places the independently evolved systems side by side and asks what has actually been learned — the theory's scope, its relationship to software engineering and to engineering more broadly, and what it implies for the profession.

This is a book about engineering — specifically, about what engineering work becomes when writing the code stops being the hard part. For most of the history of software, the scarce resource was implementation: someone had to sit down and type the thing into existence, and that typing was the bottleneck everything else queued behind. Coding agents have moved that bottleneck. Code is now cheap to produce and fast to change. What is not cheap is judging, constraining, validating, and maintaining it — deciding what to build, catching where fast code went wrong, and keeping a growing system trustworthy while it changes under you every day. That shift is the subject of this book.

Engineering starts from a goal, limited resources, and competing requirements, and it demands that the properties of a proposed solution be analyzable against those requirements. Engineers do not assess the limit of a bridge by driving larger and larger trucks over it. They compute the load it will carry, locate its limits, and know what evidence justifies that belief before failure teaches it. Software built with agents earns the same standard, not a weaker one because the machine can produce plausible implementations quickly. A system may work, and may even work impressively, with little explicit modeling; MAGE does not dispute that. It draws one distinction: success by observation is not engineering assurance.

This book began as an attempt to solve one hard engineering problem, watched closely over months of building a real production system. The theory emerged from that one deeply studied system. The order was deliberate. I did not begin by surveying what other teams had built and distilling the common themes; I built and studied one system deeply enough to propose a theory, then asked whether it held up against independent industrial practice. As the manuscript developed, I turned its vocabulary outward, interpreting it against a growing body of independent engineering reports from organizations including Cloudflare, Docker, Shopify, Spotify, Siemens, and Zenseact. Those reports are not only corroboration. They are independent engineering systems against which the theory is tested — counterexamples as much as agreement — and across the book it is challenged against them, refined where they press on it, and occasionally extended to hold. They frequently converge on similar mechanisms, while revealing where MAGE supplies vocabulary and explanatory structure not explicit in the original reports. What emerges is not a common implementation. It is a recurring engineering grammar beneath very different systems. Figure 0.4-1 traces that path from the one case to the theory the closing chapters now carry.

This book ultimately argues something larger than a lesson about documents — that software engineering is a long-horizon reasoning task, and a model's central role is not to record a system after the fact but to extend how much of it a human and an agent can hold in view at once — but that is the direction the book heads, earned later, not a claim to spend here.

What changed

Fleet. The set of coding agents working the codebase — the agentic-era workforce this book governs, in place of a team of human engineers.

Lint. An automated check that scans code for a banned pattern and fails the commit when it finds one, so a rule holds without a human remembering it.

Gate. A check placed across a pipeline step — a commit, a deploy — that refuses to let the step through until its condition is met.

Scaling a fleet hits a wall, the way scaling a team does — but a different one. Add people and you eventually meet the coordination problem Brooks described: potential communication paths grow roughly with the square of the team, and the coordination overhead eats each new hire's gain. A fleet does not drown in that circle. It hits a different bound. A fleet is limited by how well its models reason and how much they can hold in view, and when it reaches that bound, ungoverned, it decays into churn. Context pressure is the sharpest driver: when the work outgrows what the fleet can hold in view, the agent loses the thread, re-derives what it already built, and confidently undoes yesterday's fix. The wall is churn.

Churn's causes are three things the fleet does not know. It does not know what to build: the requirements are ambiguous. It does not know how to realize the change: the architecture has drifted into a soup no context window can hold. And it does not know how to change the system without breaking it: every edit lands blind on invariants nobody wrote down. Every technique here attacks a cause, not the symptom. Models attack the first two not-knowings — they bind intent to structure and hold the architecture in a compact form, so the agent knows what to build and the work fits in-window. And in doing so they leave something governance can use: a representation explicit enough to check a change against. Governance attacks the third not-knowing on that surface — it keeps a change from introducing errors in the first place, or makes them visible the moment it does.

Churn. The condition in which an increasing share of agent effort is spent rediscovering context, undoing recent changes, repairing regressions, or reconciling inconsistencies rather than advancing the system. It is the agent-fleet form of velocity decay: fixing crowds out building.

The stakes here are tokenomics, not just tidiness. Keeping a task inside the window is far cheaper than letting it spill, and the gap compounds: I once ran four Claude Max subscriptions flat out to keep the fleet fed, and the same velocity now barely fills two — because a model lets each agent find what it needs instead of re-deriving it, and keeps the work in-window, off the cliff where the token bill jumps. The point of MAGE is not only to spend less effort fighting churn; it is to make engineering effort compound — each conversion banked as capability the next task inherits, the positive dual of churn the closing theory names.

Tokenomics. How many tokens it takes to get a task done — and it is not linear. Overflow the context window and the cost jumps: the harness compacts (a lossy save), and the agent then burns tokens rebuilding the context it lost. So keeping a task inside the window is not merely tidier, it is far cheaper — which is exactly what a model buys, by shrinking what the agent must hold.

Why I built a production system to study this

My name is James Davis. I am a professor of software engineering in the Elmore Family School of Electrical and Computer Engineering at Purdue University. My job is to find out things other people do not know and write them down so they do. This book records one of those findings. Over a stretch of a few months I built a real production system — a document-accessibility service, DocAble — almost entirely by dispatching AI coding agents rather than writing code myself. I watched what that mode of work demanded, and this book is what I learned about doing it well. DocAble is the running example throughout; the claims here rest on that real production system. A first concrete look at it comes early, in MAGE by Example; the full description lives at the back, in Part 5 (The Evidence).

Building DocAble was not only a way to ship a product. It was a way to ask a question that is hard to ask from the outside. Once agents write almost all of the code, what is the engineering that remains? The question is no longer whether they can produce useful code. They can. It is what is left for a human to do when they produce nearly all of it.

That question resists the usual instruments. A benchmark isolates one capability and scores it. A build report tells you a fleet reproduced a framework, compiled a kernel, or migrated a codebase in a week. A set of interviews tells you how developers supervise the tools they were handed. Each is a real piece, and none of them shows how the pieces move together over the life of a new production system: how an architectural mistake hardens into a lint, how a failure seen twice becomes a gate, how one authored model changes what an agent can do, how the controls pile up until the environment can be trusted to run itself. You cannot watch that from a scorecard or a single interview. You have to build the system, hold it to a standard someone would actually rely on, and watch it over months.

So that is the design of the study. I built something whose quality mattered, let the fleet do nearly all of the implementation, and treated every failure as evidence about the method rather than noise to clean up. The book's vocabulary — governance conversion, the Modeling and Alignment theses, constraints and sensors, the governed engineering environment — names structures that recurred often enough to earn names. I offer them as working concepts, precise enough to compare, argue with, and replace, not as the field's settled terms. Where my investigation extends the existing literature, I have tried to cite the work it stands on, so a reader can see where this case joins the conversation and where it departs.

Read the book in that spirit. One deeply worked case cannot prove that every project will behave like this one. What it can do is open a single machine far enough to say what the rest of us should look for.

Why you can trust what follows

I want to be plain about what this book is and is not. It is not a memoir of the experience, though the experience was strange enough to warrant one. It is not a survey of every AI coding tool, which would be stale before it printed. It is a field report and a method — Model-Based Agentic Software Engineering, or MAGE: the concrete engineering practices that turn a fast code fabricator into a production line you can trust. Where a claim rests on a number, I give the number. Where a practice was learned by getting it wrong first, I say so.

One caution belongs up front, because it colors everything after it. This is a single-case field report: one production system, DocAble, authored by one engineer inside a single vendor's agent ecosystem, watched over a few months and in daily use by me and my colleagues at Purdue. Where I give a number, read it as an observation from that case rather than a measured law, and every claim stays scoped to it; the closing limitations set out exactly how far that reaches. But the case joins a conversation already underway. Practitioner reports agree, in volume, that these practices work with agents — write far more tests than you would for a human team, specify before you build, make the environment hold the line. Those reports supply the what; a deep case you control end to end is the right instrument for the how and the why, the mechanism behind the practices worked out in enough detail to transfer. The book also holds itself to the method it teaches: each empirical claim is registered in a structured model that binds it to the datum or the citation behind it, and a check reports any claim left with neither.

A footnote on the evidence. The claims and their backing sit in a small ledger beside the manuscript: each empirical claim tied to a supporting datum or a positioned citation, nested under the book's argument, with a query that surfaces any claim carrying neither. It is the same discipline the book turns on code, turned here on the book's own argument. The colophon lays out the full set of models that govern the manuscript.

What the method turned out to be

Two theses drive this book, and they run through every chapter that follows. They do not run side by side: the first makes engineering intent explicit; the second gives that explicit intent authority, and can only do so because the first has built something for it to act on. Both are stated in full where each part opens; here it is only their relationship that matters, because that relationship is where the method actually lives.

Modeling makes intent explicit; Alignment makes it binding.

In other words: first write down what matters in a form both people and machines can understand; then teach the engineering environment to insist on it.

The theses are causally linked, not parallel. Modeling turns implicit engineering knowledge into a representation both humans and machines can reason over; Alignment attaches authority to it. Richer representations make more obligations enforceable, so the surface Modeling builds is what Alignment acts on. Alignment depends on Modeling; Modeling does not depend on Alignment — a dependency, not a disappearance, which is why there are still two.

The order of the two working parts follows that dependency. Part 2 develops Modeling, the surfaces; Part 3 develops Alignment, the authority. You can only make binding what you have first made explicit.

Neither thesis is the method by itself. MAGE is what happens when they run as a cycle: model the intent, give it authority, do the governed work, convert recurring failures into engineering capital, and repeat.

Read top to bottom, the whole argument is a single chain:

Commodity intelligence creates a reasoning problem. Context engineering begins the response. Durable context becomes structured knowledge. Structured knowledge becomes engineering models. Models make intent explicit. Explicit intent makes more judgment mechanizable. Alignment gives that intent authority. Failures enrich the representations and controls. The environment compounds. Autonomy scales.

That is MAGE.

That chain is the reorganization the opening named — judgment, representations, evidence, authority, and governance — assembled into one causal line rather than a list.

The closing Part asks whether this is ultimately a fact about software at all.

The through-line, and who it is for

The through-line is a single idea; hold every chapter against it. When you build with an agent, you are not managing a coder; you are running a printer. A 3D printer builds almost anything you can describe, but only what you describe — hand it a bad model and you get a bad part. An agent is the same. It builds most of what you can explain to it, and the entire discipline of this book lives in that "so long as you can explain it." Explaining a system to a probabilistic machine well enough that it builds the right thing, at speed, without a human reading every line — that is a real engineering craft, not a knack, and it can be taught.

This book is for the software engineers who suspect the ground has moved and want to know where to stand. This is therefore not primarily a book about the internals of agents. It is about the engineering structures that become necessary once agents are capable enough to matter. It is also for the technical leads, architects, and engineering managers who must decide how autonomous software development should work inside their organizations. Governing a fleet is no longer only an individual's craft; it is an organizational choice, and the closing part turns the method outward to that scale — a way to think about an engineering organization, not only an engineer. On the surface it is about a tool I built to help people with disabilities access documents. Underneath, it is about a working method for building anything with agents: how to frame the problem, model the world for the machine, put every change on rails from dispatch to production, and convert each failure you hit into a guardrail that stops the next one. You will not need my domain to use any of it. You will need a codebase, an agent, and the willingness to treat the instructions as the job.

After reading it, you should be able to recognize a recurring governance problem when it surfaces, reason about the alternative engineering environments that might answer it, and deliberately design mechanisms that fit your organization rather than copying another company's process. That is the whole promise, and the rest of the book is one move toward it, repeated.

Everything that follows is one move, repeated: convert judgment into infrastructure.

The DevOps era learned to carry infrastructure, policy, and configuration as code. This book carries the next thing: judgment, as code — more generally, judgment as mechanism, since much of what follows is models, constraints, validators, and workflows rather than source. The phrase is a slogan, not a literal claim; the glossary makes it exact.
