Preface

Prefer to read offline? Download the PDF.

How do we safely grant autonomy to commodity intelligence?

Society has long entrusted consequential work to computers. Software flies aircraft, calculates tax returns, routes telephone calls, and controls power plants. Traditionally, software engineers stood between human intent and machine action: they translated goals, constraints, and judgments into sufficiently precise specifications, programs, and controls, and computers executed what they had made explicit.

Now, the boundary between human intent and machine action is shifting. General-purpose machine intelligence can interpret intent, exercise judgment, and produce implementation at low enough cost and high enough capacity to become an ordinary engineering resource. This increasingly available capability has been described as commodity intelligence.11. John Nosta, “Intelligence as a Commodity,” Psychology Today, March 12, 2026, https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-digital-self/202603/intelligence-as-a-commodity. 22. Darwin Ling, “The $0.50 Barrel of Intelligence,” GoodAI, July 20, 2026, https://goodai.substack.com/p/the-050-barrel-of-intelligence. Software engineering has begun to confront the consequences. Implementation has become cheap enough that agents can produce and modify software at extraordinary speed. What must the surrounding engineered environment do so that they can act autonomously without making the system untrustworthy?

None of the underlying engineering problems begins with AI. Large systems have always exceeded the reasoning capacity of individual engineers, and software engineering has always depended on abstractions, decomposition, evidence, and controls to make them tractable. Commodity intelligence changes the economics. Implementation becomes abundant relative to engineering judgment, so the old constraints become more visible and more consequential: what should be built, what representation makes the relevant property tractable, what evidence supports a change, and which obligations the environment can hold without asking a human to reconstruct them each time.

Why MAGE? Why Models?

This book—Model-Based Agentic Software Engineering (MAGE)—argues that modeling becomes central when implementation becomes abundant. Coding agents can produce implementation faster than engineers can reconstruct the system-level knowledge needed to judge it. Models externalize that knowledge at useful levels of abstraction, making larger engineering questions tractable to both humans and agents.

Modeling is not the whole answer. Engineering obligations also need authority: constraints, sensors, validators, and gates that can hold autonomous work to them. MAGE calls this Alignment. Modeling and Alignment solve different problems, but they reinforce one another: better representations expose more consequential properties to reasoning and control.

The ideas in MAGE first emerged from one production system studied in depth, then were tested against the experience of other engineering organizations. Those public accounts describe related structures reached through different routes. Across them, important system knowledge is made explicit, mechanisms constrain autonomous work, and some lessons are preserved for later reuse. They also differ in revealing ways. Some rely heavily on explicit models; others depend more on local controls and human judgment. Those differences help distinguish the underlying method from any particular implementation of it.

This is a book about engineering—specifically, about what engineering work becomes when implementation capacity becomes abundant. That does not mean software engineers historically spent most of their day writing source code; empirical studies have long shown otherwise.** Both studies distinguish source-code production from the many other activities that make up professional software work. CITE0 CITE1 The relevant constraint is broader. Software design is repeatedly realized and revised through implementation, debugging, testing, refactoring, integration, and contact with the working system. For most of software's history, that realization loop consumed scarce skilled labor and constrained how many alternatives a team could afford to explore and how quickly it could learn. Coding agents change those economics. Implementation capacity becomes much less scarce relative to engineering judgment: deciding what should be built, representing a system well enough to reason about it, determining whether a change is correct, and keeping a rapidly changing system coherent.

Engineering starts from goals, limited resources, and competing requirements, and it asks what properties a proposed solution will have and what evidence supports that belief. Engineers do not assess the limit of a bridge by driving larger and larger trucks over it until something fails. They calculate what it should carry and understand the assumptions behind that calculation. Software built with agents deserves the same standard. A system may work, and may even work impressively, with little explicit modeling; MAGE does not dispute that. Success by observation is not engineering assurance.

Where the theory came from

This book began with one hard engineering problem, watched closely over months of building a real production system. Only later did I turn its vocabulary outward, reconstructing independently described systems from Cloudflare, Docker, Shopify, Spotify, Siemens, and Zenseact. Part V presents both views of the evidence: DocAble in depth, then the industrial reconstructions in breadth. Part VI asks what general account can explain both. What changed

Fleet. The set of coding agents working the codebase — the agentic-era workforce this book governs, in place of a team of human engineers.

Large software systems have always exceeded what any one engineer can keep in mind. Software engineering developed mechanisms for coping with that fact: decomposition, architecture, interfaces, specifications, tests, models, reviews, and organizational structures that let people work on parts without reconstructing the whole. Brooks's coordination problem is one consequence of scaling the human organization around that work. Commodity intelligence does not abolish the underlying reasoning problem. It changes how quickly implementation can move relative to the representations, evidence, and authority needed to keep the system coherent.

An agent fleet can generate, inspect, and modify implementation far faster than a human team, but adding implementation capacity does not automatically add equivalent capacity for system-level reasoning or engineering judgment. When the necessary knowledge remains implicit, the fleet repeatedly reconstructs it. When an obligation is locally checkable, a test, sandbox, type, or gate may hold it without a model. But where the obligation depends on architecture, ownership, end-to-end behavior, or another abstraction the environment does not possess, somebody must supply the missing semantics. In many organizations that somebody is still a human reviewer. The result can work, but the human sits in the gap between what the environment can check and what the system actually needs guaranteed.

Churn is what happens when too much of that knowledge and judgment must be paid for again: agents rediscover context, undo recent changes, repair regressions, reconcile inconsistencies, or wait for humans to resolve questions the environment cannot settle. MAGE attacks the recurring causes. Modeling makes more consequential properties explicit and tractable; Alignment makes selected obligations authoritative; governance conversion turns failures in either into structure the next task inherits.

Why I built a production system to study this

Lint. An automated check that scans code for a banned pattern and fails the commit when it finds one, so a rule holds without a human remembering it.

Gate. A check placed across a pipeline step — a commit, a deploy — that refuses to let the step through until its condition is met.

This book began with DocAble, a production service for remediating inaccessible documents. Over several months I built it almost entirely by dispatching AI coding agents rather than writing the implementation myself. The experiment asked a simple question: once agents write almost all of the code, what engineering remains? DocAble is the running example throughout the book: Parts II and III hold the mature system still while the engineering question changes; Part V changes tense, reconstructs how the machinery emerged, then widens to independent industrial practice.

A benchmark cannot answer that question. It isolates a capability and scores it. A build report can show that a fleet reproduced a framework, compiled a kernel, or migrated a codebase in a week. Interviews can show how developers supervise the tools they were handed. Each captures something useful, but none shows how engineering mechanisms accumulate over the life of a production system: an architectural mistake becoming a durable boundary, a repeated failure changing the environment, a model changing what an agent can reason about, or recurring human judgment becoming explicit enough for the environment to carry.

I therefore built something whose quality mattered, let the fleet perform nearly all of the implementation, and treated failures as evidence about the engineering method. The vocabulary in this book—Modeling, Alignment, governance conversion, engineering capital, constraints, sensors, and the governed engineering environment—names structures that recurred often enough to require names. They are working concepts, not settled terminology: I state them precisely enough to compare, challenge, and replace. Where they extend existing software-engineering ideas, I cite the literature they build on.

Evidence and scope

MAGE rests on two complementary kinds of evidence. The first is a single longitudinal case: one production system, built by one engineer using one vendor's agent ecosystem over several months and used in production by me and my colleagues at Purdue. Numbers from that case are observations, not measured laws. The second is a set of industrial reconstructions from independently described systems. Those accounts provide less process depth but greater variation across technical and organizational choices, including systems that rely more heavily on explicit models and systems that rely more heavily on controls and human judgment. Part V presents both evidence sources and states their limitations.

The book also applies its own method to its evidence. Empirical claims are registered in a structured ledger that connects each claim to supporting data or a positioned citation, and an automated check reports claims carrying neither.

What the method turned out to be

Two principles organize MAGE. Modeling makes consequential knowledge and intent explicit; Alignment gives engineering obligations authority. They solve different problems and can exist independently. A sandbox can forbid an action without a model of the system; a test can block a regression against raw behavior; a human reviewer can adjudicate an obligation no machine-readable representation captures. But these forms of Alignment have a natural limit: they can govern only what the environment can state, observe, or ask a person to reconstruct. Modeling changes that boundary. By making architecture, policy, behavior, ownership, measurement, and other system properties explicit, it lets both the fleet and the surrounding controls operate over larger engineering questions.

MAGE therefore places Modeling first as a methodological choice, not a logical prerequisite. The method asks the engineer to build the right abstractions before spending human attention repeatedly bridging what the environment cannot see. Once the relevant properties are explicit, some judgments that formerly required reconstruction become available to repeatable machinery.

  1. Model the consequential knowledge. Choose the engineering question first, then externalize the facts and intent needed to answer it without carrying unnecessary detail. The purpose is not a complete model of the system; it is to make larger properties tractable without repeatedly reconstructing them from implementation.
  2. Give obligations authority. Encode important obligations into constraints, sensors, validators, and gates. Some can act directly on raw actions or artifacts; explicit models make richer, system-level obligations available to mechanical analysis and, where appropriate, enforcement.
  3. Do the governed work. Let agents operate inside the resulting environment. Models provide reasoning surfaces; governance mechanisms provide evidence and boundaries. Human attention moves toward the questions that remain genuinely judgment-laden rather than being spent repeatedly reconstructing deterministically checkable facts.
  4. Convert recurring failures. When autonomous work exposes missing knowledge, a weak abstraction, or an unenforced obligation, repair the instance and then encode the lesson into durable structure. The environment improves because the next task inherits the model or control rather than the memory of the person who solved the problem.

Neither principle is MAGE by itself. The method is the cycle they create: model where better representation changes the engineering problem; align what can be made authoritative; do the governed work; convert recurring failures and judgment into durable structure; repeat. Useful structure accumulates as engineering capital when later work benefits from inheriting it.

Who this book is for

This book is for software engineers who suspect the ground has moved and want to know where to stand. It is not primarily about the internals of agents; it is about the engineering structures that become important once agents can perform substantial implementation work autonomously. It is also for technical leads, architects, and engineering managers deciding how autonomous software development should work inside an organization. A fleet's models, policies, evidence, infrastructure, and remaining points of human judgment are organizational engineering decisions, not merely features of an individual's tool.

The book is also intended for advanced undergraduate and graduate students studying software engineering, software architecture, or AI-assisted development. It assumes familiarity with ordinary software-development practice: source control, testing, APIs, build and deployment workflows, and the basic idea of software architecture. No prior experience with coding agents, formal methods, model-based engineering, or machine learning is required. Concepts from those areas are introduced where the argument needs them and illustrated through the systems and examples in the book.

The scope is deliberately software engineering rather than a comprehensive treatment of every discipline MAGE draws from. In particular, this is not a textbook on model-based systems engineering (MBSE). MAGE adopts the engineering instinct of reasoning through explicit representations and develops a framework for choosing, structuring, and governing models in agentic software engineering. It does not attempt to teach the full range of modeling languages, viewpoints, transformation techniques, systems-engineering methods, or MBSE practice.

Several other lineages appear for the same reason. Model-driven software engineering supplies established ideas about metamodels, transformations, and generated artifacts; programming languages and compilers supply types, intermediate representations, and constrained transformation; formal methods supply executable specifications and machine-checked properties. MAGE uses those ideas where they serve the method rather than attempting to teach the disciplines that developed them. Those subjects have their own literatures and textbooks. The same restraint applies to machine learning: the book introduces such concepts only where they illuminate the framework or its examples.

After reading this book, you should be able to recognize when an engineering problem is being paid for repeatedly in reconstruction or human judgment; decide whether a better representation, stronger authority, or both would retire that cost; and design mechanisms suited to your own system rather than copying another organization's process. One move recurs throughout: convert recurring judgment into durable engineering structure. DevOps learned to make infrastructure, configuration, and policy durable as code. MAGE extends that instinct to models, constraints, evidence, validators, workflows, and the engineering judgment encoded in them.
