\begin{quote}
\mbox{}%
\subsection{Key Ideas}\label{heading:depth-and-breadth}

MAGE was developed through one deeply observed production build. That case provides chronology, mechanism, and repeated observations within one system.

Independent industrial accounts provide a different kind of evidence: variation across systems built by other organizations under different constraints.

\textbf{The first shows how the method developed. The second asks whether the same engineering problems and responses appear elsewhere.}
\end{quote}

\magevalue{chapter:one-problem-many-models} followed Modeling and Alignment through individual engineering episodes, showing how representations and controls changed as DocAble evolved. We now widen the view from those episodes to the production system that made them possible. An agent may implement a change, but the surrounding factory determines how work is selected, what knowledge reaches the agent, which decisions remain open, how results are evaluated, and when a change may be accepted.

The Industrial Revolution offers a useful comparison. It did more than increase the power available to fabricate things. It changed how production was organized and controlled. As machinery increased the rate of fabrication, engineers developed production processes, standards, and measurements that allowed work to proceed without continuous supervision of every operation. The factory became an engineered system in its own right.

This chapter examines the corresponding change in software engineering. \magevalue{sec:the-software-factory} develops two concerns of factory engineering: process design, which organizes production, and tolerance, which establishes acceptable variation in what is produced. \magevalue{sec:inside-docables-software-factory} examines DocAble's factory from the inside, tracing its development, operating mechanisms, and measured results. \magevalue{sec:mage-in-the-wild} then examines factories developed independently by other organizations. These accounts show how the same engineering concerns lead to different arrangements when organizations have different systems, personnel, and obligations.
