Part 4 — The MAGE Method  ::  Chapter 17

4.1 The MAGE Workflow

The previous Parts introduced the ideas one at a time. This Part joins them into an engineering method — and a method is something you run, not something you read. MAGE is not a catalogue of isolated techniques; it is one recurring cycle that every task turns through. Modeling makes intent explicit, Alignment gives that intent authority, and the failures the work reveals feed back into both — so each pass begins from a stronger position than the last.

Three verbs carry the method — Model, Align, Convert — and they are exactly the three the cycle below runs on every turn: model the intent, give that intent authority, convert what recurs into capital. Two other names describe work this Part covers elsewhere. Bootstrap is the harder case, when nothing has been modeled yet and the loop has to start from a drifted estate instead of a clean one — Migrating to MAGE runs it at the scale of a whole codebase. Operate is what the loop becomes once a governed environment has run long enough to have a lifecycle worth watching — Operating MAGE is that machinery. Neither is a fourth step alongside the three; both name conditions the same loop runs under.

THE MAGE CYCLE

Model the intent. Give that intent authority. Convert recurring failures into engineering capital.

Repeat.

None of these three verbs is invented here. Parts II and III earned each the hard way, one property of the substrate at a time: finite reasoning routed to Model, probabilistic action to Align, recurring failure to Convert. Part IV runs the three together.

Suppose you are beginning a new engineering effort. Not a legacy estate — a clean start, a prototype, a company that shipped its first commit last week. What does MAGE actually have you do on Monday morning? This chapter answers that, walking the cycle one turn at a time before the rest of the Part puts each move to work in earnest. The loop is the same whether you are migrating a hundred-thousand-line tangle or building on a blank page; only the starting rung differs. A brownfield estate starts you low on the capability ladder and asks you to climb from where you stand. Greenfield lets you start higher and stay ahead of the mess. Either way you run the same loop, and every turn of it leaves the environment more capable than before.

4.1.1 The loop, one turn at a time

Need. A turn begins with something you want to be true — a feature to ship, a property to guarantee, a class of bug to end. Name it plainly, because the name is what everything downstream binds to. The need is not "write this function"; it is the smaller, sharper claim underneath — this input yields that output, this invariant holds across every path, this mistake can never ship again. Most of the method is disciplined about answering one question: what is the cheapest representation that makes this need checkable?

How much of the need is actually known? Sometimes the input is already precise: a Jira ticket names the affected behavior, the acceptance criteria, and the constraints that must continue to hold. In that case, much of the specification work has already been done; the engineer can turn those claims into models and checks and proceed.

Other work begins with something much softer: a customer says that onboarding is confusing, an operator says the system "feels slow," or a product team suspects that a new workflow would be useful. Those are not defective specifications waiting for an agent to elaborate them. They are evidence that the problem itself is still being discovered. The first engineering task is therefore not to make the agent implement a more detailed version of the guess. It is to learn which claim is worth making precise.

MAGE does not require certainty before work begins. It requires honesty about where the uncertainty lives. Represent what is known strongly enough to preserve it; leave what is genuinely uncertain open to exploration. As evidence accumulates, promote the parts that stabilize into explicit requirements, models, invariants, and eventually mechanisms.

Be precise about what you know, experimental about what you don't, and convert learning into durable structure as knowledge stabilizes.

Model. Turn the need into an explicit model — a representation of the system's intent and structure that an agent can reason through, rather than intent left implicit in a few people's heads. This is the Modeling Thesis, the first move of every turn: make the thing you care about legible to a machine before you ask a machine to change it. You do not start by building a complete executable model. You start at the lowest rung that pays and strengthen the representation only when a recurring question earns the next one. On a brownfield estate the model you already own is the drifted wiki nobody quite trusts; Brownfield is the whole chapter on climbing from there. On a clean start the model is whatever you sketch first — a data-flow diagram, a typed contract, a property — and you refine it against the code as the code appears.

The same rule applies to uncertainty in the requirement. Do not turn a hypothesis into an invariant merely because a machine-readable invariant would be convenient. A model is valuable because it makes an engineering claim explicit; it is dangerous when it gives an unsettled claim false authority. If the customer need is still being discovered, model the part you actually know — the current behavior, the non-negotiable constraints, the measurements you will use to learn — and let the proposed behavior remain provisional.

Do not give uncertain intent premature authority.

Agent work. Sometimes the next step is not production implementation at all. When the need is uncertain, agentic implementation can be used as an instrument of discovery: build two cheap alternatives, instrument them, put them in front of users, or construct the smallest prototype that will falsify an assumption. Agents enable hyper-exploration of a design space: alternatives that once cost days or weeks to build can now be generated cheaply enough to compare before committing. Commodity intelligence makes this kind of exploration cheaper too. What matters is that exploratory code not quietly acquire the authority of a settled design. Prototype to learn; model and govern what you have learned.

Now the fleet writes the code. The model is what makes that delegable: an agent briefed with the represented intent produces work aimed at the need instead of vibe-coding toward a plausible shape. The discipline of how you package the method so a fresh agent applies it on reflex — the reasoning it walks, the checks it runs, the context it pulls — is itself an engineered artifact. The Skills shows the three the companion repository ships, and the portable architecture behind them: a body of knowledge plus a decision procedure an agent triggers and follows.

Validation. The environment now checks the work against the model — and keeps checking it, on every later change, without anyone remembering to. This is the Alignment Thesis: represented intent becomes authority the environment enforces, not a one-time review you trust and forget. The strongest form of the check does not merely reproduce a known-good dot; it generates evidence built to falsify the model's claim and lets through only what survives the search. Validating Change draws that family — property tests, fuzzing, and the synthesis of fuzzing with a structured model — as the two theses standing in one place.

Failure. The checks will catch things, and some things will slip past them and come back. This is not the loop breaking; it is the loop working. A contradiction between the model and the code means exactly one of three things — the model is wrong, the model is at the wrong grain, or the code has a bug — and every one of them is a real finding. The failures the environment caught while you slept are tomorrow's agenda, not an interruption to it.

Governance conversion. Here the cycle closes and compounds. When a failure recurs, you do not just fix the instance — you convert the class into a mechanism the environment applies for you, so a later agent meets the check instead of rediscovering the failure. A recurring rediscovery becomes a lint; a costly-and-frequent mistake becomes a structured model that makes it impossible; a convention that must hold becomes a blocking gate. The size of the mechanism is set by how much the failure costs times how often it happens. Closing the loop, later in this chapter, is the method for that move — how a recurrence is recognized, sized, and mechanized, and when a soft convention that aims earns promotion to a hard control that holds.

Repeat. Then the loop turns again, from firmer ground. Models grow richer, mechanisms grow stronger, and the environment accumulates engineering capital instead of paying the same reasoning costs over and over. Each day's judgment becomes the next day's infrastructure. That compounding is the point of running the loop rather than reaching for the moves ad hoc: a fix made once holds for every agent that follows, and the cost of the next change falls because the last one left a mechanism behind.

Figure 4.1-1 draws the shape the loop actually rides: precision is earned as knowledge stabilizes, not assumed at the start.

4.1.2 Closing the loop: governance conversion

When a check goes red, the reflex the method trains is not to patch the instance but to close the class it belongs to. The reason is plain: you cannot read every diff a fleet produces, so you govern the failures, not the steps. A conversion is that reflex run on purpose — it turns an ex-post failure into an ex-ante mechanism, in the same six steps every time, and naming them makes the move repeatable rather than lucky.

Those two words carry the whole procedure, so they are worth pinning down. Governance comes from two places. Some obligations you genuinely know from the start, and you should write every one down — this is specification-driven development, and where you can do it, you should. A coding style guide is the everyday case: every function carries a doc comment, nothing calls the shell outside this one approved place. Give the agent those rules, then write the deterministic check for each, or better, have the agent write the checker. That is ex-ante governance: decided before the work, cheap to encode once the rule is known.

But you will not know everything up front, and pretending otherwise is the mistake. You discover that performance is a problem and you have no performance model, that a model version has a tic and the tic moves when you upgrade. So you build governance ex-post: after the fact, iteratively, strapping on mechanisms as failures reveal themselves. Ex-post is not a fallback for a weak plan. It is where most of the real mechanisms come from, because most of the real failures are the ones no specification predicted. You set up what you can imagine in advance, launch the work, then watch it break and convert what broke — defining what you can up front, and staying willing to discover the rest. The six steps are that ex-post conversion, run on purpose.

  • Failure. A real breach — a check that lied, a silent data loss, a subsystem that looks right and is wrong across every file. The raw material is always a concrete thing that went wrong, not a hypothetical.
  • Diagnosis. Trace the breach to the point it actually violated. Not the seed input, not the one file — the rule underneath it. A crash traces to the point in the format specification the input broke; a bad merge traces to the decision no one made. Diagnose one level below the symptom.
  • Generalization. Ask what class this failure belongs to. One stringly-typed module is an instance; "the agent reads an under-specified instruction for the cheapest green checkmark" is the class. The mechanism you owe defends the class, so you have to name it.
  • Mechanism. Build the thing that makes the whole class impossible or caught — a type that removes the ambiguity, a lint that fires on every recurrence, a gate the agent cannot talk its way past, a decision rule you apply by reflex. Prefer the smallest mechanism that closes the class.
  • Retirement of the failure class. The mechanism now runs on every future agent, automatically, so the class stops recurring. The failure is not fixed once; it is retired — removed from the space of things anyone has to rediscover, until the mechanism is revised or itself retired.
  • Engineering capital. What you banked is not the patch. It is a standing constraint or sensor that holds the class closed for free, every time the code changes. Each conversion compounds: the environment gets a little more governed, and the next failure lands against a denser net.

Figure 4.1-2 draws the six steps as a loop — the same shape as the printer loop, escalated one level: judgment reads a recurrence as evidence of a missing mechanism and converts the class into a control the environment then enforces for free.

The loop reads the same at two scales. At the small grain, a recurring lint smell earns a blocking lint. At the large grain, an in-paradigm fix that keeps hitting the same wall is the signal to price a paradigm change — the recurrence is evidence about the ceiling, not about the fix. Both are the same move: a failure that comes back a second time has told you where a mechanism is owed, and the information was in the repetition, not the instance.

Not every conversion ends in a wall the environment enforces. Some bank a decision rule you now apply by hand — "no destructive default on a conflict," "when the cheap proxy is unsure, describe anyway." The output of the loop varies with the failure: a type, a lint, a gate, a standing policy, or a detector for a wrong frame. What does not vary is the discipline of spending the judgment once and encoding it, so neither you nor any later agent re-makes the call at midnight.

4.1.3 From soft to hard

The mechanisms a conversion produces sort along one axis: soft governance aims the agent, hard governance holds regardless of cooperation. A brief, a convention, a note in a doc — these aim; the agent can still miss or ignore them. A lint, a type, a gate — these hold; the agent cannot proceed through them uncooperatively. Both belong in a mature environment, and the skill the loop demands is reading which a given failure has earned.

The operational question is not what soft and hard mean; it is when a failure earns promotion from guidance to machinery. Not everything earns a wall — a low-cost, rarely-recurring failure can stay a note. But the moment you catch yourself saying "this must never happen," you have already specified a hard mechanism, and you should build it. A convention that says never is a convention that eventually gets violated at velocity. The cost of the failure, not its tidiness, decides how far right on the axis it belongs.

Hardness also assumes confidence in the obligation itself. "Never leak a credential" is stable enough to become a wall immediately. "Users prefer this workflow" usually is not. A hard mechanism gives a claim authority over every later change, so do not harden a product hypothesis merely because it is easy to encode. A premature gate can be worse than no gate: a poorly calibrated model given blocking authority converts uncertainty into outages. Where the desired behavior is still being learned, keep the mechanism soft, instrument the outcome, and let evidence sharpen the claim. Promote it when both the cost of violation and the stability of the obligation justify the wall.

The move that makes governance conversion compound is turning audits into lints. The first time a failure class appears, you inspect for it by hand — an audit, expensive and easy to forget. The signal that you owe a mechanism is the second time. A failure that recurs has earned its mechanism, and once you build it the same failure is caught on every future agent, automatically, until the mechanism is revised or retired. Today's audit finding is tomorrow's lint; a bug found in more than one place is not "fix N files" but "fix N files and add the check that keeps file N+1 from ever having it." That promotion — hand-inspection to standing sensor — is how a governed environment stops paying attention per instance and starts paying it once per class.

The case project runs this as a standing discipline. A defect caught once in a manual audit is encoded the same day as a check the environment enforces on every later agent, and the two commits — the fix and the lint that forbids its recurrence — ship together under one paired tag. That tag marks some two hundred commits, and the control substrate it grew is charted where the project's scale evidence lives: The Build counts its lint files and gate scripts climbing from zero, one class at a time. Every entry on that curve is a failure that will not return.

Run the loop long enough and the pattern behind it shows through: the discipline that pays is the one that converts judgment into infrastructure. Every decision you make well, you encode once — as a type, a lint, a gate, a rule — so you never make it again and neither does any agent after you. That is the whole method compressed to a sentence.

4.1.4 The daily practice

Put end to end, the cycle has a daily shape. You start the day by reading what broke — the failures the environment caught while you slept are the agenda. You spend the middle of it modeling: turning the spine of whatever you are building into something an agent can reason through. You end it by converting — the failure that recurred today earns its mechanism before tomorrow, so it never recurs again. Do that on a loop and the environment compounds: each day's judgment becomes the next day's infrastructure. The operator's version of this rhythm — the morning questions and the between-sessions reflex that fires them automatically — is the Daily Operator Review card.

Improving the process itself is engineering too — you optimize the product, and you optimize the process that makes it. Manufacturing folded that work into the line, and Toyota's production system named the moves: poka-yoke, mistake-proofing that shapes the work so a defect cannot happen; jidoka, stopping the line the instant a defect appears; and kaizen, the habit behind both — every recurring problem engineered out for good. Those name the manufacturing lineage of the moves this book already runs: the constraint, the sensor, and the conversion loop. Kaizen is the closest name for MAGE's own method — the improvement loop, run daily on the lifecycle itself, the same convert-when-it-recurs reflex this chapter turns on.

The instinct reaches past the factory floor. Resilience engineering treats an incident as a signal about the system, not a verdict on the operator 11. E. Hollnagel et al., Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts (Ashgate, 2006).; site reliability practice turns each outage into a blameless postmortem whose action items become durable work 22. B. Beyer et al., Site Reliability Engineering: How Google Runs Production Systems (O'Reilly Media, 2016).; Shingo built mistake-proofing into the fixture itself 33. Shigeo Shingo, Zero Quality Control: Source Inspection and the Poka-Yoke System, trans. Andrew P. Dillon (Productivity Press, 1986).. MAGE inherits that whole family. The changed ingredient is velocity: the fleet surfaces structural gaps fast enough that the environment accumulates reliability one class at a time.

The method is not tied to one product, and it did not come from a whiteboard. Every move in the cycle was forced by real systems under real deadlines — the one deep case this book induces from, and the industry teams that reached the same moves independently under other names. Once you can see the loop, you can ask which real environment already runs a version of it. Part VI's MAGE in the Wild sets six of them side by side — Siemens, Spotify, Shopify, Docker, Cloudflare, Zenseact — reconstructions that show the same moves recurring under other names. One of them makes the first move of the loop concrete.

Worked Examples

Siemens. Siemens is a model-first engineering organization: for decades its engineers have stated intent against explicit, owned system models — a digital thread that carries a design across mechanical, electrical, and software domains — instead of leaving it implicit in code and documents. The consequential decision routes through the model, and a deterministic solver, not a person's recollection, returns the verdict. The move a software team can copy is that routing — put the thing you care about into a model you own, then let a deterministic check answer against it.

Takeaway. Models-as-working-language is neither exotic nor new; a model-first discipline has run it at industrial scale for decades. What MAGE adds is carrying that discipline into software a fleet writes, where the model an agent reasons through is what makes the work checkable at all.

4.1.5 One-shot or supervised?

Not every task the fleet touches needs the loop this chapter runs. Some a capable model simply does: describe the input and the output, press the button, and a working program comes back on the first pass — no iteration, no governance, no loop to manage. Knowing which tasks are like that, and which are not, is the first judgment an agentic engineer makes, and it is the judgment that decides whether the rest of this Part even applies.

The two modes

Start at the decision on the left. You have a computational task and you can say what it takes in and what it must produce. From there the path forks on the task's reasoning horizon — the intermediate reasoning state it must hold at once, derived in Part I's The Reasoning Horizon. A long-horizon task is one whose reasoning state will not fit losslessly within the working memory available to hold it.

One-shot scripting is the righthand branch. A task belongs here when its relevant reasoning state fits comfortably in one pass and the result is cheap to inspect — a few thousand lines is a rough indicator, not the rule. Think of a CSV-to-HTML report script. You specify the behavior once, the agent writes it in a single pass, and you read the result. Its reasoning horizon is short — the whole transaction fits in one context window and one sitting. When a task really is this size, the full apparatus of governed autonomy is wasted effort. Send it one-shot and move on.

Supervised autonomy is the downward branch — the method for a long-horizon task, and where most of this book lives. Its reasoning state is too large to hold in a single context window, or to trust to a single pass, so you supply four things in two groups. The system side shapes what gets built: correctness conditions — what a right answer looks like — and sub-tasks — the breakdown that divides the work. The development side shapes how the building runs: tools that determinize recurring processes, so the same operation runs the same way every time, and monitoring and intervention, so you can watch the work and step in when it drifts.

Where the emphasis falls

That fork runs along the reasoning horizon. Writing a shell script, proving a theorem, debugging a deadlock, and re-architecting a subsystem sit at very different points on it, and the horizon is a property of the task, not of who does the reasoning — a human, an agent, or some later machine. The longer the horizon, the more the work has to be supervised rather than one-shot.

This book covers both modes. It has to, because the first skill is telling them apart, and getting that wrong in either direction is expensive. Treat a one-shot task as if it needed supervised autonomy and you drown a five-minute script in ceremony. Treat a supervised-autonomy task as if it were one-shot and you get confident, plausible, subtly wrong output at a scale no human can read.

Breaking the work into smaller, checked steps is how you pull a long horizon back within reach: each step holds less reasoning state at once. But modularity reduces the reasoning horizon; it does not eliminate it. The pieces still have to compose, and holding how they compose is itself reasoning state. The craft is in cutting the task so the state any one step carries stays inside what the reasoner can hold.

The weight of the method is on the downward branch. The interesting engineering, the part that did not exist a few years ago and is not yet written down, is supervised autonomy: building larger-scale products whose quality needs exceed what a model can be trusted to do alone, and whose size exceeds current and foreseeable context windows. That is the regime where you stop pressing go on a script and start running a printer, framing the goal, modeling the world for the machine, and putting every change on rails from dispatch to production.

How short a leash a given task can run on is the sizing question the next section makes precise.

4.1.6 Task sizing

The unit of work you hand an agent should be large enough to exploit the model's capabilities and small enough that its result can be meaningfully evaluated. Frame each task as a transformation — an explicit input, an explicit output, and a checkable step between them — and size that step to the model and to the guarantee you need. Speak at too low a level and you have written the code yourself; ask for too large a leap and you get back something plausible you cannot check. A stronger model supports a bigger leap, but task fit and representation often matter more than raw strength. How long a leash a task can run on comes down to two things: how densely the model's training data covers the kind of work you are asking for, and how capable the model is at it. The denser the coverage and the stronger the model, the more briefly a task can be described and the farther it can be trusted; the nearer the task sits to the edge of what the model has seen, the more the reasoning must be broken into smaller, checked steps.

Keep the leaps short, because independent per-step risk compounds. A long, unaudited chain of edits accumulates error faster than intuition expects, and a failure buried in the middle of one is the hardest kind to localize. So put a checkable boundary at every seam. Size each transformation small enough that its result can be inspected as it lands, not gathered up and hoped over at the very end — a short transformation is not merely less likely to fail; it is a place you can check, and the check is what caps the risk.

DocAble sizes this deliberately. The remediation core could ask a strong model for the whole leap — here is a document, hand back an accessible one. A capable model can sometimes do exactly that. But the call reasons over the entire document every time, which is slow and expensive, and the result is unauditable: you cannot see which change it made, or why. So the system sizes the leap down. It scopes the work to a closed vocabulary of small typed edits — set the alt text on this image, set the role of this block, reorder these children — the same edit language the interactive editor speaks in Part V. The agent is asked for one bounded edit at a time. Each call reasons over one scoped task instead of the whole document, and each edit comes back typed, stamped, and reversible. Same fleet, same model; a different task size turns an opaque rewrite into a stream of edits you can check.

Learn more about this governance mechanism: remediation verbs.

Sizing is only half of it. The transformation also has to be the right shape for the task and the model, and I learned that the expensive way. For two weeks I sized a transformation beautifully and pointed it at the wrong problem: I pulled a PDF's reading order out of the geometry of its bounding boxes and handed that geometry to cheap vision models, which are bad at geometry. The model cheerfully made a doomed shape better and better. A well-sized transformation of the wrong shape is still the wrong transformation. So the skill has two halves — size the leap, and find the one leap that fits both your task and your model, then abandon the one that does not.

4.1.7 What the rest of this Part does

The chapters ahead take the loop off the page and run it, and each runs a conversion at a different scale. Migrating to MAGE starts where most readers actually stand — a legacy estate and a drifted wiki — and climbs the capability ladder one rung at a time: adopting the method on a brownfield tree is conversion at the scale of the whole estate. Validating Change holds the result to a model built to be broken, so its generative checks become conversions worn as tests. The Skills packages the method so a fresh agent applies it without being re-taught — conversions packaged to travel. Operating MAGE keeps the whole thing running once it has a lifecycle worth watching. A short closing then strips the case away and leaves the handful of moves you can run on a codebase this book has never seen. You already have the mindset, the governed environment, and the models; the rest of this Part puts them to work.
