# How to Read This Book

This front matter is reference material. You do not need to understand it before beginning the book.

**First time here?** Read the Preface and continue into {{chapter:problem}}. You may skip the rest of this front matter for now; it is designed to become more useful as the terminology and argument become familiar.

**Returning to the book?** Use *What This Book Argues* for the six central claims, *MAGE on One Page* for the relationship among the major concepts, and the *Glossary* for definitions.

The Preface asks one question: *How do we safely grant autonomy to commodity intelligence?* {{chapter:problem}} frames the resulting engineering problem. Chapters {{chapter:modeling|num}}–{{chapter:evidence|num}} then develop MAGE through software engineering: they show the mechanisms in a worked domain and provide the evidence from which the broader account is drawn. {{chapter:theory}} abstracts that experience into a theory; {{chapter:profession}} asks what follows for engineering more broadly. The Conclusion returns to the opening question and asks its converse: *What cannot be delegated?* [ref:book-map] traces that argument.

The argument and the case can also be read at different depths. Readers primarily interested in MAGE as an engineering argument can follow the Preface → {{chapter:problem}} → {{chapter:theory}} → {{chapter:profession}} → Conclusion. That route gives the problem, theory, broader implications, and closing claim. Chapters {{chapter:modeling|num}}–{{chapter:evidence|num}} are what substantiate that argument: they develop MAGE through software engineering, show its mechanisms at engineering resolution, and provide the evidence from which the theory is drawn. Read them straight through for the full case, or return to them when you want to see where a theoretical claim came from.

Coming from another engineering discipline—or from work that may be engineering in the functional sense used here? Start with the Preface, {{chapter:problem}}, and {{sec:agentic-engineering-beyond-software}}. From there, {{chapter:theory}} gives the theory, while Chapters {{chapter:modeling|num}}–{{chapter:evidence|num}} provide the software-engineering case from which it was developed. Circle back as the mechanisms become relevant to your own work.

Three shorter routes serve different purposes:

- **Do it Monday.** {{chapter:problem}} → {{chapter:method}} → Appendix Part I: Practice. Start with the premise, move directly to the working method, then use the practice appendices as a reference while applying it.
- **Show me the data.** {{chapter:problem}} → {{chapter:evidence}} → Appendix Part II: Evidence. Read the framing, examine the empirical argument, then inspect its evidentiary basis.
- **Give me the engineering argument.** Preface → {{chapter:problem}} → {{chapter:theory}} → {{chapter:profession}} → the Conclusion. Read the problem, the theory, where it transfers beyond software engineering, and the responsibility that closes the argument. Chapters {{chapter:modeling|num}}–{{chapter:evidence|num}} remain the evidentiary foundation; return to them to see where a theoretical claim came from.
- **Adopt it in an organization.** {{chapter:problem}} → {{sec:mage-in-the-wild}} → [appendix: appendix-adopting-genai]. Start with the changed engineering problem, see how several organizations are already restructuring agentic work, then use the adoption guide to decide where context, models, evidence, and enforcement are worth organizational investment. Chapters {{chapter:modeling|num}}–{{chapter:method|num}} provide the engineering practices; {{chapter:theory}} provides the underlying theory.
