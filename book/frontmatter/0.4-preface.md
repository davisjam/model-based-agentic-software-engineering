# Preface

<!-- web-only: *Prefer to read offline? [Download the PDF](mage-book.pdf).* -->

**How do we safely grant autonomy to commodity intelligence?**

Everything in this book is an answer to that question. Not just software. Not just coding
agents. MAGE is one answer.

In this book, that question appears first as software engineering: when implementation becomes
cheap enough that agents can build almost anything you can describe, what must the surrounding
engineering environment do so they can act autonomously without making the system untrustworthy?
The problem is autonomy, not code generation. The answer this book develops has two parts and a
loop: make intent explicit in models the fleet reasons through, make the environment enforce the
obligations that matter, and, when autonomy exposes a failure class nobody foresaw, convert that
lesson into durable structure, so the next agent inherits a safer, more capable world. That the
intelligence is now a *commodity*, abundant and cheap and general, is why the frame holds. As
intelligence stops being the scarce thing, engineering reorganizes around what still is:
judgment, representations, evidence, authority, and governance.

> **Why MAGE?**
>
> Nearly every mature engineering organization now reports independently discovering some
> combination of structured representations, automated enforcement, deterministic validation,
> and environmental governance. MAGE's contribution is not to recommend these practices. It
> explains how they fit together, gives them a common vocabulary, and names the mechanisms and
> design spaces not yet explicit in those individual reports.

Those reports describe successful engineering practices; MAGE attempts something different. It tries
to extract a coherent engineering theory from them — to relate the practices to one another, explain
why they work, and name the engineering design spaces the individual reports leave implicit. That is
worth doing because engineering advances when recurring structures become nameable. Once a failure
pattern, an organizational investment, or a design tradeoff carries a shared name, engineers can
compare systems, weigh alternatives, and deliberately improve them, instead of rediscovering the same
ideas independently.

This is a book about engineering — specifically, about what engineering work becomes when
writing the code stops being the hard part. For most of the history of software, the scarce
resource was implementation: someone had to sit down and type the thing into existence, and
that typing was the bottleneck everything else queued behind. Coding agents have moved that
bottleneck. Code is now cheap to produce and fast to change. What is not cheap is judging,
constraining, validating, and maintaining it — deciding what to build, catching where fast
code went wrong, and keeping a growing system trustworthy while it changes under you every
day. That shift is the subject of this book.

This book began as an attempt to solve one hard engineering problem, watched closely over months
of building a real production system. The theory emerged from that one deeply studied system. The
order was deliberate. I did not begin by surveying what other teams had built and distilling the
common themes; I built and studied one system deeply enough to propose a theory, then asked whether
it held up against independent industrial practice. As
the manuscript developed, I turned its vocabulary outward, interpreting it against a growing body
of independent engineering reports from organizations including Cloudflare, Docker, Shopify,
Spotify, Siemens, and Zenseact. Those reports are not only corroboration. They are independent
engineering systems against which the theory is tested — counterexamples as much as agreement — and
across the book it is challenged against them, refined where they press on it, and occasionally
extended to hold. They frequently
converge on similar mechanisms, while revealing where MAGE supplies vocabulary and explanatory
structure not explicit in the original reports. What emerges is not a common implementation. It is
a recurring engineering grammar beneath very different systems. [ref:research-arc] traces that path
from the one case to the theory the closing chapters now carry.

This book ultimately argues something larger than a lesson about documentation:
software engineering is a long-horizon reasoning task. Coding agents make
implementation cheap, but they do not remove the finite reasoning capacity a growing
system outruns. So the central role of a model is not to record the system after the
fact; it is to extend how much of that system a human and an agent can hold in view and
reason about coherently. What follows earns that claim; here it is only the direction
the book heads.

## What changed

<!-- index-def: fleet -->
<!-- gloss: Fleet | The set of coding agents working the codebase — the agentic-era workforce this book governs, in place of a team of human engineers. -->

<!-- gloss: Lint | An automated check that scans code for a banned pattern and fails the commit when it finds one, so a rule holds without a human remembering it. -->

<!-- gloss: Gate | A check placed across a pipeline step — a commit, a deploy — that refuses to let the step through until its condition is met. -->

Scaling a fleet hits a wall, the way scaling a team does — but a different one. Add people and you
eventually meet the coordination problem Brooks described: potential communication paths grow roughly
with the square of the team, and the coordination overhead eats each new hire's gain. A fleet does not
drown in that circle. It hits a
different bound. A fleet is limited by how well its models reason and how much they can hold in view,
and when it reaches that bound, ungoverned, it decays into churn. Context pressure is the sharpest
driver: when the work outgrows what the fleet can hold in view, the agent loses the thread, re-derives
what it already built, and confidently undoes yesterday's fix. The wall is churn.

Churn's causes are three things the
fleet does not know. It does not know *what* to build: the requirements are ambiguous. It
does not know *how* to realize the change: the architecture has drifted into a soup no
context window can hold. And it does not know how to change the system *without breaking
it*: every edit lands blind on invariants nobody wrote down. Every technique here attacks a
cause, not the symptom. Models attack the first two not-knowings — they bind intent to
structure and hold the architecture in a compact form, so the agent knows what to build and
the work fits in-window. And in doing so they leave something governance can use: a
representation explicit enough to check a change against. Governance attacks the third
not-knowing on that surface — it keeps a change from introducing errors in the first place, or
makes them visible the moment it does.

<!-- index-def: churn -->
> **Churn.** The condition in which an increasing share of agent effort is spent
> rediscovering context, undoing recent changes, repairing regressions, or reconciling
> inconsistencies rather than advancing the system. It is the agent-fleet form of velocity
> decay: fixing crowds out building.

The stakes here are **tokenomics**, not just tidiness. Keeping a task inside the window is far cheaper
than letting it spill, and the gap compounds: I once ran four Claude Max subscriptions flat out to keep
the fleet fed, and the same velocity now barely fills two — because a model lets each agent find what it
needs instead of re-deriving it, and keeps the work in-window, off the cliff where the token bill jumps.
The point of MAGE is not only to spend less effort fighting churn; it is to make engineering effort
*compound* — each conversion banked as capability the next task inherits, the positive dual of churn the
closing theory names.

<!-- gloss: Tokenomics | How many tokens it takes to get a task done — and it is not linear. Overflow the context window and the cost jumps: the harness compacts (a lossy save), and the agent then burns tokens rebuilding the context it lost. So keeping a task inside the window is not merely tidier, it is far cheaper — which is exactly what a model buys, by shrinking what the agent must hold. -->

## Why I built a production system to study this

My name is James Davis. I am a professor of software engineering in the Elmore Family
School of Electrical and Computer Engineering at Purdue University. My job is to find out
things other people do not know and write them down so they do. This book records one of those
findings. Over a stretch of a few months I built a real production system — a
document-accessibility service, [DocAble]({{product_url}}) — almost entirely by dispatching
AI coding agents rather than writing code myself. I watched what that mode of work demanded,
and this book is what I learned about doing it well. DocAble is the running example
throughout; the claims here rest on that real production system. A first concrete look at it
comes early, in [MAGE by Example](1.2-mage-by-example.html); the full description lives at
the back, in {{part:5}}.

Building DocAble was not only a way to ship a product. It was a way to ask a question that is
hard to ask from the outside. Once agents write almost all of the code, what is the
engineering that remains? The question is no longer whether they can produce useful code. They
can. It is what is left for a human to do when they produce nearly all of it.

That question resists the usual instruments. A benchmark isolates one capability and scores
it. A build report tells you a fleet reproduced a framework, compiled a kernel, or migrated a
codebase in a week. A set of interviews tells you how developers supervise the tools they were
handed. Each is a real piece, and none of them shows how the pieces move together over the
life of a new production system: how an architectural mistake hardens into a lint, how a
failure seen twice becomes a gate, how one authored model changes what an agent can do, how
the controls pile up until the environment can be trusted to run itself. You cannot watch that
from a scorecard or a single interview. You have to build the system, hold it to a standard
someone would actually rely on, and watch it over months.

So that is the design of the study. I built something whose quality mattered, let the fleet do
nearly all of the implementation, and treated every failure as evidence about the method
rather than noise to clean up. The book's vocabulary — governance conversion, the Modeling and
Alignment theses, constraints and sensors, the governed engineering environment — names
structures that recurred often enough to earn names. I offer them as working concepts, precise
enough to compare, argue with, and replace, not as the field's settled terms. Where my
investigation extends the existing literature, I have tried to cite the work it stands on, so a
reader can see where this case joins the conversation and where it departs.

Read the book in that spirit. One deeply worked case cannot prove that every project will
behave like this one. What it can do is open a single machine far enough to say what the rest
of us should look for.

## Why you can trust what follows

I want to be plain about what this book is and is not. It is not a memoir of the experience,
though the experience was strange enough to warrant one. It is not a survey of every AI
coding tool, which would be stale before it printed. It is a field report and a method — [+M+]odel-Based [+Ag+]entic Software [+E+]ngineering, or MAGE: the
concrete engineering practices that turn a fast code fabricator into a production line you
can trust. Where a claim rests on a number, I give the number. Where a practice was learned
by getting it wrong first, I say so.

One caution belongs up front, because it colors everything after it. This is a single-case
field report: one production system, built by one engineer, watched over a few months. Where I
give a number, read it as an observation from that case rather than a measured law. But this case joins a conversation already
underway. Practitioner reports agree, in volume, that these practices work with agents: write
far more tests than you would for a human team, specify before you build, make the
environment hold the line. Those reports supply the *what*. What the conversation lacks is the
*how* and the *why*: the mechanism behind the practices, worked out in enough detail to
transfer. A deep case you control end to end is the right
instrument for mechanism — I have built the machine, watched it run, and seen when it worked
and how it failed.

A word about where the evidence comes from. Everything in this book rests on one system:
DocAble, authored by me inside a single vendor's agent ecosystem, deployed and in daily use
by me and my colleagues at Purdue. It is one deep case rather than a broad sample, and every
claim stays scoped to it; the closing limitations set out exactly how far that reaches. The
book also holds itself to the method it teaches. Its empirical claims are not loose lines in

## What the method turned out to be

Two theses drive this book, and they run through every chapter that follows. They do not run
side by side. The first makes engineering intent explicit; the second gives that explicit
intent authority, and it can only do so because the first has built something for it to act on.

<!-- index-def: thesis-modeling -->
> **The Modeling Thesis.** Externalize engineering knowledge and intent into explicit, structured
> representations that give commodity intelligence a compact world to reason through and give
> engineers a surface on which to specify, analyze, and predict the system. As those representations
> grow richer — from selected context, to connected knowledge, to invariant-bearing executable
> models — they make progressively more engineering questions tractable without returning to raw
> implementation.
>
> **In other words:** don't make every agent rediscover your architecture from raw code — move the
> engineering knowledge out of people's heads and into representations the next engineer, and the
> next agent, can start from.
>
> **Why it matters:** every fact you externalize is one an agent stops paying to rediscover on each
> task, and one the environment can later be taught to check.

<!-- index-def: thesis-alignment -->
> **The Alignment Thesis.** Give engineering intent authority over autonomous work by encoding
> obligations into deterministic mechanisms that constrain action, observe violations, evaluate
> evidence, and control admission. Where the Modeling Thesis makes intent explicit, the Alignment
> Thesis makes that intent binding on what the fleet can do and what the environment will accept.
>
> **In other words:** once you've decided how the system should behave, stop asking every future
> agent to remember that decision — teach the environment to enforce it instead.
>
> **Why it matters:** every decision that becomes enforceable is one less decision every future
> agent must rediscover; authority, not memory, carries the policy forward.

The relationship between the two is where the method actually lives.

> **Modeling makes intent explicit; Alignment makes it binding.**
>
> **In other words:** first write down what matters in a form both people and machines can
> understand; then teach the engineering environment to insist on it.

The theses are causally linked. Modeling turns implicit engineering knowledge into a
representation both humans and machines can reason over; Alignment attaches authority to that
representation. The richer the model, the more obligations move from probabilistic judgment into
deterministic enforcement. So Modeling does not merely sit beside Alignment. It creates the
surfaces Alignment acts on, and Alignment in turn keeps the models and the system from silently
diverging.

That makes the dependency real, and worth stating plainly. Alignment depends on Modeling;
Modeling does not depend on Alignment. It is a dependency, not a disappearance: both theses
survive on their own, which is why there are still two. A model with no mechanism attached still
earns its keep, the way a service catalog helps an agent navigate a system whose invariants no
one has written down. A mechanism with little modeling behind it earns its keep too, the way a
sandbox can run an untrusted change against tests without any rich model of what the change
means. MAGE's strongest form joins them, but neither collapses into the other, and not every
guardrail needs a model standing behind it.

The order of the two working parts follows that dependency. Each takes one thesis: Part 2
develops Modeling, the surfaces; Part 3 develops Alignment, the authority. You can only make
binding what you have first made explicit. [ref:mage-overview] draws the whole method in one
picture.

<!-- figure: assets/mage-overview.svg | The MAGE method in one picture. A cheap agent fleet, left ungoverned, drifts into churn as its work outruns the window. Run through the governed environment, it converges on trustworthy software at velocity. Read the environment as one spine, not two divided halves: the Modeling Thesis gives the intelligence a world to reason through — the capability ladder folded inline, context through invariant — and *creates surfaces for* the Alignment Thesis, which gives that intent authority by constraining, sensing, validating, and gating. Read top to bottom, the whole argument is a single chain:

> Commodity intelligence creates a reasoning problem.
> Context engineering begins the response.
> Durable context becomes structured knowledge.
> Structured knowledge becomes engineering models.
> Models make intent explicit.
> Explicit intent makes more judgment mechanizable.
> Alignment gives that intent authority.
> Failures enrich the representations and controls.
> The environment compounds.
> Autonomy scales.
>
> That is MAGE.

That chain is the reorganization the opening named — judgment, representations, evidence,
authority, and governance — assembled into one causal line rather than a list.

<!-- index-def: universal-language -->
Hold both theses at once and a larger claim comes into view — the one this whole book argues
toward. **Models are the universal language of engineering.** Every mature engineering
discipline reasons about the system it builds through models, not through the raw artifact:
a load diagram, a circuit, a control loop. Software could rarely afford to, because keeping
the model equal to fast-moving code cost a person's standing labor. Agents cut that recurring
cost sharply. They make the code cheap and run the drift checks that keep the map in sync for
almost nothing (someone still authors the model and the gate), and that shift relocates
the engineer's job upward — to the essential part it could never fully reach before: deciding
what the system is *for*, and authoring the models whose properties drive the tradeoffs. In the
system that underpins this book, the code stopped being the bottleneck; the model became the
work. For software engineering, models are an idea whose time has come.

And a model cheap enough to keep true stops being a picture of the system and starts running it. The
fleet reads its models at commit time to grade its own gates, and at measurement time to shape its own
metrics. The map no longer merely stays equal to the territory; it steers the machinery that keeps it
equal.

## Three ways to run a fleet

The hard problem of building software with agents is simple to state: the tools are fast
and the tools are unreliable. A coding agent can produce more change in an afternoon than a
team used to produce in a week, and some fraction of that change is confidently, plausibly
wrong. Every account of how to work this way is really an answer to one question — where
does the reliability come from? Three answers dominate. [ref:oversight-modes] sets the three side by side.

<!-- label: oversight-modes -->
<!-- figure: assets/oversight-modes.svg | Three process models for agentic software engineering. Velocity-centric rings job titles around the work — planner, developer, tester, reviewer — while the quality mechanism stays implicit, and implicit quality does not survive volume. Oversight-centric straddles a human over each bounded piece of work; it is honest about the failure mode, but human attention does not scale with the fleet, so the reviewer becomes the bottleneck. Governance-centric is the synthesis and the book's choice: agents work fast inside mechanisms the environment enforces, set up once, so reliability becomes a property of the ground they stand on rather than a toll paid per change. The first two are the two ends of one dial; governance steps off the dial entirely. -->

**The first answer is velocity.** Give the agents autonomy, and organize them the way you
would organize people: a planner hands work to a developer, the developer to a tester, the
tester to a reviewer, and around the loop it goes. Assigning agents job titles makes the
work look like engineering, and the throughput is real. But process resemblance is not
control. A ring of role-labeled agents can imitate the *form* of software work — the
handoffs, the review step, the sign-off — without any of it actually catching a bad change.
The quality mechanism is left implicit, and implicit quality does not survive contact with
volume.

**The second answer is oversight.** Treat the agent as a useful but untrustworthy
assistant, and keep a human next to the implementation loop. You prompt a bounded piece of
work, the agent does it, you read the result, you prompt the next piece. This is honest
about the failure mode, since agent output does need inspection, and for a while it works well.
Its limit is arithmetic. The control is human attention, and attention does not scale with
the agents. Turn the volume up and the human becomes the bottleneck: the fleet produces
changes faster than any person can read them, and the reading is now the slow step in the
whole line.

Field work fills in what that oversight actually involves. Interviews with seventeen
experienced developers working alongside coding agents identify four recurring forms — a
priori control, co-planning, real-time monitoring, and post hoc review — and find that
agent-written code is often hard to inspect directly, so developers lean on tests and other
indirect evidence to judge it [cite: dhanorkar2026oversight]. But naming the work does not
repeal its arithmetic: every one of those forms still spends human attention per change.

**The third answer is governance.** Build the environment before the agents work in it.
Decide the obligations up front (what must never happen, what must always hold), and encode
each one as a mechanism the environment enforces: a type the compiler checks, a lint that
blocks a commit, a gate that refuses a bad deploy. The human hands the environment its
policies; the environment holds the agents to them. Quality stops depending on a tired
reviewer noticing, and starts being a property of the ground the agents stand on.

These three stances are the landmarks of a live debate, and it helps to place the names on
them. At the velocity end sits *vibe coding*, the term Andrej [Karpathy coined](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1886192184808149383)
for prompting an agent and accepting what looks right — Steve Yegge's [Gas Town](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/welcome-to-gas-town-4f25ee16dd04)
is the same spirit written large. At the far end sits the rigor of formal verification, argued
by Bertrand Meyer in *From Probable to Provable* [cite: meyer-probable]
and pursued by the emerging vibe-OS and vibe-tools work on formal tooling for AI-written code.
This book stands between them — closer in temperament to the rigorous end, but reaching its
assurance by a different route than a proof.

Notice what the first two answers have in common: they are the two ends of one axis. Slide
the dial toward velocity and you buy speed by spending reliability; slide it toward oversight
and you buy reliability by spending speed. Every point on that line charges the same toll:
per-change human attention, whether the human is coaxing the output at the fast end or
inspecting it at the slow end. Call it the *pet tax* — the price of caring for each change by
hand. It grows with the size of the fleet until the human becomes the bottleneck no matter
which way the dial is set.

Governance is not a point on that line. It is a different move. Instead of choosing where
to sit between speed and safety, you change what the reliability is made of: you build the
fences and chutes once, up front, and let the guardrails ride every change on their own. The
old distinction between *pets* and *cattle* names the shift exactly. A pet server is nursed
by hand, one at a time; a herd of cattle is run by the fences and the routine, not by
attention paid to each animal. Governed agents are cattle. You stop paying attention
per-change and start paying it per-*class-of-failure* — once, when you build the mechanism that
retires the class. The dial-trading stops, because you are no longer on the dial.

This book takes the third stance, and then pushes past where the current accounts stop. The
governance literature starts from obligations you know in advance (a regulation, an access
policy, a rule the organization already wrote down) and asks what mechanism would enforce it.
That is the easy half. The hard half, the half I lived, is the obligations you *cannot* know
in advance: the ones that stay invisible until an agent, moving fast, trips over them and
breaks something. The engineering that matters is what you do next — reading that failure as
evidence of a missing mechanism, and encoding it so the whole class of failure cannot happen
again. Velocity is what exposes the missing mechanism. Judgment is what converts it into a
guardrail. That conversion, done over and over until the environment can be trusted to run
itself, is the engineering this book is about.

## The through-line, and who it is for

The through-line is a single idea; hold every chapter against it. **When you build with an
agent, you are not managing a coder; you are running a printer.** A 3D printer builds almost anything you can describe, but only what you
describe — hand it a bad model and you get a bad part. An agent is the same. It builds most of what you can explain to it, and the
entire discipline of this book lives in that "so long as you can
explain it." Explaining a system to a probabilistic machine well enough that it builds the
right thing, at speed, without a human reading every line — that is a real engineering craft,
not a knack, and it can be taught.

This book is for the software engineers who suspect the ground has moved and want to know
where to stand. It is also for the technical leads, architects, and engineering managers who must
decide how autonomous software development should work inside their organizations. Governing a fleet
is no longer only an individual's craft; it is an organizational choice, and the closing part turns
the method outward to that scale — a way to think about an engineering organization, not only an
engineer. On the surface it is about a tool I built to help people with disabilities
access documents. Underneath, it is about a working method for building anything with agents:
how to frame the problem, model the world for the machine, put every change on rails from
dispatch to production, and convert each failure you hit into a guardrail that stops the
next one. You will not need my domain to use any of it. You will need a codebase, an agent,
and the willingness to treat the instructions as the job.

After reading it, you should be able to recognize a recurring governance problem when it surfaces,
reason about the alternative engineering environments that might answer it, and deliberately design
mechanisms that fit your organization rather than copying another company's process. That is the
whole promise, and the rest of the book is one move toward it, repeated.

> Everything that follows is one move, repeated: convert judgment into infrastructure.

The DevOps era learned to carry infrastructure, policy, and configuration *as code*. This book
carries the next thing: **judgment, as code** — more generally, judgment as mechanism, since much of
what follows is models, constraints, validators, and workflows rather than source. The phrase is a
slogan, not a literal claim; the glossary makes it exact.
