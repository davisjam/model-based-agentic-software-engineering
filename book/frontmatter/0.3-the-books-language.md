# The Book's Language

## Glossary

Do not memorize this vocabulary. This glossary is a map to return to as the terms acquire meaning in the chapters ahead.

Agentic software engineering is a fast-evolving topic, and it seems that many concepts have been given overlapping or contradictory names.
Part of the purpose of this book is to articulate coherent vocabulary for the field.
The glossary is divided into three parts: the core ideas of the book, the vocabulary for coding agents and their accompanying phenomena, and the terms for the governed engineering environment that stabilizes them.

## The core ideas

The ideas the whole argument turns on — the two theses, the conversion that grows the environment, the governing metaphor, and the vocabulary of models underneath them.

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — glossary section-map, orientation reference chrome not a numbered cross-ref float -->

<!-- figure: assets/gloss-core-ideas.svg | The core ideas. Commodity intelligence changes the economics of engineering; Modeling and Alignment address different constraints within that new economy, while governance conversion makes their lessons durable. -->

<!-- index-def: commodity-intelligence -->
**Commodity intelligence.** General-purpose machine intelligence available at sufficiently low cost and high capacity that implementation effort becomes less scarce relative to engineering judgment. The term describes an economic change, not a claim that intelligence is free, unlimited, or itself sufficient for engineering.

**Governance.** The engineering discipline of turning consequential judgment into durable structure that autonomous work inherits. In MAGE, that structure includes models, constraints, validators, policies, workflows, and other machine-interpretable or executable artifacts; governance is not synonymous with management or compliance.

**Engineering, as used here.** Designing under goals, constraints, and trade-offs by reasoning about the properties alternatives are expected to produce and the evidence that supports those expectations.

**Software engineering, as used here.** Engineering applied to software: designing and evolving software systems by reasoning about intended properties, constraints, evidence, and trade-offs rather than relying on the implementation alone to reveal them.

**Model.** A purposeful reduction of a system that preserves the information needed to answer an engineering question while suppressing detail the question does not need. Models externalize engineering knowledge and intent so that engineers and agents can reason over selected system properties without repeatedly reconstructing them from lower-level artifacts.

**Structured.** Represented in a form whose elements and relationships can be addressed reliably by machines rather than recovered from prose or transient reasoning. Structure makes a representation queryable, joinable, and available to mechanical analysis. Whether its semantics support deterministic judgment or authority is a separate question.

**Descriptive vs. prescriptive.** Two things a model's facts can do. A *descriptive* fact records what the system currently is — a service exists, a call is made. A *prescriptive* (intent-bearing, blueprint) fact declares what is allowed or required — only this class may consume that queue. Descriptive facts make current reality explicit. Prescriptive facts make intended reality explicit; the more precisely that intent is represented, the more of it becomes available to deterministic analysis and enforcement.

<!-- index-def: model-classes -->
**Model classes.** A working ontology for models, organized by the engineering questions they help answer. MAGE uses six recurring classes. The classes are useful rather than exhaustive or mutually exclusive: one representation may participate in several classes, and a class does not prescribe a particular notation or implementation form. The class suggests the questions to ask and the relationships worth preserving. It does not prescribe one notation, and a useful model may cross class boundaries.

- **Structural model.** Represents what exists and how parts relate: components, boundaries, dependencies, interfaces, zones, and other system structure.
- **Behavioral model.** Represents what may happen and in what order: states, transitions, sequences, protocols, and other behavior over time.
- **Ownership model.** Represents who or what controls a resource, responsibility, or unit of work, including how that control is acquired, transferred, released, or expires.
- **Decision model.** Represents choices, permissions, policies, or relationships that determine what actions are allowed or appropriate under specified conditions.
- **Measurement model.** Represents quantities and their relationships to expectations, bounds, or operating envelopes: cost, latency, capacity, quality, resource consumption, and similar properties.
- **Provenance model.** Represents consequential history: what happened, to what, by whom or what, under which operation or pass, and with what supporting evidence.

<!-- index-def: system-knowledge -->
**System knowledge.** The connected body of explicit engineering knowledge available through a system's models. Different model classes may use different representation forms while sharing identities, traceability, derivations, and query surfaces. MAGE does not require them to form one universal model.

<!-- index-def: representation-form -->
**Representation form.** The concrete form used to encode a model, such as a graph, registry, state machine, table, typed contract, or quantitative relation. Representation form is distinct from model class: the engineering question determines the model's purpose, while several forms may be suitable for representing it.

Governance is not a seventh class. It names an *action on* a model — the Move axis, whether a mechanism constrains, senses, or packages — not a kind of representation. A governance mechanism governs a Decision model or a Structural one; it is not itself a model class.

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — glossary Model-entry reference map, not a cross-referenced numbered float -->

<!-- figure: assets/model-classes.svg | The six model classes, by the engineering question each answers. Governance is a Move, not a class. -->

**Modeling Thesis.** Externalize engineering knowledge and intent into explicit, purposeful models so humans and agents can reason over larger engineering questions without reconstructing the relevant facts from lower-level detail.

**Alignment Thesis.** Encode engineering obligations into authoritative mechanisms that constrain actions, produce evidence, validate that evidence, and control admission. Alignment can act directly on actions and artifacts; explicit models enlarge the set of properties those mechanisms can state and check.

**Governance Conversion.** Converting a recurring failure and the judgment needed to resolve it into durable engineering structure — a model, a packaged procedure, or an authoritative mechanism — that future work inherits.

<!-- index-def: compounding -->
**Engineering capital.** The stock of durable engineering assets — models, packaged procedures, and authoritative mechanisms — that reduce the recurring cost of change. It grows through governance conversion and depreciates when it no longer fits the system. Each recurring judgment converted into a durable asset lowers the future cost of change, allowing engineering effort to compound.

**The Printer.** The book's governing metaphor: treat a coding agent like a 3D printer. It builds almost anything you specify — and only what you specify.

## How coding agents work (and fail)

What a fleet of coding agents is, and the ways it comes apart.

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — glossary section-map, orientation reference chrome not a numbered cross-ref float -->

<!-- figure: assets/gloss-agent-vocab.svg | The agent stack and a characteristic failure mode. When a task's reasoning horizon exceeds the working state the harness can keep active, repeated reconstruction contributes to churn. -->

**Churn.** The agent-fleet form of velocity decay: an increasing share of effort spent rediscovering context, undoing recent changes, repairing regressions, or reconciling inconsistencies rather than advancing the system.

**Context Window.** The bounded amount of information the foundation model can hold directly in context at once.

**Foundation model.** The trained model at the bottom of the agent stack. Its capabilities and context capacity establish important bounds that the surrounding harness and engineering environment must work within.

**Agentic harness.** The runtime wrapped around the foundation model — Claude Code, OpenCode, and their kin — that feeds it prompts, manages the context window, and hosts the hook, skill, and tool mechanism points.

**Skill.** A reusable body of guidance, with trigger conditions, for a recurring class of agent work.

**Tool.** A deterministic capability exposed to an agent through code or an interface, allowing it to perform an action instead of approximating one through reasoning.

<!-- index-def: mastery-skill -->
**Mastery skill.** A skill that packages domain knowledge plus a repeatable decision procedure for doing a class of work well, independent of any one tool. Contrast a *tool skill*, which teaches one interface. Self-governance, self-operate, and self-communicate are the book's three shipped mastery skills.

**Self-governance.** The mastery-skill that represents the MAGE method itself — Modeling and Alignment turned on an agent's own engineering work. It routes each engineering question to the facet that owns it and converts durable lessons into engineering capital. It is not a third activity beside Modeling and Alignment, and not a third thesis.

**Self-operate.** The operational mastery-skill. It routes an operating condition to a typed runbook and runs it, and returns engineering deficiencies up to self-governance rather than redesigning the environment itself.

**Fleet.** The set of coding agents working on a shared codebase or engineering system.

**Reasoning horizon.** The amount of intermediate reasoning state a task requires held at once to carry it to a correct result. It is a property of the task, not of who reasons — human, agent, or a later architecture. A *long-horizon task* is one whose reasoning horizon outruns the available working memory, forcing lossy compression and reconstruction of that state; the context window instantiates that limit today.

## The Governed Engineering Environment

The environment that holds the fleet to a standard, and the mechanisms it is built from.

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — glossary section-map, orientation reference chrome not a numbered cross-ref float -->

<!-- figure: assets/gloss-gee.svg | The governed environment. An obligation reaches a mechanism — constraint, sensor, validator, gate — by two paths: an observable action or artifact, or an explicit model adding richer semantics. -->

**Governed Engineering Environment.** An engineering environment in which consequential policy is carried by durable mechanisms rather than depending primarily on human memory or repeated inspection. Explicit models can extend those mechanisms to properties that would otherwise require human reconstruction or judgment.

**Governance Mechanism.** A repeatable structure in the governed engineering environment that encodes an engineering policy. Every mechanism primarily serves one of four archetypal roles: constraint, sensor, validator, or gate. These categories describe the role a mechanism plays in the engineering environment, not the outcome it ultimately contributes to.

*The four mechanism roles.*

**Constraint.** Restricts the permissible action space.

**Sensor.** Observes the system and produces evidence about what happened.

**Validator.** Evaluates evidence against an engineering obligation.

**Gate.** Controls whether work may proceed across a workflow boundary.

A single implementation may participate in more than one role. A linter, for example, both produces the finding-evidence and judges it against the ban — a sensor and validator bundled — while the commit hook or CI pipeline is the gate that blocks the change from advancing.

**Lint.** An automated check that scans code for a banned pattern and fails the commit when it finds one, so a rule holds without a human remembering it.

**Hook.** A mechanism that fires automatically at a defined point in a workflow — before or after a step — to enforce policy without anyone remembering to. The harness's before/after-action hooks are one instance; Git, CI, build, and deploy hooks are others.

**Invariant.** A predicate that must hold over a system, model, artifact, or process. Making the relevant state explicit determines whether the invariant can be checked mechanically and at what scale.

**Model drift.** A model and the system, evidence, or other representation to which it is meant to correspond cease to agree.

**Drift Gate.** A build-time check that fails when a model and the code it describes disagree, keeping the model from decaying into stale documentation.

**Traceability.** Explicit, machine-readable relationships connecting model elements to the artifacts, evidence, obligations, or other model elements they represent or govern.
