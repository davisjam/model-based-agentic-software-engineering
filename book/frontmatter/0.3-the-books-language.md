# The Book's Language

## Glossary

Agentic software engineering is a fast-evolving topic, and it seems that many concepts have been given overlapping or contradictory names.
Part of the purpose of this book is to articulate coherent vocabulary for the field.
The glossary is divided into three parts: the core ideas of the book, the vocabulary for coding agents and their accompanying phenomena, and the terms for the governed engineering environment that stabilizes them.

## The core ideas

The ideas the whole argument turns on — the two theses, the conversion that grows the environment, the governing metaphor, and the vocabulary of models underneath them.

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — glossary section-map, orientation reference chrome not a numbered cross-ref float -->

<!-- figure: assets/gloss-core-ideas.svg | The core ideas, as a map: commodity intelligence forces engineering to reorganize around governance, which the two theses, the conversion, and the Printer metaphor spell out. -->

<!-- index-def: commodity-intelligence -->
**Commodity intelligence.** Machine intelligence that has become abundant, inexpensive, and broadly available. Once intelligence is no longer scarce, engineering reorganizes around judgment, representations, evidence, authority, and governance.

**Governance.** The engineering discipline of encoding judgment into durable mechanisms that autonomous systems inherit automatically. In MAGE, governance includes models, constraints, validators, policies, workflows, and other executable or machine-interpretable artifacts; it is not synonymous with management or compliance.

**Engineering.** What separates engineering from mere construction: the responsibility to compare alternatives and predict their trade-offs *before* building. A model makes that prediction cheap — you reason over the representation instead of over the running system.

**Software engineering.** Engineering applied to software: choosing among workable designs by reasoning about the properties each will produce, *before* building. When agents make implementation cheap, you can build more than one and select among them — and selection needs a model to predict from.

**Model.** A structured approximation of a system that preserves the information needed to answer some class of engineering questions. Engineers and agents reason through the model rather than reconstructing those answers from lower-level system detail. To be governed, a model must have an explicit, machine-readable structure that can be queried, validated, and checked for drift.

**Structured.** An explicit, declared shape a machine can read and validate—a schema rather than unconstrained prose. Structure makes the representation reliably queryable, joinable, and governable at the level of its meaning.

**Descriptive vs. prescriptive.** Two things a model's facts can do. A *descriptive* fact records what the system currently is — a service exists, a call is made. A *prescriptive* (intent-bearing, blueprint) fact declares what is allowed or required — only this class may consume that queue. Descriptive facts let an agent reason; prescriptive facts give the environment something to enforce.

<!-- index-def: model-classes -->
**Model classes.** Five kinds of model, sorted by what the model *represents*. The kind fixes the diagram it wants and the invariant it can carry, so naming it first is how a reader reads a model page on reflex.

- **Behavioral.** Represents how the system acts across runs — its states, its transitions, and the properties that must hold over them. The job-lifecycle **state machine** (the process view) is one: `leased` holds if and only if an owner is set.
- **Structural.** Represents the system's shape — its parts, its zones, and which parts may compose. The **component-zone model** (the development view) is one, induced from the real directory tree and gated against the imports the code actually makes.
- **Decision.** Represents a choice the system must honor — a contract, a policy, a permitted-value set. The **service-flow model** and the **access policy** generated from it (the logical view) are one. A contract or policy model is a Decision model.
- **Measurement.** Represents a quantity the system exposes — a cost, a load, a coverage surface. The **performance-and-cost model** (the physical view) is one, reading a per-host ceiling and budget the scheduler rations against.
- **Documentation.** Represents human-facing explanation as a governed artifact — generated from a model and provenance-checked, so a hand-edit reddens the build instead of drifting unseen. A **doc surface** is a first-class node in the traceability graph, joined to the code it explains by a re-proved edge.

Governance is not a sixth class. It names an *action on* a model — the Move axis, whether a mechanism constrains, senses, or packages — not a kind of representation. A governance mechanism governs a Decision model or a Structural one; it is not itself a model class.

**Modeling Thesis.** Externalize engineering knowledge and intent into explicit, structured models so engineers and agents share a compact representation to reason through.

**Alignment Thesis.** Encode engineering obligations into authoritative mechanisms that constrain actions, produce evidence, validate that evidence, and control admission. The mechanisms may be mechanically checkable or may require agent or human judgment; what matters is that the obligation has authority over the engineering process.

**Governance Conversion.** Converting a recurring failure and the judgment needed to resolve it into durable engineering structure that future work inherits.

<!-- index-def: compounding -->
**Engineering capital.** The stock of durable engineering mechanisms that reduce the recurring cost of change. It grows through governance conversion and depreciates when it no longer fits the system. Each recurring judgment converted into a durable mechanism lowers the future cost of change, allowing engineering effort to compound.

**The Printer.** The book's governing metaphor: treat a coding agent like a 3D printer. It builds almost anything you specify — and only what you specify.

## How coding agents work (and fail)

What a fleet of coding agents is, and the ways it comes apart.

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — glossary section-map, orientation reference chrome not a numbered cross-ref float -->

<!-- figure: assets/gloss-agent-vocab.svg | The agent stack and how it comes apart: the harness wraps a foundation model, hosts skills and tools, and manages a context window whose overflow drives churn. -->

**Churn.** The agent-fleet form of velocity decay: an increasing share of effort spent rediscovering context, undoing recent changes, repairing regressions, or reconciling inconsistencies rather than advancing the system.

**Context Window.** The bounded amount of information the foundation model can hold directly in context at once.

**Foundation model.** The trained model at the bottom of the agent stack. Its capabilities and context capacity establish important bounds that the surrounding harness and engineering environment must work within.

**Agentic harness.** The runtime wrapped around the foundation model — Claude Code, OpenCode, and their kin — that feeds it prompts, manages the context window, and hosts the hook, skill, and tool mechanism points.

**Skill.** A reusable body of guidance, with trigger conditions, for a recurring class of agent work.

**Tool.** A deterministic capability exposed to an agent through code or an interface, allowing it to perform an action instead of approximating one through reasoning.

**Fleet.** The set of coding agents working on a shared codebase or engineering system.

**Reasoning horizon.** The amount of intermediate reasoning state a task requires held at once to carry it to a correct result. It is a property of the task, not of who reasons — human, agent, or a later architecture. A *long-horizon task* is one whose reasoning horizon outruns the available working memory, forcing lossy compression and reconstruction of that state; the context window instantiates that limit today.

## The Governed Engineering Environment

The environment that holds the fleet to a standard, and the mechanisms it is built from.

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — glossary section-map, orientation reference chrome not a numbered cross-ref float -->

<!-- figure: assets/gloss-gee.svg | The governed environment: a governance mechanism enforces an invariant by playing one of four roles — constraint, sensor, validator, gate — firing at a hook, compounding into engineering capital. -->

**Governed Engineering Environment.** An engineering environment in which policy is encoded into models and mechanisms rather than relying on human memory or repeated inspection.

**Support ratio.** Support-apparatus lines of code divided by production lines of code — a measure of how much governed environment a fleet has built around the product it governs.

**Governance Mechanism.** A repeatable structure in the governed engineering environment that encodes an engineering policy. Every mechanism primarily serves one of four archetypal roles: constraint, sensor, validator, or gate. These categories describe the role a mechanism plays in the engineering environment, not the outcome it ultimately contributes to.

*The four mechanism roles.*

**Constraint.** Restricts the permissible action space.

**Sensor.** Observes the system and produces evidence about what happened.

**Validator.** Evaluates evidence against an engineering obligation.

**Gate.** Controls whether work may proceed across a workflow boundary.

A single implementation may participate in more than one role. A linter, for example, both produces the finding-evidence and judges it against the ban — a sensor and validator bundled — while the commit hook or CI pipeline is the gate that blocks the change from advancing.

**Lint.** An automated check that scans code for a banned pattern and fails the commit when it finds one, so a rule holds without a human remembering it.

**Hook.** A mechanism that fires automatically at a defined point in a workflow — before or after a step — to enforce policy without anyone remembering to. The harness's before/after-action hooks are one instance; Git, CI, build, and deploy hooks are others.

**Invariant.** A predicate over a model that must always hold — the testable claim a gate or a model-checker enforces.

**Model drift.** A model and the system, evidence, or other representation to which it is meant to correspond cease to agree.

**Drift Gate.** A build-time check that fails when a model and the code it describes disagree, keeping the model from decaying into stale documentation.

**Traceability.** Explicit, machine-readable relationships connecting model elements to the artifacts, evidence, obligations, or other model elements they represent or govern.
