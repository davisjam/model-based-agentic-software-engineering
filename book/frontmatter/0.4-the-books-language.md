# Glossary

Agentic software engineering is moving quickly, and its vocabulary has not settled. This glossary is a reference to the vocabulary used throughout the book. Definitions are collected here for lookup; the main text introduces and motivates the important concepts in context. It has three parts: software engineering vocabulary, software agents, and the governed engineering environment.

## Software engineering vocabulary

**Engineering.** The discipline of exercising informed *control* over consequential systems and accepting *responsibility* for their outcomes. *Control* means retaining sufficient command of a system to understand and direct it, evaluate the evidence for its consequential properties, recognize when its assumptions fail, and intervene when necessary. *Responsibility* means remaining answerable for the consequential decisions made under one's authority and for the resulting system. Engineering does not require personally performing every act of implementation, and work may be delegated to other humans or to machine intelligence. The term is used functionally rather than institutionally. Engineering is defined by the control, evidence, expertise, and responsibility exercised, regardless of whether the work belongs to a conventionally named engineering discipline. Software engineering applies this discipline to software systems.

**Engineering judgment.** The use of technical knowledge, evidence, and experience to decide what matters in an engineering situation and what action is justified. It includes choosing appropriate models and assumptions, assessing whether evidence is sufficient, and weighing the consequences for people affected by the system.[cite: petroski1994judgment; swenson2025judgment] Competence in making these decisions, together with accountability for their consequences, helps justify society entrusting engineers with authority over consequential systems.[cite: engineeringcouncil2026ethics]

**Software engineering.** The engineering discipline of controlled change in software systems: it creates and evolves software while preserving sufficient control over the properties and consequences that matter. The definition is shared with the companion *Software Engineering Handbook*, which develops it fully.[cite: davis2026sehandbook]

**Agentic engineering.** The engineering of systems and environments in which autonomous agents perform consequential work. MAGE develops one approach, using software engineering as its principal example.

**Engineering obligation.** A requirement or engineering decision that the responsible engineering authority has determined a system or its development must satisfy. Obligations may arise externally—from users, regulations, standards, contracts, or operating environments—or internally, from decisions about architecture, behavior, quality, or process. An obligation is enforceable when the environment can evaluate whether it holds; enforcement additionally requires a mechanism that prevents or refuses work that violates it.

**Model.** A purposeful simplification of a system that preserves what matters for a particular engineering question. By leaving other details out, a model makes the relevant properties easier to understand and reason about than the system itself.

**Model classes.** MAGE uses six recurring classes of models, organized by the engineering questions they help answer ([ref:gloss-model-classes]). The list is not exhaustive, and one representation may serve more than one class.

**Modeling Principle.** Make engineering knowledge and intent explicit in purposeful models so humans and agents can reason about larger questions without reconstructing the needed facts from lower-level detail.

<!-- index-def: degrees-of-freedom -->

**Tolerance and degree of freedom.** Engineering obligations need not determine a single acceptable implementation. A tolerance bounds the variation that an obligation permits; a degree of freedom is a choice that engineering leaves open within the applicable obligations. The term borrows its usual mathematical sense: constraints reduce the number of quantities that may vary independently. MAGE applies the same idea to engineering choices. As obligations constrain an implementation, some choices cease to be free while others remain open to realization. If a service must respond within 200 milliseconds, for example, implementations may vary in response time within that bound, while choices such as programming language may remain degrees of freedom if they do not affect any consequential obligation. An unmodeled choice is simply a choice that the current engineering representations do not make explicit; it is not necessarily free. If some alternatives would be rejected because they violate an engineering obligation, the choice is constrained even if the model does not say so.

**Governance.** Deciding what autonomous work must know, what it may do, what evidence it must produce, and what mechanisms will hold those decisions over time.

**Authority.** Who is entitled to exercise engineering judgment: to decide what a system must do, permit or refuse an action, and answer for the result. Authority is a matter of decision rights and responsibility, held by a person or institution and delegated deliberately, not a property of a mechanism. It is distinct from *enforcement*—the mechanisms through which the environment holds work to an obligation—and from an *authoritative source*, the canonical origin of a fact. {{chapter:profession}} treats professional authority as first-class.

**Alignment Principle.** Make selected engineering obligations enforceable through constraints that restrict actions or states, sensors that produce evidence, and gates that evaluate evidence and control whether work may proceed. Some obligations can be enforced directly on actions or artifacts; explicit models let the environment state and check richer properties, including acceptable bounds on variation.

**Governance conversion.** Turning a lesson from a recurring failure, repeated judgment, or observed gap into durable engineering structure that future work inherits. The structure may be a model, packaged procedure, or mechanism that enforces an obligation. Fixing one forbidden dependency repairs the immediate defect; recording the dependency rule and installing a check can help later work avoid repeating it. The lesson has been converted when future work can use it without waiting for someone to remember the incident.

<!-- index-def: compounding -->

**Engineering capital.** Durable engineering structure—models, packaged procedures, mechanisms, canonical documentation, and other reusable assets—that makes later work cheaper or less uncertain. Governance conversion creates such structure; it becomes capital when later work benefits from it, and depreciates when it no longer fits the system or costs more to maintain than it returns.

<!-- index-def: commodity-intelligence -->

**Commodity intelligence.** General-purpose machine intelligence available cheaply and at enough scale that implementation becomes less scarce relative to engineering judgment. The term describes an economic change, not a claim that intelligence is free, unlimited, or itself sufficient for engineering.

## Software agents

<!-- figure: assets/gloss-agent-vocab.svg | The agent stack and a characteristic failure mode. When a task exceeds the system’s reasoning horizon, the agent may repeatedly reconstruct information without making progress. This churn can occur when the system cannot keep the information needed for later decisions available. -->

**Agent.** An AI system that pursues an open-ended or underspecified task through multiple steps, exercising judgment about what to do next and using tools in response to intermediate results. In this book, agent means a coding or engineering agent operating over software artifacts, tools, and an execution environment.

**Churn.** Work spent rediscovering context, undoing recent changes, repairing regressions, or reconciling inconsistencies instead of advancing the system.

**Foundation model.** The trained model at the bottom of the agent stack. Its capabilities and context capacity set important limits that the harness and engineered environment must work within.

**Agentic harness.** The runtime that turns a foundation model’s reasoning into action by managing context, invoking tools, and returning results to the model. It may expose hooks at which additional code can run. How those facilities are configured and used is part of the engineered environment.

**Skills and tools.** Capabilities exposed through an agentic harness that extend what an agent can do. A skill provides reusable guidance for a recurring class of work; a tool provides a deterministic capability the agent can invoke rather than approximate through reasoning.

**Fleet.** The set of coding agents working on a shared codebase or engineering system.

**Reasoning horizon.** The practical limit of the work a reasoner and its supporting machinery can carry through reliably, given the available representations and resources. For an agent, this depends on the foundation model together with the harness, external memory, and tools. Better machinery can help the system handle more demanding tasks. Better representations can reduce the reasoning a task requires. {{sec:reasoning-horizon}} develops the definition and its grounding in the literature.

## The Governed Engineering Environment

<!-- noqa: book-float-ref — glossary section-map, orientation reference chrome not a numbered cross-ref float -->

<!-- figure: assets/gloss-gee.svg | The governed environment. Governance mechanisms support enforcement by restricting work, supplying evidence, or controlling whether work may proceed. They can operate directly on observable work or use explicit models that expose richer semantics. Durable models, mechanisms, procedures, and documentation accumulate as engineering capital. -->

**Governed Engineering Environment.** An engineered environment whose repeatable mechanisms enforce selected engineering obligations. The engineered environment comprises the project’s representations, tools, processes, permissions, and checks, including how harness facilities are used. A harness supplies hooks; configuring them to require project checks contributes to the environment. Build gates and review procedures can contribute independently of the harness. Explicit models let the environment govern properties that would otherwise require a person to reconstruct or judge them.

**Governance mechanisms.** Repeatable structures in the engineered environment that constrain agentic work, produce evidence about it, or control whether it may proceed.

- **Constraint.** Restricts available actions or states.
- **Sensor.** Observes the work or system and produces evidence about artifacts, proposed actions, or executions.
- **Gate.** Evaluates whether an action or result may proceed and enforces the decision. Gates can govern changes, releases, resource use, or runtime operations.

A single mechanism may perform several functions.

**Semantic gap.** The distance between where an engineering obligation arises and where the environment has enough meaning and evidence to decide whether the obligation is satisfied. The gap explains why some checks must wait until a later boundary, when enough of the system has been assembled to make the obligation decidable.

**Model drift.** A model and the system, evidence, or other representation to which it is meant to correspond cease to agree. The disagreement identifies a correspondence problem whose resolution requires engineering judgment. Updating the model to describe a forbidden dependency would restore agreement while preserving the violation. Reconciliation therefore requires returning to the engineering intent that the model was meant to preserve.

**Traceability.** Explicit, machine-readable links from model elements to the artifacts, evidence, obligations, or other model elements they represent, constrain, or correspond to. A link can remain intact while its meaning has gone stale: a test may still carry a requirement’s identifier after it has stopped testing that requirement.
