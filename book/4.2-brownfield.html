Part 4 — The MAGE Method  ::  Chapter 19

4.2 Migrating to MAGE

Most readers do not begin on a blank page. You inherit some combination of code, tests, schemas, architectural documents, CI rules, operational lore, permissions, dashboards, and conventions. That estate carries both liabilities and assets: technical debt, certainly, but also accumulated engineering capital in forms nobody has connected strongly enough to reuse. Migration is partly asset recovery — find the useful representations and controls already present, reconnect them to their territory, retire what has rotted, and invest where recurring costs justify stronger structure. That is a richer starting story than "legacy code is spaghetti."

Two axes fix the starting move. The first is how much system already exists. A mature estate gives you implementation, history, and latent representation to recover; a new system does not. The second is how settled the relevant intent is. A detailed contract can justify substantial modeling and authority before implementation; an exploratory product question cannot. These axes are independent. Greenfield does not imply uncertainty, and brownfield does not imply settled intent: a blank-page regulated system can carry highly settled obligations, while a mature product may still be exploring what its users need.

Figure 4.2-1 crosses the two axes. The cells do not prescribe how much "governance" an entire project deserves. They prescribe how aggressively a particular body of intent should be represented and given authority at the start. The more settled a particular obligation, the more authority it can safely receive — which is why a security, legal, or safety obligation may earn enforcement even inside an otherwise exploratory product.

Brownfield work enters through many doors, but two are useful extremes. At one end is the fast-built system whose implementation outran its explicit engineering account: code exists, perhaps even a working product, but the architecture, obligations, and operating rules were never made durable. DocAble began near this end — it vibe-coded a plausible product, then migrated it into a governed one; Part 5 (The Evidence) diagnoses the gap it was crossing, from demoable to productizable. At the other end is the established organization with years of accumulated structure: ADRs, schemas, ownership files, developer portals, CI policy, tests, and operational playbooks. Its problem is not absence but fragmentation.

The first estate must induce and author more of its initial models. The second can recover and reconcile more of what it already knows. They converge quickly because both eventually face the same four questions: which representations answer recurring engineering questions, which existing controls already carry useful authority, where do humans still bridge missing semantics, and what is the cheapest next improvement that pays for itself? Industry is reaching the same conclusion about the surfaces an organization already owns: a developer portal's value was never its UI but the curated context underneath — service and API catalogs, golden-path templates, ownership, CI/CD signals — and agentic engineering turns that existing human-facing surface into an execution substrate "the portal isn't dying; it's becoming infrastructure" 11. Red Hat, “Why Developer Portals Matter More in the Age of AI Agents,” Red Hat, 2026, https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/why-developer-portals-matter-more-age-ai-agents..

Neither door should scaffold a complete governed environment before useful work begins. Preserve the obligations already worth preserving, surface the representations already worth trusting, and let implementation and operation expose what is still missing. The method is constant; the justified starting stock is not. A safety-critical project may begin with extensive explicit requirements and heavy validation; a product search may begin with a few non-negotiable constraints and measurements; a brownfield estate begins with the accumulated evidence of its own history, however poorly represented.

4.2.1 Start with what you already have

Survey the estate before designing the replacement. Architecture documents, API specifications, deployment manifests, ADRs, schemas, ownership files, tests, CI configuration, permissions, and runbooks are all partial engineering structures. Some are models: reduced representations of the system used for a purpose. Some are already governance mechanisms: CI rejects a merge, a schema excludes an invalid state, a permission denies an action. Many are both weakly connected and unevenly trusted.

So the first brownfield move is two inventories in parallel, not one:

  • What representations do we already reason through? Which document, schema, graph, test structure, or configuration repeatedly saves someone from reading raw implementation?
  • What authority already acts? Which permissions, types, validators, CI checks, reviews, and operational gates currently constrain or admit work — and what semantics can each actually see?

That second inventory matters because the old claim that inherited artifacts are all non-authoritative is too broad. A CI configuration may already gate merges; a schema may already reject data; a type system may already constrain states; tests may already carry admission authority. Mature organizations often have a great deal of Alignment and weak system-level Modeling — their human reviewers occupy the semantic gap. The migration target is not "turn every artifact into a model." It is to connect useful representations to the territory they claim to describe, make important obligations explicit enough to evaluate, and place appropriate authority where the necessary semantics are available. The migration strengthens both inventories, rather than mechanically repairing the map first and then installing authority.

A wiki is a useful first path because many organizations already have one and the migration distance is short. Treat it as an informal model: a reduced representation people already use to reason about the system, even though its semantics are weak and nothing mechanical guarantees correspondence with the code. Pages supply nodes of a kind, links supply relationships of a kind, and search supplies navigation. That does not make the wiki equivalent to the structured graph Part 2 built. It makes it raw material.

The first improvement is connection, not replacement. Important claims should point to the code, tests, configuration, or runtime evidence that realizes them; important implementation regions should point back to the pages that explain why they exist.** The earlier Parts develop the stronger form of this join: every model node points to the code it governs, and that code points back to the model node that explains it. The resulting trace is not yet a proof that the prose is true. It is a disciplined join that lets audits and later mechanisms discover when the map and the territory disagree. Figure 4.2-2 draws the two independent ways that join can be strengthened.

Inset — Informal, structured, or executable: which kind of model?

"Model" spans a range of strengths, and this chapter walks up it.

  • Informal model. A reduced representation you reason over — a diagram, a prose spec, a wiki. Its claimed properties are legible to a human; nothing mechanical maintains them. The wiki starts here.
  • Structured model. A machine-readable declared shape — typed fields, invariants, dependencies — that a tool can parse. Legible now to an agent and a check, not only a reader.
  • Executable model. A structured model a tool actually consumes — to derive context, generate checks, drive retrieval, validate, or measure. Traceability and drift controls may keep the model trustworthy, but they are not what makes the representation executable; being consumed is.

A wiki is a real model at the first strength — do not wait for the third to start.

4.2.2 Audit, synchronize, govern, extend

The migration runs in four stages, each a useful stopping point, so an organization does not have to reach executable models before the work starts paying for itself.

  • Audit. Build the join and classify gaps without blocking work. The representation is known to drift; making every claim mandatory before repairing it would only canonize old errors.
  • Synchronize. Use ordinary feature and maintenance work to reconcile nearby claims with the current repository. Require disagreements to be surfaced rather than silently choosing prose or code as truth.
  • Govern. Once particular obligations have earned trust, make the relevant checks part of acceptance. This may include required analysis of linked material — but that is guidance and workflow policy; authority comes from the mechanism that evaluates or gates the obligation, not from placing the wiki in context.
  • Extend. Promote high-value claims into more structured representations where a real consumer exists: retrieval, analysis, generation, validation, measurement, or gating. Never add metadata without a reader — a tag, a backlink, or an ownership field earns its place only when an audit, a brief, a retrieval step, an analysis, or a gate reads it. Spotify built exactly this ground before pointing any agent at its estate: a machine-readable catalog of services and owners, each fact wired to a check that reads it so it cannot drift unread.

The four stages compress to one line you can keep at the bench: link the surface → drain the drift → make synchronization mandatory → extend selected claims into executable models. The companion From-Drifted-Wiki-to-Trusted-Model operator card holds the drill whole.

4.2.3 Know the destination, but migrate by useful slices

The near destination is a trusted map over the parts of the system that matter now: important claims have known anchors, important implementation regions have explanatory homes, and agents and engineers can navigate the join without rediscovering it from scratch. In a bounded subsystem this can begin paying off within weeks — long before the far destination arrives.

The farther destination is not a completely modeled codebase. It is adequate representation and evidence for the system's quality case: enough architecture, behavior, ownership, measurement, and traceability to answer the properties you actually mean to defend. For any property you claim, you can point to the code that implements it, the analysis that enforces it, the tests that confirm it, and the record of every change and why it was made. Some organizations stop at a trusted, human-facing map. Others promote selected claims into typed or executable models. The right endpoint is the one whose future value justifies its upkeep.

4.2.4 Migrate one bounded surface at a time

A live estate rarely tolerates a wholesale cutover. Choose a bounded surface — a subsystem, service, policy family, or recurring workflow — and let old and new coexist while the replacement earns trust. A strangler migration and a pilot cell are two versions of the same operational principle: bound the experiment, preserve reversibility, and generalize only what the slice proves. DocAble's serverless re-platforming ran this shape — a stateful job pipeline moved off long-lived polling workers onto instances that scale to zero behind a managed push queue; the push plane grew beside the poll plane behind a default-off toggle, proved green on a staging namespace running identical code, took the traffic, then the old cluster was deleted and preserved on an archive branch.

Within that slice, recover the representation using whichever direction the evidence supports. Top-down work starts from intended architecture or requirements and refines toward implementation. Bottom-up work induces stable abstractions from the code and rises toward a model. Clean-and-induce first removes accidental complexity — under tests — so the latent abstractions become legible enough to recover; agents induce models from code far more reliably when the code presents named abstractions and shallow control structure. Real migrations use all three, and the directions should meet at the grain where the relevant property becomes answerable.

A disagreement then has the familiar three-way diagnosis — the representation is wrong, the grain is wrong, or the implementation is wrong. Do not decide which in advance; shifting a legacy codebase from "the tests pass" to "these properties hold across all behaviors" surfaces disagreements by design.

Inset — When the model and the code disagree

Tighten a model against real code and it will, sooner or later, contradict the code. A contradiction is not a failure of the method; it is the method working. It means exactly one of three things:

  • The model is wrong. It claims something the code never promised. Refine the model.
  • The model is at the wrong grain. It is asking a question at a level of abstraction that cannot see the answer. Build a different model at the grain the property lives at.
  • The code is wrong. The model is right, the code disagrees, and the modeling just found a bug.

The discipline is to decide which before you act. Fixing code when the model was wrong bakes in a false claim; refining the model when the code had a bug hides the bug behind an adjusted spec.

Inset — Compute the blast radius before you replace the ground

One question comes before you re-pave a bounded surface everything else runs on: which mechanisms assume the substrate I am about to replace? Have each governance mechanism declare, as typed metadata, the substrate assumption it bakes in — bound to one substrate, substrate-aware, or substrate-agnostic. Joining those declarations against the topology model on a migration target turns a hopeful grep into a computed blast radius; a declaration lint requires the stance, so the one mechanism whose assumption is implicit cannot hide from the join. Worked once on the plane the fleet actually migrated: retiring a poll plane for a push plane, the join named every mechanism that baked in "workers pull from a shared sorted set" — the queue-depth metric, the stale-job sweep, the cancellation cleanup, the priority-preservation requeue — before the change landed.

4.2.5 Introduce new authority safely: Audit → Drain → Promote

A valid future invariant is not always a valid present admission rule. A legacy tree may violate the rule everywhere on the day you first decide the rule is worth having. Turning it blocking immediately does not improve the estate; it merely breaks all work already in flight. So introduce a new obligation over an inherited estate in three beats — and the order is the safety.

The move — Audit → Drain → Promote

  • Audit. Install the proposed rule as evidence only. It reports every existing violation and blocks no commit, so it never breaks an in-flight agent.
  • Drain. A fix wave repairs the existing findings under sufficient tests and other evidence to preserve intended behavior, converting the legacy code to the new convention behavior-for-behavior.
  • Promote. Once the estate satisfies the obligation, grant the mechanism blocking authority so new violations cannot re-enter through the governed boundary.

This is an authority-migration pattern, not a universal Modeling→Alignment dependency. The representation or checker may already exist — you can hold permissions, tests, and gates without an explicit system model. The point is temporal: establish what the future obligation will be, bring inherited reality into conformance, then make that obligation authoritative. Cloudflare reached the same approved → enforced order independently, landing each policy control advisory and flipping it to enforced once the tree is clean, so the promotion never breaks work in flight 22. Timo Reimann, “How Cloudflare Enforces Engineering Standards Using Ai,” Cloudflare, August 4, 2026, https://blog.cloudflare.com/engineering-standards-enforcement/.. 4.2.6 Measure what the map still cannot explain

Model induction has a stopping problem: a representation can describe everything it knows without telling you what it omitted. Traceability can provide a useful discovery instrument. Walk model → code and you can ask whether represented claims are exercised by tests. Walk code → model and you can ask which exercised behaviors trace to no relevant model claim. A test whose code reaches no model node is an orphan; cluster the orphans by the unmodelled code they share, and you hold a candidate list of the models you have not written yet.

Treat the orphans as candidates, not automatic defects. Some expose a genuinely missing model; some are missing anchors to a model that already exists; some sit legitimately below the model's chosen grain. The instrument earns its keep only if it distinguishes those cases well enough to produce an actionable backlog — otherwise it flags every unmodelled line and drowns you.

DocAble used such a reverse trace to drive a brownfield modeling loop; Part 5 (The Evidence) reports the drain and its limitations, including the honest caveat that this is a solo field report, not a controlled before-and-after. The method-level point is smaller: when you can instrument what the representation omits, use the omissions to choose the next modeling investment instead of modeling whatever happens to attract attention. A companion mechanism closes the loop from the other side, projecting your test coverage onto the model's nodes so an untested invariant shows up as a visible gap rather than hiding inside a line-coverage percentage — the catalogue names it coverage-to-model mapping.

4.2.7 Derive, don't copy

Recovering a model is only the first step. The next question is what happens to the facts it owns.

A common failure is to make a representation authoritative in name while continuing to copy its contents elsewhere. A service belongs to one team in the model and to that team again in a config file. An architectural edge exists in the graph and again in a hand-maintained allowlist. A symbol-to-requirement relation exists in the model and again as a string in a test. The copies agree on the day they are written. After that, agreement becomes maintenance work — and the copy drifts the instant the model moves while the consumer keeps passing, reasoning about a world that no longer exists.

The stronger rule is short: if a fact already has a queryable authority, consume or derive it rather than copying it. This holds for relationships as much as for scalar facts. Prefer a join over two independently maintained lists. Prefer a stable symbol or identifier over a copied path or line number — a snapshotted cross-reference rots silently, while a derived one reddens the moment the code moves. Prefer generation over synchronized hand-editing when one representation can honestly own the result.

Some facts have two legitimate homes that must both stay authoritative. There, make the correspondence observable rather than assumed. A parity check earns its keep precisely because neither side is silently trusted: the implementation can expose a stale model, and the model can expose an implementation that has escaped its declared intent. Check both directions. A one-way regenerate-from-code check quietly makes the code the truth and leaves the model free to lie; bidirectional parity is stricter on purpose, because a change must then update both sides in the same commit.

Figure 4.2-4 draws the shape: once a fact has an authority, downstream consumers query, join, derive, or generate from it, and where two authorities must remain, a correspondence check makes disagreement visible.

Hidden assumptions deserve the same treatment as copied facts. A control that depends on a particular runtime, directory layout, deployment topology, tool version, or model capability carries that dependency whether or not anyone wrote it down. When changing the substrate could invalidate the control, represent the dependency strongly enough that its blast radius can be queried before the change — the earlier inset on computing a migration's blast radius is exactly this move worked through on a real re-platforming. Do not model every dependency. Model the ones whose rediscovery by breakage is expensive. The durable rule is one line: make consequential assumptions queryable before they become surprises.

4.2.8 Price the investment

A recurring gap does not automatically deserve machinery. Price the expected future loss against the cost of building and carrying the response. Frequency and consequence are useful first approximations; detectability, reversibility, legal exposure, and the stability of the obligation can move the decision substantially. Cheap, rare failures often remain judgment. Cheap, frequent failures may justify a lint, script, or reusable procedure, because repeated agent and human attention is itself cost. Consequential failures justify a larger investment and a stronger assurance response: better representation, prevention by construction where possible, stronger sensing and validation, and authority at an appropriate boundary. Catastrophic or legally intolerable failures need not recur even once to earn that investment.

Table 4.2-1 crosses how much a failure costs against how often it happens, and each cell names the proportionate move — the smallest change that still closes the class. The reflex to reach for a control on every failure is the expensive habit; float the larger scheme, and let the cost of the failure justify building it.

Price the upkeep too. A model must be reconciled, a validator recalibrated, a lint maintained, a skill updated. Engineering capital depreciates. A control that once retired meaningful cost can become technical overhead when the obligation disappears or the maintenance burden exceeds the return — the first brick in the teetering tower. Build the smallest asset that retires the recurring cost, and be willing to retire the asset when it stops paying. Migration at fleet scale puts the same discipline under a bigger multiplier: a fleet-wide change that once drew in hundreds of teams for weeks can be scoped by one engineer over a few days, the tooling targeting and scheduling the work while agents implement it concurrently 33. Max Charas and Marc Bruggmann, “Honk: Autonomous Code Migration at Spotify,” Spotify Engineering, 2026, https://engineering.atspotify.com/.. Scale does not undo the incremental discipline; it raises the payoff.

DocAble's month-long retrofit is the deep case behind this chapter — an agent told to type Python that reached for string everywhere, a 300,000-line prototype pared to 200,000 modeled lines lint by lint and model by model. Part 5 (The Evidence) follows the migration itself; here the relevant point is that the estate was matured in place rather than rewritten from a clean slate. That is the move any team can run at any starting rung: begin from the environment you already own — a drifted wiki, a heap of spaghetti, whatever partial models and controls you already hold — inventory both what it represents and what it already governs, and mature both in place until an agent can reason through it.

Worked Examples

DocAble. A janky 300,000-line prototype started from a heap of spaghetti — the lowest rung — and was matured in place, lint by lint and model by model, until an agent could reason through it, rather than waiting for a clean estate to arrive. Part 5 (The Evidence) follows the month in full.

Takeaway. The move is bootstrap-governance: begin from the environment you already own — a drifted wiki, a heap of spaghetti, whatever partial models and controls you already hold — and mature it in place, one rung at a time, until an agent can reason through it, rather than waiting for a clean one to arrive.
