Part 4 — The MAGE Method  ::  Chapter 18

4.2 Migrating to MAGE

You have the mindset, the governed environment, and the models; this Part puts them to work, and it starts where most readers actually stand.

Everything so far has described the method as though the hard part of starting were the existing system. That is only one kind of difficulty. A greenfield system has little inherited technical structure, but it may also have little settled intent. A brownfield system may be architecturally tangled while its obligations are understood in exquisite detail.

Treat those as separate axes. How much system already exists? determines what representation you inherit. How much do you know about what the system should become? determines how aggressively you should specify and govern it.

A greenfield effort with a detailed contract, regulatory obligations, and known interfaces can model heavily from the start. A product experiment driven by a customer's vague dissatisfaction should not. Build enough to learn, preserve the constraints you already trust, and promote discoveries into models as they stabilize. Greenfield gives you freedom from inherited implementation; it does not give you foreknowledge.

Brownfield poses the complementary problem: much of the system already exists, often along with years of requirements, tests, operational experience, and institutional knowledge. The work is to recover, reconcile, and strengthen those latent models rather than inventing the system anew.

Readers reach this chapter through one of two front doors. The doors diverge at the very first move, then converge on the same path.

Through the first door comes the vibe-coder. You have a demoable MVP an agent built fast, and no wiki, no schemas, no ADRs — nothing written down to build a model from. The engine room runs, but no one has said what it is supposed to do. Your first move cannot be to surface a model, because none is recorded. It is to induce the first model from the running code and retrofit governance onto it afterward. DocAble came through this door: it vibe-coded a plausible product, then migrated it into a governed one. That order is the credential here, not a confession. Part 5 (The Evidence) diagnoses the gap you are crossing — a vibe-coded novel system is demoable, not productizable until an engineer gives it the account it never had. That chapter names the gap; this one prescribes the path across it, MVP to product.

Through the second door comes the established org. You have at least a wiki, at most ADRs, schemas, CI config, and ownership files — latent models already sitting in your artifacts, waiting to be extracted — and you are just as curious how to migrate. Your first move is not to induce a model from nothing. It is to surface the model already latent in the surfaces you own, then strengthen and govern it. The rest of this section follows that door in detail, because its distance is shortest, but the two doors meet quickly. Martin Davidson makes the point sharply: enterprises still run on structures built for a world in which writing code was expensive, even after that assumption stopped holding 11. Jennifer Riggins, “AI Coding Got Faster. Why Didn't Engineering?,” The New Stack, August 9, 2026, https://thenewstack.io/ai-productivity-measurement-gap/. — which is why the established org so often owns latent models it has never been pressed to surface.

Neither door scaffolds a full governed environment before useful work begins. Nor should a greenfield team. MAGE is not a demand to finish the specification, model, tests, schemas, linters, and gates before the first feature appears. Bootstrap what you already know is worth preserving; then let implementation and use expose what you did not know. The governed environment grows with the system.

What differs by setting is the starting stock. A safety-critical or contract-driven project may begin with extensive explicit requirements and therefore justify substantial modeling and validation before implementation. A product search may begin with only a few non-negotiable constraints and measurements. A brownfield estate begins with the accumulated evidence of its own history, however poorly represented. The method is constant; the amount of structure justified at the start is not.

The unhelpful way to read that estate is against the state of the art: you look at a company that already runs a model-based platform, count what you lack, and conclude the gap is the lesson. It is not. You are not starting from nothing, and you do not have to start from an estate you envy first. You are starting from what you already have — and you probably already have latent models. Your architecture document, your API specification, your deployment manifests, your ADRs, your schema definitions, your ownership files, and your CI configuration are each a partial model of the system: each keeps some structure an engineering purpose needs and drops the rest. None is authoritative today — each drifts, and none is mechanically tied to the code. So MAGE's first brownfield move is not to build a model estate from scratch. It is to survey the representations you already own and decide which of them should become authoritative — which partial model, made trustworthy and joined to the code, would answer a question you keep re-answering by hand. That is where you start Monday morning: from the Git history, the CI config, the wiki, the docs, and the agents you already run, not from a platform you have to acquire before the method can begin.

This chapter follows one of those latent models the whole way, and it picks the wiki on purpose, because the distance is shortest. That codebase comes wrapped in documentation: tests of uneven quality, an architecture known mostly by reputation, and a body of writing everyone consults but nobody quite trusts. Usually that writing is a wiki. It matters because of the distance involved. The path from code to a complete executable model is long. The path from code to a better wiki is short. The wiki already exists, engineers already know how to use it, and everyone already agrees it should describe the system more accurately than it does. So most engineering organizations already own the beginnings of a governed engineering environment, one where engineering knowledge and policy live in shared models, tools, and checks instead of in a few people's memory. They just call it the wiki. This chapter starts there. It turns a drifted wiki into a linked and increasingly trusted picture of the system, then carries that picture toward the stronger models and mechanisms the earlier Parts built.

Industry is reaching the same conclusion about the surfaces an organization already owns. Red Hat observes that a developer portal's value was never its UI but the curated context underneath — service and API catalogs, golden-path templates encoding architecture and compliance, ownership, CI/CD signals — and that agentic engineering turns that existing human-facing surface into an execution substrate simply by adding a programmatic (MCP) access layer: "the portal isn't dying; it's becoming infrastructure" 22. Red Hat, “Why Developer Portals Matter More in the Age of AI Agents,” Red Hat, 2026, https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/why-developer-portals-matter-more-age-ai-agents..

Brownfield adoption applies both of MAGE's theses incrementally — the daily loop's first two beats, run rung by rung: repair the map first — make the system's intent and structure explicit in a representation an agent can reason through — then install the mechanisms that keep the map and the code from drifting apart.

Where this chapter fits

This chapter builds both from what you already have. The wiki is an accessible first model; links and audits give it traceability; lints and completion checks hold the repaired relationship in place; selected high-value claims later graduate into structured, executable models.

Part 2 taught the capability ladder — the climb from context selection, through externalized knowledge and structured relationships, up to explicit invariants, model-derived analysis, and drift enforcement. Part 2 teaches that ladder; this chapter is how you climb it when you start from a brownfield estate instead of a blank page. So brownfield does not ask "how do I install MAGE." It asks two smaller questions: where on the ladder am I now, and what is the cheapest next rung that solves a real reasoning problem? You do not begin by building a complete executable model. You start at the lowest rung that pays, and you strengthen the representation only when a recurring engineering question earns the next one.

In other words: don't climb for the view. Take the next rung when a question you keep re-answering by hand would stop recurring once the representation could answer it for you.

4.2.1 Start with the model you already have

Do not dismiss a drifted wiki as failed documentation. Treat it as an immature model — a cheaper representation of the system that keeps the structure some engineering purpose needs and drops the rest.** The wiki is an informal model — a reduced representation you reason over, not yet the machine-readable, mechanically-maintained kind Part 2 admits as executable. A wiki qualifies. It is already a lightweight knowledge graph, which means you do not start at the bottom of the capability ladder Part 2 defined — you already stand on its lower rungs. Its pages are nodes, its links and backlinks are edges: the externalized knowledge and structured relationships Part 2 put at the base of the climb. Its tags and categories carry loose metadata, and its search makes all of it navigable. The semantics are weak, but the graph is already there — the crude version of the model you want, and the rung you climb from.

Inset — Informal, structured, or executable: which kind of model?

"Model" spans a range of strengths, and this chapter walks up it — the same climb Part 2's capability ladder lays out at finer resolution. These three bands coarsen its eight rungs: informal sits at the lower rungs, executable at the top.

  • Informal model. A reduced representation you reason over — a diagram, a prose spec, a wiki. Its claimed properties are legible to a human; nothing mechanical maintains them. The wiki starts here, on the ladder's externalized-knowledge and structured-relationship rungs.
  • Structured model. A machine-readable declared shape — typed fields, invariants, dependencies — that a tool can parse. Legible now to an agent and a check, not only a reader: the ladder's system-model and explicit-invariant rungs.
  • Executable model. A structured model a tool actually consumes — to derive context, generate checks, or gate drift — and that is kept tied to the running system. This is the strong sense Part 2 admits, the ladder's top: model-derived analysis, traceability, drift enforcement.

A wiki is a real model at the first strength — do not wait for the third to start.

What the wiki lacks is a disciplined join to the territory. A page describes a service but never names the code that implements it. A test guards an invariant but never links the decision that says why the invariant matters. A subsystem runs in code with no explaining page at all. The graph floats beside the repository instead of joining it.

So the first brownfield move is not to replace the wiki. It is to connect it. Establish traceability as a round trip: the important wiki claims point down to the code and tests that realize them, and the important code and test regions point back up to the pages that explain their purpose. This is traceability in the book's specific sense — a two-way join, not a heap of hyperlinks.†† The earlier Parts develop the stronger form of this join: every model node points to the code it governs, and that code points back to the model node that explains it. Figure 4.2-1 draws the join, and draws why the wiki you have and the model you want are the same shape at different strengths.

Once the links run both ways, an audit can sort every gap into one of three cases:

  1. a missing page or model;
  2. a missing anchor to a page that already exists;
  3. code that sits legitimately below the representation's grain — too fine or too generic to earn a page of its own.

The distinction keeps the audit honest. The goal is not one wiki entry per function. The goal is a map whose claimed surface agrees with the territory it represents.

The minimum package

Four small additions turn the wiki from optional documentation into the start of an engineering environment. Add all four, or the wiki stays a surface nobody trusts: together they are the minimum viable governed environment, the smallest thing that earns the name.

  • Bidirectional links. Wiki pages name the code and tests that back their claims; code and tests name the pages that explain their purpose.
  • Agent briefs. Agents are told how to reach the wiki, how to walk its links and tags, and when a page's claims should shape the task in front of them.
  • Design templates. New work names the wiki pages it touches, and records when a missing page has to be created.
  • Definition-of-Done checks. Before work is accepted, the affected pages are read against the current repository, not trusted because an agent reports it updated them.

One rule governs all four: never add metadata without adding consumers. A tag, a backlink, an ownership field, a machine-readable mirror — each earns its place only when an audit, a brief, a retrieval step, an analysis, or a gate reads it. Metadata with no reader is one more surface free to drift. Spotify built exactly this ground before pointing any agent at its estate: a machine-readable catalog of services and owners, each fact wired to a check that reads it so it cannot drift unread.

Audit, synchronize, govern, extend

The migration runs in four stages. Each one reaches a useful stopping point, so an organization does not have to arrive at executable models before the work starts paying for itself.

Audit. Spend a bounded set of agent sessions building the graph. Inventory the major wiki regions, find the code and tests each one claims to describe, add the links in both directions, and create or propose pages for the large implementation regions with no explaining home. The exit criterion is coverage of the surface that matters: every major wiki region links to the code and tests that realize it, and every major code and test region is linked, marked below the wiki's grain, or recorded as owing a new entry. This is an audit, not a gate. The wiki is known to drift; making it mandatory before repairing it would only promote old errors to official instructions.

Synchronize. Once the graph exists, drain the drift with ordinary feature and maintenance work. An agent that touches linked code or tests reads the attached pages, checks their claims against the implementation, fixes the nearby factual errors, adds the missing links, and flags the disagreements that need a human to settle. This is a boy-scout rule, not a whole-wiki rewrite: leave the linked surface no less accurate than you found it. The exit criterion is a fresh audit that shows the important wiki claims and the repository substantially in agreement, with no major orphan left unclassified.

Govern. After the wiki earns trust, promote it from optional context to required input. Agent briefs now call for task-specific wiki analysis before implementation. Design templates name the pages the work should touch. The Definition of Done requires the relevant claims to be checked against the repository at HEAD. The promotion order is the one this chapter later applies to lints: audit first, drain the findings, then make it mandatory. This is the Alignment Thesis applied one increment at a time — an obligation that used to depend on someone remembering to update the wiki becomes part of the environment's acceptance criteria.

Extend. A trusted wiki can stay a human-facing model for as long as that serves. Where the value justifies the cost, selected pages go further. Add structured correctness criteria, invariants, dependencies, ownership, allowed transitions, evidence obligations, and links to the validators or tests that discharge them. Mirror those fields in a machine-readable form once a real consumer exists. The data can then drive task-specific context retrieval, drift analysis, test-obligation derivation, semantic lints, or admission gates. The exit criterion is not "the wiki holds structured metadata." It is sharper: every structured field added during extension drives at least one retrieval path, analysis, generator, validator, or gate.

The four stages compress to one operating line you can keep at the bench: link the surface → drain the drift → earn trust → make synchronization mandatory → extend selected claims into executable models. That single sequence is the drill; the companion From-Drifted-Wiki-to-Trusted-Model operator card holds it whole for the day you run it.

4.2.2 The starting point and the destinations

Name the starting point and both destinations before you move.

The starting point is familiar: some spaghetti, a drifted wiki, thin or uneven tests, and invariants that live only in one person's head — the person you hope never retires.

The first destination is a trusted map. The major wiki claims link to the code and tests that realize them; the major implementation regions link back to the pages that explain their purpose; agents read and maintain those joins during ordinary work. In a bounded subsystem, this can begin paying off within weeks — long before the far destination arrives.

The far destination is the full model-based environment the earlier Parts built — an aspirational maturity state, not a normal stopping point. It is not complete representation of the codebase; it is adequate representation of the claims your quality case depends on — the views that bear on performance, correctness, security, or privacy you actually mean to argue. You want code that reflects those models, so the map equals the territory over the relations the model claims to represent. Zoom in on the map and you see county lines, not Switzerland. And you want traceability for your quality case, the ability to state the argument automatically. For any property you claim, you can point to the code that implements it, the static analysis that enforces it, the protocol you model-check it against, the tests that confirm it across the inputs you try, and the record of every change and why it was made. With that in hand, soft and hard governance hold the whole thing in place and keep it current.

The wiki path does not replace the three migration approaches that follow. It gives them somewhere to meet.

  • Top-down work contributes the intended architecture, the requirements, and the correctness claims.
  • Bottom-up induction finds the structure the implementation actually holds.
  • Lint and coverage make the code legible and safe enough for the two to converge.

The wiki is the first shared surface where those views can be compared. Some organizations stop at a trusted, linked, human-facing map. Others keep going, pulling the highest-value claims into typed, executable, drift-gated models. Either way, a real migration uses all three approaches, so take them one at a time.

4.2.3 Migration strategies: the moves both doors share

Whichever door you came through, the migration runs on a small set of organizational strategies. These are not architectural idioms about how to structure the system you build. They are playbooks for how an estate moves while it keeps running. Five carry most migrations, and one shape holds all five. Each box below names the doors it serves; the strategy the two doors converge on — where the vibe-coder's induced model and the established org's surfaced model meet — is Brownfield-Model-Extraction.

Migration Strategy — Strangler-Fig · serves both doors; the shape the others run in

  • Problem. A live estate cannot be rewritten in one cutover. You have to move it while it keeps serving traffic.
  • When to use. Any re-paving of a running path — a deployment substrate, a queue plane, an ungoverned module. Less a strategy you pick than the shape the others move in.
  • Approach. Martin Fowler's three beats. Coexist: build the new path beside the old, landed behind a switch that is off by default. Route around: shift traffic and trust one increment at a time, the old path staying the fallback. Eliminate: once nothing depends on the old path, delete it — code, config, and the failure class it carried — keeping an archived copy for a cold rollback.
  • Tradeoffs. Buys safety and reversibility at the price of a period running two paths. It needs a real toggle and a proof the new path is green before it takes traffic.
  • Example (DocAble). The serverless re-platforming. A stateful job pipeline moved off long-lived polling workers and a hand-built autoscaler onto instances that scale to zero behind a managed push queue. The push plane grew beside the poll plane behind a default-off toggle, proved green on a staging namespace running identical code, then took the traffic; the old cluster and its scaler were deleted, preserved on an archive branch. This chapter's own audit → drain → promote drill is the same shape in miniature — a lint coexists audit-only, a fix wave routes the tree onto the clean path, then the lint turns blocking.

Migration Strategy — Brownfield-Model-Extraction · both doors converge here

  • Problem. You inherit a system that implies models nobody has drawn, and you cannot model all of it at once.
  • When to use. The default path for most readers. Extract just enough model to stand up one governance mechanism on one slice, then repeat.
  • Approach. Carve one capability. Give it a model — top-down from intent, bottom-up from code, or lint-cover-induce where the code is too messy to read (the three approaches below). This is where the two doors meet: the vibe-coder induces that first model from the running code, the established org surfaces it from the wiki, and from there both run the same loop. Wire one mechanism to the model — a boundary lint, a drift gate — leave the rest, and let the Missing-Model Metric name the next slice. Keeping the model alive with a drift gate is a step inside this loop, not a separate strategy.
  • Tradeoffs. Slow and un-heroic; you never get a "whole system modeled" milestone. But every slice pays before the next begins, and the orphan metric keeps you from choosing slices by only what you happen to see.
  • Example (DocAble). A zone model extracted from the codebase replaced four drifting allowlists with one typed field and unlocked three lints. The Missing-Model-Metric loop then drove a subsystem's orphan rate down wave by wave and caught real shipping bugs the passing tests had missed — a subprocess exit code misfiled, a chunk leaked on redelivery, a last-write-wins editor race. Part 5 (The Evidence) runs the full account.

Migration Strategy — Governance-Conversion · serves both doors

  • Problem. Failures recur. Patching the instance leaves the class open for the next agent to rediscover.
  • When to use. The moment a failure appears a second time — the recurrence is the signal — or the moment you catch yourself saying "this must never happen again."
  • Approach. Run the six-step conversion loop: a failure, a diagnosis one level below the symptom, a generalization to the class, the smallest mechanism that closes the class, the retirement of the class, and the engineering capital left behind. The output varies with the failure — a type, a lint, a gate, or a banked human decision rule, where judgment rather than machinery is what recurs. Each conversion mints the control the next one needs, so the capital compounds: deposit a little governance every sprint and the estate governs more of itself each month.
  • Tradeoffs. Spends judgment once, up front, to save it forever; the discipline is refusing the quick patch. Soft guidance that aims versus a hard mechanism that holds is a real choice — not every failure earns a wall.
  • Example (DocAble). An agent told to "use types" satisfied the letter and made everything a string. The conversion was to induce a structured model per dense primitive region and drive every commodity lint to blocking as it hit zero — a month of governance conversion at brownfield scale, told below in the field note and in full in Part 5 (The Evidence).

Migration Strategy — Surface-Before-Authority · both doors, especially the established org

You cannot gate what the environment does not yet represent, and premature enforcement breaks in-flight work. So promote every implicit rule in one order: represent it (a model, a metadata field, a lint landed audit-only) → drain its findings to zero → promote it to authority (a blocking gate, a required input). Never add metadata without a consumer; never flip a lint blocking while findings remain — flipping first would break every in-flight agent's commit at once, so the ordering is the safety. DocAble runs it as the wiki's Audit → Synchronize → Govern → Extend stages and as the audit-only → drain → promote lint-landing drill. Cloudflare reached the same approved → enforced order independently: it lands each policy control advisory, then flips it to enforced once the tree is clean, so the promotion never breaks work in flight 33. Timo Reimann, “How Cloudflare Enforces Engineering Standards Using Ai,” Cloudflare, August 4, 2026, https://blog.cloudflare.com/engineering-standards-enforcement/..

Migration Strategy — Pilot-Cell · both doors

A whole-org or whole-system transform is too risky and too slow to learn from. Stand up one governed enclave instead — a single subsystem, service, or bounded corpus — run the full discipline there, measure, and generalize only what the pilot proves. A cheap pilot that kills a bad path is a win, and the measurement gates the real decision. DocAble piloted the serverless move on one narrow subsystem, explicitly not a broad migration, gated on a missing cost-and-latency measurement, and ran the Missing-Model Metric over its single best-modeled subsystem before generalizing.

4.2.4 Approach one: top-down, from a whiteboard

Start coarse and refine. Your first model can be as crude as "on this input, the test suite passes and we get the right output" — and you should be able to write that today. From there you ask what happens between input and output: insert a file, and out comes a score, a spreadsheet, whatever your program produces; insert a picture, and out come the cats and antelopes it found. Refine until the model captures the abstractions you need, and stop when you hit diminishing returns — the territory is the territory, and the map only has to be detailed enough to carry the assurance you are after.

Siemens runs the industrial-scale version of this move: an engineer states intent in plain words and a deterministic solver, not the reasoner, returns the verdict on whether the part survives the load — routing the decision through an owned model, not the code.

You can seed this from a literal whiteboard: photograph the diagram, hand it to the agent, and have it produce a data-flow diagram in whatever representation you like. Then iterate against the code — "what nodes am I missing, what flows am I missing?" — and tighten the policies as the model sharpens: static assertions ("we never call exec"), then the invariants of each module, then the properties preserved across module interactions. Every disagreement the tightening turns up resolves one of three ways — refine the model, remodel at a different grain, or fix a real bug (see the inset). All three are legitimate moves, and shifting a legacy codebase from "the tests pass" to "these properties hold across all behaviors" surfaces disagreements by design.

Inset — When the model and the code disagree

Tighten a model against real code and it will, sooner or later, contradict the code. A contradiction is not a failure of the method; it is the method working. It means exactly one of three things:

  • The model is wrong. It claims something the code never promised. Refine the model.
  • The model is at the wrong grain. It is asking a question at a level of abstraction that cannot see the answer. Build a different model at the grain the property lives at.
  • The code is wrong. The model is right, the code disagrees, and the modeling just found a bug.

The discipline is to decide which before you act. Fixing code when the model was wrong bakes in a false claim; refining the model when the code had a bug hides the bug behind an adjusted spec.

4.2.5 Approach two: bottom-up, inducing the model from code

The second approach starts from the code and flies upward. Ask the agent to induce the model: "look at the codebase and help me develop the model inductively." Where the first approach starts from your coarse whiteboard understanding and refines down toward the code, this one starts on the ground and rises. Think of standing on a street and flying a drone up: the individual buildings blur, the street grid appears, then the highways, then the cities. As you climb, the levels of abstraction reveal themselves — and whether you came top-down or bottom-up, you are trying to meet in the middle, at the level that lets you enforce the properties you care about.

Which level that is depends on the assurance you need. Finer models expose more implementation detail and often support stronger local claims, but the right grain is the coarsest one that can express the property you need — and a finer model costs more to write and to run without always buying a stronger guarantee. For low assurance, just run the tests — the coarsest model is "this input, that output, checked dynamically." For the strongest guarantees, you need elaborate analyses over the control-flow graph and every function's inputs and outputs — expensive, because the checker must search every acre of the whole county. So begin by writing down what correct even means, because correctness is not one thing: it means one thing for security, another for semantics, another for privacy, and the definition tells you the abstraction level you need. Memory safety demands a fine-grained model that sees every access — and you will pay for it. A semantic property is often far cheaper.

Think of an audit trail on a spreadsheet — an accountant proving the books were not cooked: "every modification is stamped." You do not model every function to enforce that. You just require that every code path which modifies the ledger calls the stamp routine on the way out — a property about the order of two calls in the control-flow graph, not about what either call does. That is a high level of abstraction and therefore cheap to enforce. The companion catalogue ships this exact pattern as a product sensor: a lint that checks every format-mutating routine is wired to its provenance stamp, so an unstamped mutation cannot ship. The catalogue names this mechanism the provenance-stamp wiring lint. Proving the stamp routine itself is correct would need a much finer model — but if a human review or a test covers that, the coarse model is enough. Choosing the fitness function, then the abstraction that enforces it cheaply, is the new job, and it varies by codebase. It is yours to think through.

4.2.6 Approach three: lint, cover, then induce

Both approaches assume you can make sense of the code — and often you cannot, because it is a mess. Agents induce models from code more reliably when the code presents named abstractions, rationale, and shallow control structure. Deep nesting has to be held in working memory, and an agent pays for that context just as a human pays in confusion. So before you extract models from spaghetti, improve the code — and there is a loop for that, the same loop from Engineering the Loop. Define the inputs, the judgment, the output, and the metric. The metric here is a smell detector.

A smell is code that works but will break the moment it is touched, because no one can reason over it. You measure smells with cheap static analyses — lints (the dryer-lint kind, the stuff you pull out to make things tidy) — that parse the syntax tree and count the tells: a high density of bare ints and strings, deeply nested conditionals. In ordinary hand-maintained product code these are strong presumptive smells: a 2,000-line function, a module imported by every other file, a switch with 400 string cases, structs nested three anonymous levels deep, functions returning unnamed many-element tuples. Exempt the legitimate cases explicitly — a generated parser, a large dispatch table, a domain-specific case split can each be large for a real reason. So run the lints, and have the agent fix them in a behavior-preserving way — which means you need tests. If you have none, start there: have the agent write tests for line or branch coverage, module by module. Coverage supplies a floor for refactoring; the behavioral assertions and invariant checks inside those tests determine how trustworthy the floor is. Use the tests and the lints together as your success metric.

Audit, Drain, Promote

Figure 4.2-2 draws how a lint is worn into a legacy tree without breaking any in-flight agent — the order is what keeps it safe.

Then comes the move that makes it stick: as each lint reaches zero, make it blocking. Now the success metric is "all tests pass and that lint still passes" — no more unnamed tuples, no more imports buried mid-function. This is the Alignment Thesis worn into a legacy tree one lint at a time: each promotion is a governance mechanism that bounds what any later agent can reintroduce. Order matters, and the companion repository holds to it deliberately — flipping a lint to blocking while findings remain would break every agent's commit at once, so the figure's sequence is the safe one: audit-only, drain to zero, then promote — the Surface-Before-Authority strategy applied at the grain of a single lint. Once blocking, that mechanically recognized form cannot return unnoticed — a lint catches the syntactic class it recognizes, so a smell reintroduced in a shape the lint does not match still needs a human to see it. When you are done, you have cleaned-up spaghetti with enough shape that model induction has something to grip: classes and enums and named structs give the induction real structure to reason over, the structure that spaghetti hid. A companion mechanism closes the loop from the other side: it projects your test coverage onto the model's nodes, so an untested invariant shows up as a visible gap rather than hiding inside a line-coverage percentage. The catalogue names it coverage-to-model mapping.

The metric that points at what you missed

Induction gives you a first model, but it never tells you where it stops. You built the map of the concurrency-critical spine and the durable-state contracts; the rest of the code sits below the horizon, and nothing in the model announces its own edges. You need an instrument that points at the blank spots. The Missing Model Metric is that instrument, and it runs as the dual of coverage: operationally, it reverses the traceability walk — coverage walks model→code, this walks code→model.

Coverage, run backwards

Coverage walks one direction: model to code. For each thing you modelled, find its code and ask whether a test touches it — that finds the modelled but untested. The Missing Model Metric walks the other way, code to model. For each test, reach the production code it exercises and ask whether any of that code traces up to a model node — an invariant, a state machine, a service edge. A test whose code reaches no model node is an orphan. Cluster the orphans by the unmodelled code they share, and you hold a candidate list of the models you have not written yet.

The duality is what makes the metric a modeling instrument rather than a testing one.

Coverage measures implementation. Orphan rate measures understanding.

You move a coverage number by adding a test. You move this one by adding a model. An orphan is not a hole in your test suite; it is a hole in your map. So the metric seeds the modeling backlog directly: it reads out where the induced model still owes the system a view.

Data from the case study

A pilot makes the shape concrete. Run it over the most heavily-modelled slice you have — for this system, the dispatch and orchestration subsystem, across 144 tests — and you would expect near-zero orphans, because if any subsystem traces cleanly this one should. It reported 56% of those tests as orphans: more than half the tests in the best-modelled corner reach production code that traces to no invariant, state machine, or service edge. The percentage counts tests; the clustering that follows regroups those orphan tests by the unmodelled code they share. A second pass sharpened that from a scolding into a map. Every orphan reached code the model did know — anchored at the coarse structural level, where a component owns a directory — but that carried no fine behavioral model. The structural map was complete; the behavioral map covered the concurrency-critical spine and stopped. The metric had found a granularity gap, and it named exactly where to close it: three genuine candidate models — the durable-state data layer, the subprocess-and-cost-rollup pipeline, the chunk merge-and-validation gate.

What earns the instrument its trust is that it discriminates. Two of the clusters it surfaced were not missing models. One was code below the model's grain — typed-id aliases, config loaders, enums — code that carries no behavior of its own to model. The other was a missing anchor: code that belongs to a node the model already has, just lacking the link back to it. The metric separates the three: missing model, missing anchor, and legitimately below the grain. Without that discrimination it would flag every unmodelled line and drown you. With it, the orphan clusters read as a work queue you can act on.

I ran the metric on my own system, as the last rung of the road in The Road to MAGE. There it drove a loop that closed the first several clusters and, in the process, caught real shipping bugs the passing tests had missed — the brownfield recipe applied by the codebase to itself.

You will need all three approaches in combination, run inside the strangler shape — the old estate keeps working while the governed one grows up around it. There is no magic in them, only hard work and judgment. But applied together, and scaled to the quality of your starting point, they land you at the destination: the right models, encoding the right views, with the right correctness criteria, traceable to where each is enforced — statically, dynamically, or by a model check.

The brownfield loop, in five moves

The three approaches run as one repeating loop, and it is short enough to keep on a card:

  1. Find the stable abstractions — the spine the system's quality case actually rests on, not every function.
  2. Model only those — top-down from intent, bottom-up from code, meeting at the grain the property lives at.
  3. Drift-gate the model — a lint or check that fails when code and model disagree, worn in audit-only → drain → blocking.
  4. Measure the orphans — run the Missing-Model Metric; each orphan cluster is the next model you owe.
  5. Repeat — the metric hands you the next stable abstraction, and the loop turns again.

This is the modeling loop, and it is the ladder-climb made concrete — one rung per turn, taken when a recurring question earns it, never for the view; the wiki track that feeds it — Audit, Synchronize, Govern, Extend — is compressed on the companion From-Drifted-Wiki-to-Trusted-Model operator card.

I owe you honesty about the evidence here. This is a solo field report, not a controlled study. I did not run the migration twice, once with governance and once without, and measure the delta — I had one codebase and one pass through it, so I cannot hand you a clean before-and-after number. Take what follows as lived experience, weighed as such.

How much governance is enough

Which raises the real question: how much governance is enough? There is no formula. There is a judgment, and it turns on two factors multiplied together — how much a failure costs you, times how often it happens. The costlier and the more frequent the failure, the harder the mechanism it earns — a blocking lint, a gate, a structured model that makes the mistake impossible. Low times low you leave to judgment; the middle band is where taste lives, and where you will spend it.

Draw the two factors as axes and the judgment gets a shape. Table 4.2-1 crosses how much a failure costs against how often it happens, and each cell names the proportionate move — the smallest change that still closes the class. Two of the cells carry the part people skip. In the costly column, prefer architectural prevention — make the mistake impossible — but use a gate as the immediate floor and a permanent backstop where architecture is slow to arrive or cannot fully foreclose the risk. And the costly-and-frequent corner earns both — the structured model that forecloses the mistake and a gate that holds the line while the model is still spreading. The reflex to reach for a control on every failure is the expensive habit; float the larger scheme, and let the cost of the failure justify building it.

Inset — When to stop: price the upkeep

Price the mechanism's own upkeep before building it. A control guarding a failure that is both rare and cheap costs more attention to maintain than the failure ever costs you — the first brick in the teetering tower The Skills warns against. Reach for the smallest change that closes the whole class, and float the larger scheme until its cost is justified.

4.2.7 Field note — the DocAble retrofit month

What follows is one engineer's account of running this chapter's recipe on his own system — lived experience, not a controlled result. Read it as evidence for the procedure above, not more of the procedure.

The retrofit itself — an agent told to type Python that reached for string everywhere, a 300,000-line Rube Goldberg machine pared to 200,000 modeled lines in a month — is told in full in Part 5 (The Evidence). What matters here is the method that produced it.

I did it the way this chapter describes. I turned on every lint from Ruff, Pylint, and ESLint — hundreds of commodity smell checks — and for several weeks burned four Claude Max subscriptions squashing them, making each blocking as it hit zero so it could never come back. I knew the coarse architecture I wanted — a clean model-view-controller decomposition, a reactive web stack friendly to auto-scaling — and said "pursue this model," but had to get there iteratively: find the dense primitive regions, induce structure there, inch toward the target.

A dense primitive region is a stretch of code doing many low-level operations by hand — raw library calls, strings and ints passed around untyped, tree-walking spelled out inline — with no abstraction that owns them. I found them the crude way, reading for the clots: where the primitive calls piled up thickest, the missing model was loudest. Inducing structure means extracting a model that owns those primitives — one class the raw calls route through — and rewriting the region to go through it. You work region by region, because each induced model gives the next something to lean on, and the target architecture assembles out of the pieces. Each lint I drove to zero was one primitive smell given a name and a home; the model-view-controller decomposition is the same move at the coarse grain. Now I have the models, the architecture, the code that implements it, and enforcement holding all three together every time the code changes.

In MAGE terms, that month was governance conversion at brownfield scale: the recurring failures and the same rediscoveries, over and over, became the models, links, lints, and gates that now shape every later task.‡‡ Governance conversion turns a recurring failure class into a mechanism the environment applies for you, so a later agent meets the check instead of rediscovering the failure. Shopify reached the same loop from the other side, turning each recurring agent failure into a change to the shared substrate rather than a better prompt.

That retrofit is one instance of a move any team can run at any starting rung: you do not wait for a clean environment, you begin from the one you already own and mature it in place until an agent can reason through it.
