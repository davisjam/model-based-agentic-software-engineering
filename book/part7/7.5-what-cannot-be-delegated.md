# What Must Not Be Delegated?

Making implementation cheaper does not make software engineering easy; it changes where the difficulty lies. This chapter has followed that change from software work to engineering traditions, beyond software to other consequential professions, and finally to the engineer’s own expertise and authority. Across these settings, the same distinction persists. Work can be delegated, and authority to make particular decisions can be granted to people or mechanisms. Responsibility for the resulting engineering arrangement remains with those who authorize and govern it.

<!-- point: profession-reorganizes-around-responsibility-for-the-whole | Education must develop people who can take responsibility for the engineered whole even when much of the work through which earlier generations learned is delegated. -->

The profession cannot define itself by a stable residue of tasks left to humans; those tasks will continue to move as capabilities improve. Better machines may eventually construct models, evaluate evidence, propose controls, and exercise consequential judgment. The educational challenge is to develop people who can take responsibility for the engineered whole, even when much of the work through which earlier generations learned is delegated. What matters is whether people and institutions retain sufficient understanding, control, and authority to remain answerable for the resulting systems.
