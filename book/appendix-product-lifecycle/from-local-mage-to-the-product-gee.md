When useful local representations connect across lifecycle surfaces, they can form a product-wide Governed Engineering Environment (GEE): an environment in which product intent, system knowledge, operational experience, and assurance obligations become available to reasoning and authority across the life of the product. This does not require a universal product model or centralized knowledge system. The product GEE emerges from connections among representations that remain suited to their own engineering purposes.

Each lifecycle surface can develop only the representations that repay their cost. Product Discovery preserves why a capability matters; Engineering exposes the semantics needed to realize it; Product Management records the intent and acceptance conditions of later changes; Operations represents where the realization runs and what happens to it; and Assurance records obligations and the evidence supporting their satisfaction. These models should not be collapsed into one universal representation because they answer different engineering questions. They can, however, share identity, relationships, provenance, temporal scope, and obligations. A requirement can concern a feature; a ticket can authorize a change to a component; a deployed service can realize that component; an incident can expose a violated assumption; the resulting invariant can constrain later engineering and support an assurance claim. The integration is neither a lifecycle pipeline nor necessarily a single physical knowledge system. Operational evidence can revise an engineering assumption; an incident can create a maintenance ticket; Assurance can introduce an obligation that changes architecture; Product Discovery can invalidate a requirement. Meanwhile, a ticket system, architecture registry, source repository, telemetry platform, requirements system, and evidence store can remain distinct. What matters is that consequential relationships can be recovered reliably enough to answer engineering questions that cross them. Shared identifiers, typed relationships, provenance, indexes, APIs, or a knowledge layer can provide that connective tissue.

The result changes what a short instruction or question can mean because the environment supplies context that would otherwise have to be reconstructed inside each reasoning episode:

- *Implement ticket X.* The environment can supply the product decision that motivated the change, the architecture it touches, the obligations governing those components, the incidents that made particular constraints important, and the evidence required before the change can be admitted.
- *Why does the system behave this way? Can we change it?* The environment can traverse from implementation through architecture and change history to product intent, then across dependencies, operational constraints, assurance obligations, tests, and migration history. The same connected knowledge can turn *What did this incident teach us?* into a question about which models and mechanisms future work should inherit.

This is the product-wide possibility of MAGE, not its prerequisite. Every representation, relation, and mechanism has a carrying cost and should repay it through better reasoning, reduced reconstruction, stronger evidence, safer autonomy, cheaper recovery, or some other capacity inherited by later work. MAGE can therefore begin anywhere engineering knowledge or obligation repays durable treatment. Product-wide MAGE, if it emerges, emerges by connecting useful local representations and mechanisms across the lifecycle—not by specifying the whole product in advance.
