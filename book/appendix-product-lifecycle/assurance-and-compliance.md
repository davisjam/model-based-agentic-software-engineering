**Engineering question.** What must we be able to demonstrate about the product?

Assurance and compliance organize engineering knowledge around obligations and claims. Requirements, standards, policies, security threats, safety hazards, and invariants describe properties for which merely producing an implementation is insufficient: the organization must establish that the resulting product satisfies them. This makes Assurance particularly dependent on Alignment, but its scope is broader than checking either models or code in isolation.

Some assurance questions are naturally stated and analyzed over models. A security analysis may reason over a data-flow diagram to determine where sensitive information can travel, or over an access-control model to determine which principals should be able to perform which actions. Those models expose properties that would be expensive to reconstruct repeatedly from implementation. Other assurance questions attach more directly to the realization. Memory-safety analysis, for example, must establish properties of the code that actually executes. In a system whose implementation is not completely generated from authoritative models, assurance therefore spans both the models that expose consequential properties and their realization in the implemented system.

Neither side is sufficient by itself. Showing that an access-control model has the desired property does not establish that the implementation correctly realizes that model; inspecting implementation alone may make the intended access-control policy difficult to recover in the first place. Assurance must therefore move in both directions. Models make intended properties explicit and tractable for reasoning, while bidirectional traceability and Alignment help establish correspondence between those properties and their realization. Some claims can be evaluated over models, some over implementation, and some require evidence connecting the two. Traceability also connects assurance outward across the lifecycle: a compliance requirement may originate in regulation or product policy, constrain an architectural model and implementation components, require evidence from tests or operational measurements, and ultimately support a claim supplied to an auditor or other authority.

Assurance therefore creates pressure against degrees of freedom. The more consequential behavior is determined by authoritative models and trustworthy transformations, the less independently authored realization remains to inspect. At the limit, generated implementation can shift much of the assurance burden toward the governing models, their properties, and the transformations that realize them. Eliminating realization freedom is not free, however. It requires richer models, moves more engineering decisions into those models, and can make later changes expensive when a new obligation cuts across choices that have already been fixed. The appropriate assurance posture therefore depends partly on the economics of explicitness. Some properties justify tightly constraining realization; others are cheaper to establish by inspecting or analyzing the realized system.

The same tradeoff appears at the determinization frontier. A structured obligation may expose a predicate that a checker, proof, validator, test, static analysis, or model checker can evaluate repeatedly. Other obligations remain statistical, semantic, or normative and continue to require human judgment. The goal is not to mechanize assurance indiscriminately, but to establish each claim at the representation level where it can be evaluated responsibly and to connect the resulting evidence into an argument about the product as realized.

Agentic realization may also force a return from lightweight code review toward formal inspection.[^fagan] Contemporary review practices assume that human-produced changes arrive at a rate and scale that peers can inspect through diffs, comments, tests, and local judgment. Agent-generated changes stress that assumption: realization can be produced faster than reviewers can reconstruct intent, relevant system context, and the obligations a change must satisfy. MAGE therefore predicts that inspection may again become a distinct engineering activity, organized around explicit obligations, models, traceability, procedures, and evidence rather than an informal second look at code.

Such inspection would not merely examine a diff. An inspector might begin from a ticket or assurance claim, traverse to the governing architectural, behavioral, security, or other models, inspect their realization where necessary, and determine whether the available evidence establishes both correspondence and satisfaction. This resembles industrial inspection in the separation of production from verification, but software inspection remains deeply technical: the inspector may need to reason across requirements, models, source code, analyses, and operational evidence. As realization becomes cheaper, assurance can therefore consume a larger fraction of engineering effort and support greater specialization. Programming competence remains valuable, but increasingly as a prerequisite for understanding and evaluating realization rather than solely for producing it.

This makes Assurance cross-cutting rather than the final lifecycle stage. Its obligations can constrain discovery, engineering, maintenance, and operation, while its evidence can originate from all four. The smallest useful adoption is correspondingly modest: choose one consequential obligation, identify what must be established over models and realization, connect the relevant artifacts through traceability, and mechanize evidence collection or admission where the claim is sufficiently decidable.

**MAGE profile.**

- *Characteristic models.* Requirements, policies, threats and hazards, invariants, traceability, assurance cases, claims, and evidence models.
- *Lifetime.* Usually system- or organizational-lived, although individual evidence can be change- or execution-scoped.
- *Alignment posture.* Strong. Assurance may require evidence over models, over their realization, and over the correspondence between them.
- *Role of autonomous reasoning.* Interpreting semantic obligations, reasoning across modeled and realized properties, maintaining traceability, assembling evidence, identifying gaps, and evaluating claims that machinery cannot settle.
- *Determinization opportunity.* High for decidable claims over either models or implementation; limited where interpretation or normative judgment remains necessary.
- *Degrees of freedom.* Realization choices left unconstrained because they are irrelevant to the assurance obligation or cheaper to inspect than to specify in advance.
- *Smallest useful adoption.* Select one consequential obligation, identify what must be established over models and realization, connect those artifacts through traceability, and automate evidence or admission where the claim is decidable.
- *Lifecycle connections.* Assurance carries obligations across the lifecycle and draws evidence from product decisions, engineering models, implementation, change history, and operational behavior.

[^fagan]: Formal software inspection has a substantial history. Michael Fagan's work at IBM distinguished inspection from informal review by treating it as a systematic verification activity with defined roles, procedures, and recorded results. The analogy here is structural rather than procedural: MAGE does not propose restoring the original inspection process, but predicts renewed pressure to formalize inspection as realization becomes cheaper and more abundant. See Fagan 1976 [cite: fagan1976inspections] and Fagan 1986 [cite: fagan1986advances].
