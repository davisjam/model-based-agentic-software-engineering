The five surfaces need not mature together.

A startup may operate largely through structured tickets and conventional CI while using stronger MAGE machinery only around payments and customer data. A mature service organization may have sophisticated operational models, telemetry, runbooks, and deployment gates while product discovery remains intentionally informal. A regulated product may begin with assurance and traceability. A brownfield organization may begin with architecture because repeated semantic reconstruction dominates the cost of change.

These are not incomplete versions of MAGE awaiting product-wide rollout. They are rational local configurations if the investment pays.

The governing question remains:

What engineering knowledge or obligation is expensive or consequential enough to deserve durable representation or authority?

The answer can change over the life of the product. A prototype initially warrants little durable structure and later becomes critical infrastructure. A security incident reveals an obligation previously treated casually. Repeated architectural reconstruction eventually costs more than maintaining an explicit representation. A runbook used often enough becomes worth turning into deterministic automation.

Adoption therefore spreads surface by surface and obligation by obligation.

Nor is MAGE a maturity ladder whose endpoint is maximal modeling. The five profiles above deliberately point in different directions. Discovery may retain extensive semantic judgment. Operations may aggressively determinize recurring work. Engineering may invest heavily in semantic models while preserving realization DoF. Assurance may combine highly deterministic checks with irreducibly human interpretation.

The objective is not apparatus. It is an economical allocation of knowledge and authority.

Broader adoption becomes interesting when independently useful representations begin to connect.
