The five surfaces need not mature together. A startup may operate largely through structured tickets and conventional CI while using stronger MAGE machinery only around payments and customer data. A mature service organization may have sophisticated operational models, telemetry, runbooks, and deployment gates while product discovery remains intentionally informal. A regulated product may begin with assurance and traceability; a brownfield organization may begin with architecture because repeated semantic reconstruction dominates the cost of change. These are not incomplete versions of MAGE awaiting product-wide rollout. They are rational local configurations when the investment pays.

The governing question is therefore not how much MAGE an organization has adopted, but what engineering knowledge or obligations are expensive or consequential enough to deserve durable representation or authority. The answer can change over the life of the product. A prototype may initially warrant little durable structure and later become critical infrastructure. A security incident may expose an obligation previously treated casually. Repeated architectural reconstruction may eventually cost more than maintaining an explicit representation. A runbook used often enough may become worth turning into deterministic automation. Adoption can therefore spread surface by surface and obligation by obligation as the economics change.

MAGE is consequently not a maturity ladder whose endpoint is maximal modeling or determinization. The profiles above point in different directions. Discovery may preserve substantial semantic judgment because uncertainty is part of the work. Engineering may invest heavily in explicit system models while preserving realization degrees of freedom. Operations may aggressively determinize recurring work as procedures become stable. Assurance may combine highly deterministic checks with irreducibly human interpretation. In each case, the appropriate configuration depends on what knowledge must be available for reasoning, which obligations deserve authority, and what it costs to maintain the resulting structure. The objective is not apparatus. It is an economical allocation of knowledge, judgment, and authority.

Broader adoption becomes interesting only when independently useful investments begin to connect. A requirement represented for product reasons may later govern engineering; an architectural model built to reduce reconstruction may make incident diagnosis cheaper; an operational failure may expose an invariant worth enforcing during later changes; assurance evidence may reuse relationships created elsewhere. None of these benefits requires product-wide MAGE in advance. But once local investments can inherit from and contribute to one another, their value can extend beyond the surface that originally justified their cost. H.8 considers that possibility: the emergence of a product-wide governed engineering environment from useful local representations and mechanisms.
