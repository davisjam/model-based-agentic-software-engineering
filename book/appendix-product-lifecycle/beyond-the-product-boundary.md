The preceding sections have treated the product as the boundary within which MAGE compounds. That boundary is useful, but it is not fundamental. Engineering capital may be useful across products. Commodity intelligence may also allow one engineer to govern a substantially larger realization surface. These possibilities raise questions about reuse, ownership, and organization beyond the lifecycle of any one product.

The discussion that follows is conjectural. The preceding sections configure MAGE around recognizable engineering activities and artifacts; the consequences considered here are less established. MAGE provides a way to reason about them, not evidence that engineering organizations will necessarily develop this way.

## Reusing Engineering Capital

Engineering capital need not stop at the product boundary. Some models are intrinsically local: the state of one product, the rationale for one change, or the topology of one deployment. Others represent knowledge shared by many products: a security policy, regulatory obligation, protocol, accessibility requirement, architectural convention, or recurring assurance argument. Alignment mechanisms can have similar scope. A validator, checker, evidence procedure, or admission policy developed for one product may encode an obligation that another product also bears.

Software engineering has made versions of this promise before. Object-oriented and component-based development inspired visions of reusable libraries from which future systems could increasingly be assembled. Design patterns pursued a related ambition at the level of engineering knowledge: recurring design judgment could be named and carried from one system to another. In practice, the legacy was at least as much a shift in engineering principles as direct reuse: encapsulation, interfaces, composition, separation of concerns, and recurring design patterns became part of how engineers structure software, while application-specific context and judgment remained difficult to package and transfer.

Commodity intelligence changes an important premise. It separates general reasoning capacity from the particular context and engineering judgment required for a task. Code reuse carries an implementation forward. Models can carry forward represented context. What might be called judgment as code carries forward engineering decisions about obligations, evidence, and acceptable action. Alignment can then give selected judgments authority over new realizations. Design patterns had already separated some reusable judgment from its realization; commodity intelligence means that the consumer of that judgment need no longer be exclusively human.

This creates the possibility of reuse without prescribing a common realization. A reusable security obligation, protocol model, accessibility rule, or assurance procedure can govern different implementations while leaving their remaining degrees of freedom open. In this sense, the reusable asset may bound realization rather than supply it. A library gives later engineering something already built; reusable judgment can give later engineering something already learned.

Whether this makes engineering judgment substantially more portable remains an empirical question. Context still travels badly. A judgment separated from the assumptions that made it valid can be worse than no reuse at all. Cross-product MAGE therefore requires scope, provenance, applicability, and dependencies sufficient to determine when another product may responsibly inherit an asset. The old dream of reusable engineering knowledge may become more economical under commodity intelligence, but MAGE does not establish that it will.

## What Does an Engineer Own?

Commodity intelligence may also change the practical unit of engineering ownership. The conservative effect is one of scale. An engineer supported by autonomous realization may responsibly own more components, more changes, or a larger subsystem than before. Component ownership remains recognizable; the component simply becomes larger.

A stronger possibility follows when realization is no longer the dominant scarce activity. Engineers may increasingly own engineering concerns whose realizations cross conventional component boundaries: an architectural relation, authorization model, lifecycle protocol, resource invariant, accessibility obligation, deployment policy, or assurance claim. The implementation realizing such an obligation may span many components, while the engineering judgment defining and governing it remains coherent. Component ownership would not disappear, but it would no longer be the only natural way to partition responsibility.

Whether this is a change in kind or merely in scale is unclear. When implementation was expensive, organizational responsibility naturally clustered around bodies of implementation that people could feasibly understand and change. Commodity intelligence relaxes that constraint without removing the need for engineering judgment. If modeling, judgment, and responsibility remain scarce while realization becomes cheaper, some ownership boundaries may come to follow the knowledge and obligations engineers maintain rather than only the code agents realize.

## The Diversity Problem

Larger ownership domains have a less obvious cost. Large engineering projects currently aggregate the judgment of many people. Some of that multiplicity is coordination overhead, but some is useful diversity: different engineers notice different assumptions, bring different experience, challenge different decisions, and recognize different failure modes. If autonomous realization allows fewer people to govern the same engineering scope, coordination costs may fall while this incidental diversity of judgment falls with them.

A GEE should not be assumed to replace that diversity merely because it contains more engineering knowledge. Models preserve what has been represented; they do not guarantee that the right questions were asked. Alignment gives selected obligations authority; it does not discover every unknown or tacit obligation. Reducing the number of people engaged with a system could therefore make some tacit knowledge harder to discover and some apparent degrees of freedom harder to distinguish from obligations nobody has yet articulated.

This raises a further design question: whether governed engineering environments will need deliberate mechanisms for obtaining independent perspectives that larger teams once supplied incidentally. Independent human review, heterogeneous agents, adversarial reasoning, or independent sources of evidence might provide such mechanisms. Which mechanisms work, and where they are necessary, remains an empirical question. But if commodity intelligence allows fewer people to govern more engineering, preserving sufficient diversity of judgment may itself become an engineering obligation.
