Much of this book explains MAGE through the realization and evolution of software: models make consequential knowledge explicit, Alignment gives selected obligations authority, and agents realize changes inside the resulting governed engineering environment. A software product, however, is shaped across a broader lifecycle. Product staff discover what should be built; engineers realize and evolve it; operators run the resulting system and investigate its failures; assurance and compliance work establishes claims about its quality and obligations.

These activities ask different questions of the same product and therefore require different representations.

When they purposefully preserve the information needed for an engineering question, a product hypothesis and a state machine are both models in the sense used by this book. So are a well-structured ticket, an operational topology, a runbook, an incident timeline, or an assurance case. They differ in purpose, structure, lifetime, authority, and the kinds of reasoning they support.

More importantly, the MAGE configuration differs with them. Product discovery may rely heavily on semantic representations while deliberately leaving authority with people. Engineering may benefit from architecture, state, contract, and dependency models that expose system semantics to agents and validators. Maintenance may organize work around change-scoped tickets connected to longer-lived models. Operations may rely increasingly on runbooks and deterministic procedures as recurring judgment becomes mechanizable. Assurance and compliance may organize knowledge around obligations, claims, traceability, and evidence. There is therefore no canonical portfolio of MAGE models or fixed division of labor between agents and deterministic machinery.

Nor does MAGE need to arrive as a product-wide transformation. Usually it should not. Models and mechanisms have construction and carrying costs, and their return varies by engineering surface. An organization might first apply MAGE to ticket-driven change, architecture, operations, or one consequential compliance obligation while leaving the rest of its practice largely unchanged. The appropriate unit of investment is usually the engineering surface or obligation, not the organization or repository as a whole.

This appendix shows how that differential adoption might work across five recurring activities:

1. Product Discovery
2. Engineering & Realization
3. Product Management & Maintenance
4. Operations & Incidents
5. Assurance & Compliance

Organizations divide this work differently, and information flows repeatedly backward as well as forward. The decomposition is useful because each activity poses a different reasoning problem. MAGE should be configured accordingly.
