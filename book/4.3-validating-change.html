Part 4 — The MAGE Method  ::  Chapter 21

4.3 Validating Change

Every engineering process eventually reaches the same question: How do we know this change is acceptable? Classical software engineering answers that through some combination of code review, testing, and experienced engineering judgment. Those remain essential, but they become insufficient once implementation becomes abundant. When autonomous systems can propose dozens of substantial changes in the time a human once wrote a single feature, confidence must increasingly come from explicit engineering evidence rather than repeated human inspection.

MAGE broadens what counts as engineering evidence. Executable models expose engineering intent. Invariants define what must remain true. Validators and gates check those obligations automatically. Domain-specific metrics measure whether important properties have improved or degraded. Together these mechanisms allow a governed engineering environment—not merely the reviewing engineer—to determine whether a proposed change satisfies its obligations. Notice that agents do not disappear. They simply become the reason this engineering question has become urgent rather than the subject of the chapter itself.

4.3.1 What does it mean to validate a change?

To validate a change is to assemble evidence that it satisfies its obligations — and to refuse it otherwise. The classical answers assemble that evidence one way: a person reads the diff, a suite of example tests reproduces known outputs, and an experienced engineer signs off. That is honest work and it catches a great deal. But every part of it scales with human attention, and attention is exactly what runs out when changes arrive faster than anyone can read them.

The evidence that survives that pressure has one property in common: it is independent of whoever proposed the change. Never admit a change on its author's word — human or agent — generate evidence built to falsify the claim, and let through only what survives the search. This is the central concept of the chapter, and MAGE draws that evidence from three sources beyond the reviewer's eye. An executable model exposes what the change was supposed to preserve and can itself judge whether it did. Engineering metrics measure whether the properties that matter — fidelity, accessibility, correctness, coverage — improved or degraded. Deterministic validators — tests, linters, formal checks, gates — consume both, mechanically and on every change. The three sections that follow take them in turn.

4.3.2 The model as oracle

None of the individual validation techniques in this chapter is new. Property-based testing, fuzzing, model-based testing, differential testing, and their many relatives have long histories in software engineering. MAGE does not replace them with something novel. It gives them a different role. As coding agents make implementation inexpensive, engineering effort shifts toward constructing trustworthy evidence about the resulting system. The old techniques become more central than ever — not because the techniques changed, but because the engineering problem did.

The Modeling Thesis explains the shift. Explicit models are not documentation, and they are not merely prompts for agents. They are engineering representations that humans and agents reason through instead of reasoning directly over the implementation. Once such representations exist, they become shared substrates for generation, validation, traceability, measurement, and governance. The same model that helps an agent produce a change later helps a validator judge whether that change is acceptable.

Each validation mechanism exploits a particular kind of model. Property-based testing reasons over behavioral properties. Grammar-based testing reasons over input languages. Stateful testing reasons over legal transitions. Architectural validators reason over structural models. Contract checkers reason over engineering policy. The claim is not that any one of these is fundamentally new. It is that they are instances of a broader engineering principle: explicit models create surfaces on which independent evidence can be generated and deterministic engineering decisions can be made.

So as implementation becomes abundant, validation becomes less about reading code and more about evaluating evidence. The engineer's scarce resource is no longer the ability to write another implementation. It is the ability to construct models that permit trustworthy reasoning about many implementations at once. The rest of this section reads the validation techniques through that lens, starting with the sharpest case — a test whose judge is a model, not a memorized answer.

Most tests reproduce a dot. You pick an input, you write down the output you expect, and the test asserts the two match. That is honest work, of course, and it catches a great deal; but it says nothing about the input sitting right next to the one you chose. There is a different move, and it names a whole family of techniques: generate inputs and search for a counterexample to a specification. Property-based testing, fuzzing, and the synthesis of fuzzing with a structured system model belong to that family. They share one common loop — generate, execute, judge, and shrink or diagnose — but they differ materially in three places: the generator that makes the inputs, the oracle that judges them, and the search that steers toward failure. Drawing those three axes sharply is what keeps the family from collapsing into a vague "testing with randomness" — and what keeps it from flattening the other way, into a claim that the three techniques are identical.

That move is the whole book worn as a test. The specification the generator hunts is a model — the Modeling Thesis, that the system's correctness lives in an explicit model and not in prose. And the generator re-hunts a counterexample on every change — the Alignment Thesis, that the environment keeps re-checking the agent's work against that model automatically, forever, rather than trusting a one-time review. A generative-validation test is those two theses standing in one place: a compact model, and a machine that never stops trying to break it.

This is why an engineered representation validates where code and documentation alone cannot. A model in this sense is not a Wikipedia-style knowledge graph — facts scraped and associated, consulted by probabilistic lookup, true on average. It is an engineered artifact with declared types, closed sets, and stated invariants, built to support deterministic reasoning: given the model, a check returns the same verdict every time, and that verdict has a definite meaning. Code carries the behavior but not the intent; prose carries the intent but nothing a machine can check. The engineered model carries intent in a form a validator can consume — which is exactly what makes it a source of evidence rather than a description. The richer the model, the stronger the validation it permits, because the oracle a check can appeal to is only ever as rich as the model behind it.

The move: a generator hunts a counterexample to a model

Before the three members, the shared shape. An example-based test pins a single point: given this input, expect that output. A generative test does something else — it states the property the output must satisfy across the whole space of inputs, and hands a generator the job of hunting a counterexample. Where an example gives the machine one labelled dot to reproduce, a generative test gives it the law the whole space obeys and a search for the place the code breaks it.

That difference is exactly the one that matters against an agent. Faced with a failing example, an agent will do the locally-expedient thing: special-case the path that makes that input pass. The example goes green and the bug survives, now hidden behind a branch cut to fit the test. A broadly quantified property blunts that move. There is no single case to patch: special-case the one input the generator surfaced and its neighbours — which the generator keeps drawing — still exercise the law, so the branch-cut that satisfied the example fails the next generated case. (The agent is not powerless here; it can weaken the generator or overfit the property itself. Those are the failures the coverage discipline later in this chapter is built to catch.) This is why the strategy that catches the class beats the one that catches the example, and why the generative family is what the lessons chapter means when it says to pin the invariant, not the example. Fuzz + MBSE: the structured model becomes the oracle

The richest form of validation-from-a-model turns the model itself into the judge. "Fuzz + model" is this book's practical label for one point in a well-populated space: adversarial generation judged against a structured system model as oracle. The underlying techniques overlap established model-based testing and the grammar-based and stateful-fuzzing traditions — Inset A places it. The label names a combination, not a new category.

This is the synthesis. Take the wild inputs of fuzzing and, instead of asking only "did it crash," judge the outcome against the structured model the fleet already reasons through. The model names the stable point in the specification: a closed set of legal outcome classes, an invariant predicate, a transition table. Point the malformed bytes at the model's own entry point, and classify what comes back against that declared set. A clean rejection of an illegal input is a pass — the model correctly refused it. An outcome outside the legal set — an unexpected exception, a silently corrupted structure, a state the transition table forbids — is a fail, for the claims the model explicitly represents. The oracle is now as rich as the model is, and the inputs are as wild as a fuzzer's. So the trade-off the simpler validators live under is weakened, though not abolished: the richer oracle is a model you now have to build and keep true to the system.

Two instances make this concrete.

The first is the format model. Feed adversarial bytes to the structured document model's read entry point and sort the result into a small, closed set of legal outcome classes — parsed cleanly, rejected cleanly with a typed error, and so on. The set is the specification. An outcome the set does not name is the failure, and because the set is the model's own declaration rather than a hand-written assertion per seed, the same oracle judges every input the fuzzer can throw.

The second turns the fuzzer's usual approach inside out. Call it the producer-dialect corpus. Instead of mutating toward malformed bytes, round-trip a real document through a genuine third-party producer — a different office suite, a different PDF writer, a different export path — and let that producer's legal-but-unusual dialect be the adversarial input. Every producer emits its own accent within the format's grammar: unusual-but-valid object orderings, obscure-but-permitted structures, features the specification allows and your own writer never uses. Where the specification-point fix reasons inward from a failing seed to the stable rule it violated, the producer-dialect corpus reasons outward — it generates inputs that occupy the whole specification-allowed producer space, so the model is tested against the full breadth of what the format legally permits rather than the narrow slice your own tooling happens to emit. Strictly it is corpus-based interoperability testing — differential robustness against real dialects rather than mutational fuzzing — a sibling technique within the generative-validation family; but it needs no mutation engine at all, and that is what makes it distinctive: the world's producers are the generator.

Harvest producers before you hand-roll a grammar. A grammar you write yourself covers the dialects you thought to encode. The format's independent producers — every rival tool that writes it — have already generated the legal-but-unusual space for you, a breadth no single authored grammar reaches. Reach for a from-scratch generator only where no real producer exists.

Inset — What are model-based and stateful testing?

Write down a small model of what the system should do — a set of legal outcomes, or a state machine of legal transitions — and use that as the judge for generated inputs or generated sequences of calls. A stateful test drives a queue through generated sequences of enqueue and dequeue and, after each step, checks a model invariant: a dequeued item was previously enqueued, and order is preserved.

Model-based testing generates from, or checks against, an explicit model. Stateful property testing generates sequences of operations and checks a model invariant after each one. Grammar-based and stateful fuzzing sit in the same tradition — they steer generation with a structure rather than flat random bytes. These are established techniques with a substantial literature.

This book's "fuzz + model" names one point in that space: adversarial generation judged by a structured system model as the oracle. It is a practical label for a combination, not a new category — it overlaps model-based testing and the grammar/stateful-fuzzing traditions rather than standing apart from them.

You can now recognize when a test's judge is a model rather than a per-input assertion, and place "fuzz + model" inside the model-based-testing family rather than beside it.

The synthesis reaches one level up when the specification is not a format but a concurrency invariant. There the "input" is an interleaving of concurrent steps, and the oracle is the invariant predicate evaluated over the model's states — no two workers hold the same lease; a job never leaves a terminal state; a queued item is eventually served. The shape of the invariant decides which search earns its cost: a local, state predicate over structured inputs earns a property test, while a temporal or interleaving-dependent invariant earns a systematic or bounded search over interleavings — bounded by depth and model size, so "every reachable interleaving" always means within a stated bound (Inset B lays out the three ways to generate schedules). The payoff, learned the hard way, is that naming the invariant predicts where the bug is. Writing the predicate down forces you to state the exact condition that must hold, and the search then drives straight at the interleaving that violates it — a defect a strong-but-static unit suite walks right past. This is generative validation standing on the same invariants-over-models ground the rest of the book is built on.

Inset — Three ways to generate schedules

For a concurrency bug the "input" is an interleaving — the order in which concurrent steps happen to run. You find the bad interleaving by generating schedules, and there are three ways to do it, of rising cost and rising thoroughness.

* Randomized scheduler (a property test for concurrency). Perturb timing randomly and check the invariant after each run. Cheap, catches shallow races, and misses the rare interleaving that randomness never happens to hit. * Systematic concurrency testing. A controlled scheduler steers execution through distinct interleavings on purpose — covering orderings deliberately rather than hoping randomness reaches them. * Exhaustive or bounded state exploration. Enumerate the reachable interleavings up to a bound — a depth, a step count, a model size — and check the invariant on each. The strongest, but always bounded: "every reachable interleaving" means within the stated bound, never literally all of them for an unbounded system.

You can now name which schedule-generation method a concurrency check uses, and read the mainline routing rule: the invariant's shape — a local, state predicate versus a temporal or interleaving-dependent one — decides which of the three earns its cost.

4.3.3 Choosing the validation technique

Models expose intent and metrics measure degree; validators are the machine-read checks that consume both and return a verdict. Tests, linters, format validators, formal checks, and the gates that wire them into admission are all deterministic sensors — run them twice on the same input and they agree. The generative family gives the strongest of these. Reaching for one member over another is not a climb up a ladder from weak to strong. The members answer different engineering questions, and you pick among them by the signal in the code under test, not by a technique's sophistication.

The boundary between property-based testing and fuzzing is not even categorical. Systems such as Fandango combine explicit input-language grammars with semantic constraints, generating high-diversity inputs while keeping tight control over what each generated input means 11. José Antonio Zamudio Amaya et al., “FANDANGO: Evolving Language-Based Testing,” in “Proceedings of the ACM SIGSOFT International Symposium on Software Testing and Analysis (ISSTA 2025),” special issue, Proceedings of the ACM SIGSOFT International Symposium on Software Testing and Analysis (ISSTA 2025) (New York, NY), 2025, https://doi.org/10.1145/3728915.. So the useful question is never which technique sits above another. It is what representation of the input space, and what oracle, the engineering claim permits. Three sharp distinctions fall out of that table, and stating them keeps property testing and fuzzing from blurring together.

First, oracle richness and input wildness trade off, and the third member refuses the trade. The richer the oracle, the tamer the inputs: a property test knows exactly what correct means, and yet it asks the question only of inputs a type would allow. The wilder the inputs, the coarser the oracle: fuzzing reaches bytes nothing else reaches, and yet it can notice only a crash. Fuzz-with-a-model is the synthesis that takes both halves: wild inputs, judged against a rich model-declared oracle. It weakens the trade-off rather than abolishing it. The wild inputs now meet a richer oracle, at the standing cost of building and maintaining that oracle. This is the same two-thesis pattern from the opening: the model supplies an oracle for the claims it explicitly represents, and the search never stops hunting a counterexample to it.

Second, the word failure means different things, and conflating them is the classic mistake. For a property test, a failure is a stated law turning out false. For a fuzzer, a failure is the program leaving its safe envelope, crashing or corrupting, in a place where no law was ever stated. Blur the two and you get the two symmetric errors: a fuzzer that only checks for a crash when it should have been asserting an invariant, and a property test fed only tame inputs when the real risk was the malformed byte at the boundary.

Third, the specification's stable point is the bridge between fuzzing and the model. When a fuzzer finds a crash, there are two ways to fix it. You can patch the one input that broke — and leave every neighbouring input that would break the same way. Or you can trace the failure back to the point in the format's specification that the input violated, and fix that — so the fix covers every input the specification allows, not the single seed that happened to trip it. Fixing to the stable specification point is what promotes a raw fuzzer into a model-based one: the model is the stable point, written down.

Property-based testing: the invariant is the oracle

In property-based testing the author writes the law and the machine hunts the counterexample 22. Koen Claessen and John Hughes, “QuickCheck: A Lightweight Tool for Random Testing of Haskell Programs,” in “Proceedings of the Fifth ACM SIGPLAN International Conference on Functional Programming (ICFP 2000),” special issue, Proceedings of the Fifth ACM SIGPLAN International Conference on Functional Programming (ICFP 2000) (New York, NY), 2000, 268–79.. The law is a statement about every output, not a single one: parse-then-serialize preserves semantic equivalence; a remediated DocAble document contains no less content than the one that went in, measured over the content inventory; a sort produces a permutation of its input in non-decreasing order. Each of these is a compact, checkable model of what a correct output looks like — a law, not a list. The generator draws values from an authored domain model — the typed space the law is meant to hold over — and it can be tuned to reach the boundary and adversarial-but-valid values, not only mild ones. That authored domain is the real line between a property test and a fuzzer: values from a domain model the author describes, versus arbitrary byte-level mutation. (Inset D gives the mechanics, including what shrinking does.)

This is the same two-thesis pattern the opening drew: the property is a compact model the agent reasons over instead of enumerating cases, and the generator re-hunts a counterexample on every commit. In a real system this is no footnote technique. A property-based framework can run across every test project, and the discipline is to reach for it the moment a class's contract is expressible as a law rather than a list of examples. The counterexample it hands back is often a genuine defect the author never thought to write an example for. That is why you ask the machine to search the space instead of guessing at its corners.

Learn more about this governance mechanism: property-based tests.

Inset — What is property-based testing, and what does shrinking do?

Ordinary tests pin one input to one expected output. A property-based test states a law the output must obey for every input in a domain, then lets a generator hunt a counterexample. For a sort, the law is "the output is an ordered permutation of the input"; the framework generates hundreds of lists and checks that law on each, so you never had to guess which list would break the code.

The generator draws from an authored domain model — a description of the values the law ranges over (bounded integers, well-formed documents, lists of them) — not from arbitrary bytes. That is the real line between a property test and a fuzzer, and it is why a property generator can be tuned to include boundary and adversarial-but-valid values while still staying inside the domain.

When the law fails, the framework shrinks: it walks the failing input down to the smallest one that still breaks the law, so the counterexample you debug is a two-element list, not a thousand-node document with one bad byte buried inside. The minimized case you get back is the shrinker's work, not luck.

You can now recognize a property test by its shape — a quantified law plus a domain generator — and read a minimized counterexample as the generator having searched the space and then boiled the failure down. This is the oracle-rich, domain-bounded end of the generate–execute–judge–shrink loop; it says nothing about inputs outside the authored domain.

Fuzzing: the contract is "never crash, never corrupt"

Where a property test asks a rich question of inputs drawn from an authored domain, fuzzing asks a coarser question of arbitrary, mutated ones. Neither ranks above the other; they meet different signals. In its baseline coverage-guided form the fuzzer's oracle is the implicit robustness contract every input-parsing surface signs whether or not anyone wrote it down: do not crash, do not hang, do not corrupt, and reject cleanly whatever the format forbids. Modern fuzzing enriches that oracle — sanitizers, differential comparison, semantic assertions, metamorphic relations — but the baseline is the honest starting point, and Inset E unpacks it. A malformed DocAble upload — a PDF truncated mid-object, a slide deck with a corrupted offset table, a document with megabytes of garbage appended — is precisely the input a generator of valid documents will never produce, and precisely where the crash lives. The fuzzer mutates and adversarially perturbs its way into that space and watches for the process to fall over or the output to fail to re-parse.

The multiplier is discipline about the fix. When a fuzzer surfaces a crash, the temptation is to handle the exact bytes it found. The stronger move is to trace the failure to the point in the format's specification that the input violated, and fix that — because the specification is stable while any one producer's quirks are not, so a fix aimed at the specification point closes every input the format allows, not the single failing seed. One fix for the whole class, not a pile of one-off patches.

A campaign that merely runs has explored nothing, so the honesty layer matters: the host test-runner can auto-collect line and branch coverage over each fuzz campaign and track it against a baseline, so "we fuzzed it" becomes a claim with a number behind it rather than a vibe. A coverage plateau — the campaign no longer finding new edges — is one saturation signal, not proof of saturation: it can hide input-grammar variants or semantic states the generator never produced in the first place (Inset E and the coverage section return to this). Its deeper treatment belongs to the metrics chapter, which owns coverage.

Inset — What does coverage-guided fuzzing measure?

A fuzzer throws malformed and mutated bytes at a parsing surface and watches for it to fall over. Coverage guidance makes the throwing smart: the fuzzer keeps any input that reaches new code and breeds more variants from it. Feed a PDF reader truncated and bit-flipped files; when one input reaches a new branch in the object-stream parser, the fuzzer notices and mutates that one further.

The baseline oracle is coarse — did the process crash, hang, or blow up memory. Line and branch coverage play two roles at once: the search signal (which code the inputs reached, steering the mutation) and the saturation signal (has the campaign stopped finding new edges). A coverage plateau is only one saturation signal — it can hide inputs the generator never produced.

Modern fuzzers enrich the oracle well past crash-detection: sanitizers catch memory and undefined-behavior errors mid-run; differential oracles require two independent implementations to agree; semantic assertions and metamorphic relations check that related inputs produce related outputs. A clean re-parse, by itself, does not prove no corruption — a mangled output can still parse.

You can now read "we fuzzed it" as a claim with a coverage number behind it, and know to ask which oracle the campaign used — crash-only, or something richer. What counts as enough coverage belongs to the metrics chapter, not here.

The third member — fuzz plus a model, where the structured model itself becomes the oracle — is the richest deterministic validator, and this chapter develops it earlier, under The model as oracle, because its whole point is that the model is the judge. The format model, the producer-dialect corpus, and the interleaving search against a concurrency invariant all live there.

4.3.4 Coverage: did the validation explore the claim?

A validator answers a yes/no obligation; a metric answers a question of degree, and degree is its own kind of evidence. Did content fidelity hold — does the remediated document still say everything the original did? Did accessibility conformance improve or regress? Did latency, correctness, or a domain-specific quality measure move the right way? These are not pass/fail laws so much as measured properties, tracked against a baseline so a change that quietly degrades one of them is caught by the number rather than by a reviewer's memory. The book develops these as a flagship of its own — the metrics chapter owns the general treatment, including the discipline to measure one level deeper than the raw percentage. This section takes only the one metric native to generative validation, and hands the rest back.

Once you have a generator, a new question appears that example tests never had to ask: did the campaign explore the space that matters, or did it just spin? A campaign that merely runs has explored nothing, so coverage is the honesty layer that turns "we fuzzed it" into a claim with a number behind it rather than a vibe.

The native cut is grammar coverage. Line and branch coverage measure the code the inputs reached. Grammar coverage measures the inputs themselves: did the generator exercise every production of the input grammar — every structural variant, every mutation kind, every dialect the corpus is supposed to contain? It is branch coverage moved to the input side, and it answers the question the gap analyses of a real fuzzing effort keep circling back to: is the corpus rich enough? Three distinct measures live under that one name, and they are not interchangeable: grammar-production coverage (did the generator exercise every production of an explicit input grammar), mutation-operator coverage (did it apply every kind of mutation), and producer/dialect coverage (did the corpus carry every real producer's accent) — the last has no explicit grammar at all, so a producer corpus is measured by dialect coverage, not by productions (Inset C). A campaign that never generates a document with headers and footers, or never a cross-sheet formula, has such a hole no amount of line coverage will reveal, because the untested lines were never reached by any generated input in the first place.

Inset — What does grammar coverage count?

Code coverage asks which lines the inputs reached. Grammar coverage asks which kinds of inputs the generator actually produced. A document fuzzer that never once generates a file with headers and footers has a grammar-coverage hole — and no amount of line coverage will reveal it, because the lines that handle headers-and-footers were never reached by any generated input in the first place.

Three distinct measures hide under the one name, and they are not interchangeable: grammar-production coverage (did the generator exercise every production of an explicit input grammar), mutation-operator coverage (did it apply every kind of mutation), and producer/dialect coverage (did the corpus carry every real producer's accent). A hand-written grammar has productions to count; a corpus harvested from real producers has no explicit grammar at all, so it is measured by dialect coverage instead.

You can now ask of any campaign not just "what code did it cover" but "what inputs did it cover," and pick the right one of the three input-side measures for the generator you actually have.

Line and branch coverage of a campaign is the saturation signal — the honesty layer that keeps "we fuzzed it" from being an empty claim. But the coverage that ultimately matters for generative validation is model claims — did the generated inputs actually drive the invariants, transitions, and edges the model declares, measured as the metrics chapter's requirements-based coverage over the traceability graph? That is the connective tissue: the same "measure one level deeper" move the metrics chapter teaches, reused here as the saturation oracle for validation from a model. 4.3.5 Deterministic admission

These checks are deterministic sensors, machine-read — and they become hard governance when their verdicts control admission, wired as a commit gate that refuses the merge. The same fuzz campaign run only on a schedule, reporting to a dashboard, is a sensor but not yet a gate; it holds the line only once its verdict can block the commit. Wired that way, a generator that re-hunts a counterexample every commit is the Alignment Thesis made mechanical — the environment refusing to trust the last green checkmark, and checking the model again, forever.

4.3.6 Why this matters in the agentic era

Autonomous development does not change what engineering evidence is. A model exposed intent, a metric measured degradation, and a validator refused a bad change long before any agent proposed one. What changes is how much of that evidence we now require, and where the burden of producing it falls. When one reviewer wrote one feature a week, human inspection could carry most of the load; the models and validators were a supplement. When a fleet proposes changes faster than any person can read them, inspection cannot scale to meet the volume, and assurance has to shift onto the explicit representations and the automated checks that consume them.

So the chapter's central concept — independent evidence, generated to falsify a claim and admitting only what survives — stops being a supplement and becomes the load-bearing floor. The reviewing engineer does not disappear; the human gate on consequential decisions is one of the strongest validators there is. But the engineer's judgment is spent designing the models, choosing the metrics, and setting the gates, rather than re-reading every diff. The environment does the re-checking, on every change, forever. That is what lets confidence keep pace with abundance.

4.3.7 Industrial example

The move is one a team can copy: generate evidence built to falsify a claim, and admit only the claim that survives. It is not a DocAble idiosyncrasy. One of the teams in the Field Guide (Appendix F) reaches for exactly this move at the sharp end of the trust question — an autonomous coding fleet — where the author of a change is the last party you would trust to grade it.

Worked Examples

Docker. The concept it illustrates. Independent evidence — the grader must sit outside the reasoner whose work it judges. Docker's autonomous coding fleet builds the separation into its architecture: the agent that writes a change never grades it. What Docker did. An independent reviewer with its own model invocation, a test suite the team determinizes eagerly so its verdict is repeatable, and a human gate stand between every pull request and main. The fleet may open a pull request; it may not merge one. "That's the trust boundary, and it's deliberate" — merge, architecture, scope, and prioritization stay human. The sharpest statement of the philosophy is theirs: reviewing outcomes while skipping the changes themselves "is not a security model, it's a prayer." What to imitate. Separate the author of a change from its grader, and put the grader — a reviewer, a determinized suite, a human on the consequential call — where the author cannot reach it. The separation is the mechanism; the specific tools are interchangeable.

Takeaway. The shared move is independent evidence: never admit an agent's claim on its own word — generate evidence built to falsify it, and let through only what survives the search. Docker is evidence the move scales to a full autonomous fleet on a live product; DocAble runs the whole family at full depth against its own hard problem, proving a remediated document says no less than the original. Part V tells the DocAble story in full, and MAGE in the Wild sets Docker beside five other systems as evidence the practice travels.
