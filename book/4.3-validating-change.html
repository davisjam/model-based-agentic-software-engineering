Part 4 — The MAGE Method  ::  Chapter 19

4.3 Validating Change

Every engineering process eventually reaches the same question: How do we know this change is acceptable? Classical software engineering answers that through some combination of code review, testing, and experienced engineering judgment. Those remain essential, but they become insufficient once implementation becomes abundant. When autonomous systems can propose dozens of substantial changes in the time a human once wrote a single feature, confidence must increasingly come from explicit engineering evidence rather than repeated human inspection.

Validation is only as strong as the obligation behind it. If the requirement is settled, a validator can ask whether the change satisfies it. If the requirement is still a hypothesis — whether users want the feature at all, which workflow they prefer, which tradeoff they will accept — no test suite can manufacture that knowledge. In that regime the evidence is experimental: usage, comparison, observation, or direct customer feedback. The method can govern the experiment and hold its non-negotiable constraints; it cannot turn an unknown product decision into a mechanical truth.

4.3.1 What validation means

To validate a change is to assemble evidence that it satisfies its obligations — and to refuse it otherwise. The classical answers assemble that evidence one way: a person reads the diff, a suite of example tests reproduces known outputs, and an experienced engineer signs off. That is honest work and it catches a great deal. But every part of it scales with human attention, and attention is exactly what runs out when changes arrive faster than anyone can read them.

The evidence that survives that pressure has one property in common: it is independent of whoever proposed the change. Never admit a change on its author's word — human or agent — generate evidence built to falsify the claim, and let through only what survives the search. This is the central concept of the chapter, and the method draws that evidence from three sources beyond the reviewer's eye. An executable model exposes what the change was supposed to preserve and can itself judge whether it did. Engineering metrics measure whether the properties that matter — fidelity, accessibility, correctness, coverage — improved or degraded. Validators — the machine-read checks that consume both — evaluate that evidence on every change. The sections that follow take the model first, because it is the sharpest source: a judge that is itself a model.

4.3.2 The model as oracle

The Modeling Thesis explains why validation now leans on the model. Explicit models are not documentation, and they are not merely prompts for agents. They are engineering representations that humans and agents reason through instead of reasoning directly over the implementation. Once such representations exist, they become shared substrates for generation, validation, traceability, measurement, and governance. The same model that helps an agent produce a change later helps a validator judge whether that change is acceptable.

This is why an engineered representation validates where code and documentation alone cannot. A model in this sense is not a Wikipedia-style knowledge graph — facts scraped and associated, consulted by probabilistic lookup, true on average. It is an engineered artifact with declared types, closed sets, and stated invariants: given the model, a check returns the same verdict every time, and that verdict has a definite meaning. Code carries the behavior but not the intent; prose carries the intent but nothing a machine can check. The engineered model carries intent in a form a validator can consume — which is what makes it a source of evidence rather than a description. The richer the model, the stronger the validation it authorizes, because the oracle a check can appeal to is only ever as rich as the model behind it.

That sharpens the difference between two kinds of test. An example-based test pins a single point: given this input, expect that output. It reproduces a dot the author already knew, and says nothing about the input sitting right next to the one they chose. A generative test does something else — it states the property the output must satisfy across the whole space of inputs, and hands a generator the job of hunting a counterexample. Where the example gives the machine one labelled dot to reproduce, the generative test gives it the law the whole space obeys and a search for the place the code breaks it. Figure 4.3-1 draws the difference.

That difference is the one that matters against an agent. Faced with a failing example, an agent will do the locally-expedient thing: special-case the path that makes that input pass. The example goes green and the bug survives, hidden behind a branch cut to fit the test. A broadly quantified property leaves no single case to patch — the generator keeps drawing neighbours that still exercise the law, so the branch-cut that satisfied one input fails the next generated case. This is why the strategy that catches the class beats the one that catches the example, and it is what the lessons chapter means by pin the invariant, not the example.

A generative test is the whole book worn as a check. The specification the generator hunts is a model — the Modeling Thesis, that correctness lives in an explicit model, not in prose. And the generator re-hunts a counterexample on every change — the Alignment Thesis, that the environment keeps re-checking the work against that model automatically. A compact model, and a machine that never stops trying to break it.

The techniques that generate evidence this way — property-based testing, fuzzing, and the synthesis of the two with a structured model — share one loop: generate, execute, judge, shrink or diagnose. They differ in three places only: the generator that makes the inputs, the oracle that judges them, and the search that steers toward failure. They are not rungs on a maturity ladder. Choose the one whose generator and oracle match the engineering claim (Inset 1 lays them side by side; Inset 2 is the refresher on the property end).

Inset — Example, property, fuzzing, model-based: what changes?

Four ways to produce evidence, one shared loop — generate → execute → judge. They differ in where the inputs come from, where the verdict comes from, and what each is best at.

The boundary is not even sharp: some systems combine an explicit input-language grammar with semantic constraints, generating high-diversity inputs while keeping tight control over what each one means 11. The bottom line: these are not rungs on a maturity ladder. Choose the one whose generator and oracle match the engineering claim — which is the whole of the method applied to a single test.

Inset — What is property-based testing, and what does shrinking do?

Ordinary tests pin one input to one expected output. A property-based test states a law the output must obey for every input in a domain, then lets a generator hunt a counterexample 22. Koen Claessen and John Hughes, “QuickCheck: A Lightweight Tool for Random Testing of Haskell Programs,” in “Proceedings of the Fifth ACM SIGPLAN International Conference on Functional Programming (ICFP 2000),” special issue, Proceedings of the Fifth ACM SIGPLAN International Conference on Functional Programming (ICFP 2000) (New York, NY), 2000, 268–79.. For a sort, the law is "the output is an ordered permutation of the input"; the framework generates hundreds of lists and checks that law on each, so you never had to guess which list would break the code.

The generator draws from an authored domain model — a description of the values the law ranges over (bounded integers, well-formed documents, lists of them), not from arbitrary bytes. That is the real line between a property test and a fuzzer, and it is why a property generator can be tuned to reach boundary and adversarial-but-valid values while staying inside the domain.

When the law fails, the framework shrinks — it walks the failing input down to the smallest one that still breaks the law, so the counterexample you debug is a two-element list, not a thousand-node document with one bad byte buried inside. The minimized case is the shrinker's work, not luck.

You can now recognize a property test by its shape — a quantified law plus a domain generator — and read a minimized counterexample as the search having boiled the failure down. This is the oracle-rich, domain-bounded end of the loop; it says nothing about inputs outside the authored domain.

4.3.3 Generate falsifying evidence

The move — generate inputs and search for a counterexample — answers three different signals in the code under test, and the signal, not a technique's sophistication, picks the member.

  • A stated law over valid inputs earns a property test: a round-trip, a content-preservation guarantee, an ordering. The generator explores the valid domain; the oracle is the law you can already write down (Inset 2).
  • An untrusted-bytes boundary with a never-crash contract earns a fuzzer: it pushes outside, or toward the hostile edges of, the domain, where no per-input law is worth stating. The oracle is the robustness contract — don't crash, hang, or corrupt (Inset 3).
  • A structured model with a declared legal-outcome set or invariant earns fuzzing judged against that model. Point wild inputs at the model's own surface and classify what comes back against its declared set. The oracle is now as rich as the model and the inputs as wild as a fuzzer's (Inset 4 carries the sequence-and-state form).

Those three sit on one trade-off. The richer the oracle, the tamer the inputs a property test allows; the wilder the inputs, the coarser the oracle a fuzzer can afford. Fuzzing-against-a-model takes both halves — wild inputs, a rich model-declared oracle — at the standing cost of building and keeping that model true to the system. It weakens the trade-off rather than abolishing it.

When a fuzzer surfaces a crash there are two ways to fix it. Patch the exact bytes it found — and leave every neighbouring input that would break the same way. Or trace the failure to the point in the format's specification the input violated, and fix that, so the fix closes every input the specification allows, not the single seed that tripped it. Fixing to the stable specification point is what promotes a raw fuzzer into a model-based one: the model is that stable point, written down.

Harvest producers before you hand-roll a grammar. Every rival tool that writes your format has already generated its own legal-but-unusual dialect — unusual object orderings, obscure permitted structures, features the specification allows and your own writer never emits. Round-trip real documents through those producers and let their dialects be the adversarial corpus; the world's producers are a generator no single authored grammar reaches. Build a from-scratch generator only where no real producer exists.

The move generalizes when the thing generated is a sequence rather than a datum. A local, state predicate over structured inputs earns a property test; a temporal or interleaving-dependent invariant earns a systematic or bounded search over interleavings. The shape of the invariant determines the search — and naming the predicate is what predicts where the bug is, because writing the exact condition down lets the search drive straight at the interleaving that violates it, a defect a strong-but-static unit suite walks right past. This stands on the same invariants-over-models ground the rest of the book is built on (Inset 4 shows the sequence, state, and interleaving forms).

Inset — What fuzzing adds

Three ideas, in rising order:

  • Property testing generates valid-but-varied members of a modeled domain.
  • Fuzzing pushes outside that domain, or toward its hostile edges — truncations, bit-flips, corrupted offsets, megabytes of garbage appended: inputs no well-typed generator would emit, and exactly where the crash lives.
  • Coverage-guided fuzzing keeps any input that reaches new execution territory and breeds more variants from it, so the campaign steers itself toward unexplored code.

Search signal is not oracle. Line and branch coverage tell the fuzzer where it got — that is the search signal. They do not say the behavior was correct, and a coverage plateau is one saturation signal, not proof of saturation: it can hide input variants the generator never produced. The baseline oracle stays coarse — did the process crash, hang, or blow up memory — and a clean re-parse does not by itself prove no corruption; a mangled output can still parse.

Real fuzzers enrich that crash/hang oracle: sanitizers catch memory and undefined-behavior errors mid-run, differential oracles require two implementations to agree, and metamorphic relations check that related inputs produce related outputs.

You can now read "we fuzzed it" as a claim with a coverage number behind it, and ask which oracle the campaign used — crash-only, or something richer.

Inset — Stateful and concurrency validation

Write down a small model — a set of legal outcomes, or a state machine of legal transitions — and use that as the judge. The single-datum rule generalizes as the generated thing grows:

Single-input property   input → program → invariant
Stateful property       operation sequence → state transitions → invariant after each step
Concurrency property    interleaving → concurrent execution → invariant over shared state

A stateful test drives a queue through generated sequences of enqueue and dequeue and checks a model invariant after each step: a dequeued item was previously enqueued, and order is preserved. A concurrency test makes the interleaving the input and checks an invariant over shared state — no two workers hold the same lease; a job never leaves a terminal state.

Search strength rises with cost: random schedules sample interleavings; controlled schedulers explore them deliberately; bounded state exploration enumerates them within an explicit bound, so "every reachable interleaving" always means within a stated depth or model size, never literally all of them for an unbounded system.

You can now recognize when a test's judge is a model rather than a per-input assertion, and match the schedule-generation method to the invariant's shape — a local state predicate versus a temporal, interleaving-dependent one.

4.3.4 Did the search cover the claim?

Once you have a generator, a question example tests never had to ask appears: did the campaign explore the space that matters, or did it just spin? A campaign that merely runs has explored nothing, so coverage is the honesty layer that turns "we fuzzed it" into a claim with a number behind it. The degree question — how much coverage is enough — belongs to the metrics treatment in Operating MAGE, which owns it, including the discipline to measure one level deeper than a raw percentage. This section takes only the cut native to generation.

The native cut is that coverage of the code is not coverage of the input space. Line and branch coverage measure the implementation the inputs reached. Input-space coverage measures the inputs themselves — did the generator ever produce a document with headers and footers, a cross-sheet formula, the semantic region the claim is about? A campaign that never generates such an input has a hole no amount of line coverage will reveal, because the handling lines were never reached by any generated input in the first place. This is the chapter's tie to the book's larger concern with missing models: a validator cannot falsify a claim in a region its generator never represents (Inset 5 separates the two coverages).

Line and branch coverage keep "we fuzzed it" honest, but the coverage that ultimately matters is whether the generated inputs actually drove the invariants, transitions, and edges the model declares — model-claim coverage, measured over the traceability graph. That is the same measure-one-level-deeper move the metrics treatment teaches, reused here as the saturation oracle for validation from a model. Figure 4.3-2 draws the loop, closing on that coverage.

Inset — Coverage of the generator is not coverage of the code

Code coverage asks what implementation the campaign reached. Input-space coverage asks what kinds of cases the campaign generated. You need both.

A document fuzzer that never once generates a file with headers and footers has an input-space hole, and no amount of line coverage will reveal it: the lines that handle headers-and-footers were never reached by any generated input in the first place. High line coverage elsewhere tells you nothing about the semantic region no input ever visited.

Three input-side measures hide under the name and are not interchangeable: grammar-production coverage (did the generator exercise every production of an explicit grammar), mutation-operator coverage (did it apply every kind of mutation), and producer/dialect coverage (did the corpus carry every real producer's accent — the case with no explicit grammar to count).

You can now ask of any campaign not just "what code did it cover" but "what inputs did it cover," and pick the right input-side measure for the generator you actually have.

4.3.5 Deterministic admission

These checks are repeatable — run one twice on the same input and it agrees — and they keep Part III's roles distinct even when one artifact carries several. A sensor produces evidence; a validator evaluates it against the obligation; a gate controls admission on the verdict. A test harness bundles sensing and validation in one executable — it both runs the input and judges the result — and a continuous-integration pipeline may additionally gate on that verdict, refusing the merge. The bundling is convenient, not a collapse of the roles: naming which role a check is playing is what lets you reason about where evidence is produced, where it is judged, and where it is enforced.

A check becomes hard governance when its verdict is granted the authority to control admission. The same fuzz campaign run only on a schedule, reporting to a dashboard, is a sensor with no authority — it observes but cannot refuse. Wire its verdict into the commit gate and it holds the line. A generator that re-hunts a counterexample every commit, given that authority, is the Alignment Thesis made mechanical: the environment declining to trust the last green checkmark, and checking the model again.

4.3.6 Why agentic velocity changes the economics

None of the techniques in this chapter is new. Property-based testing, fuzzing, model-based testing, and their relatives have long histories in software engineering. The method does not replace them with something novel; it gives them a different role. As coding agents make implementation inexpensive, engineering effort shifts toward constructing trustworthy evidence about the resulting system — so the old techniques become more central than ever, not because the techniques changed, but because the engineering problem did.

Autonomous development does not change what engineering evidence is. A model exposed intent, a metric measured degradation, and a validator refused a bad change long before any agent proposed one. What changes is how much of that evidence we now require, and where the burden of producing it falls. When one reviewer wrote one feature a week, human inspection could carry most of the load. When a fleet proposes changes faster than any person can read them, inspection cannot scale to the volume, and assurance shifts onto the explicit representations and the automated checks that consume them.

So the chapter's central concept — independent evidence, generated to falsify a claim, admitting only what survives — stops being a supplement and becomes the load-bearing floor. The reviewing engineer does not disappear; a human gate on a consequential decision is one of the strongest validators there is. But the engineer's judgment is now spent designing the models, choosing the metrics, and setting the gates, rather than re-reading every diff. The environment does the re-checking, on every change. That is what lets confidence keep pace with abundance.

4.3.7 Industrial example — Docker

Worked Examples

Takeaway. The shared move is independent evidence: never admit a change on its own author's word — generate evidence built to falsify it, and let through only what survives the search. Separate the author of a change from its grader, and put the grader where the author cannot reach it; the separation is the mechanism, and the specific tools are interchangeable. Docker is evidence the move scales to a full autonomous fleet on a live product; DocAble runs the whole family at depth against its own hard problem — generating strong evidence that a remediated document says no less than the original, without claiming the certainty of a proof. Part V tells the DocAble story in full, and MAGE in the Wild sets Docker beside other systems as evidence the practice travels.
