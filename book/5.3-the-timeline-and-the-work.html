Part 5 — The Evidence  ::  Chapter 26

5.3 The Build

This chapter illustrates

✓ The Printer · ✓ The Governed Engineering Environment · ✓ Governance Conversion

The last chapter set the bar. This one is the receipt for clearing it: how the build actually went, from a five-minute experiment in the back of a committee meeting to a production system that, by the end, was writing almost all of its own code. Every governance idea in this book was learned at a specific point on that arc, in response to a specific failure. The methods are not a theory that came first. They are the sediment left by the work.

The scale is easy to state and hard to believe. Over about 20 weeks of focused effort, a fleet of AI coding agents and I produced a repository of 491,090 lines of production code, 1,501,907 lines of support apparatus, 35,323 lines of infrastructure-as-code, and 28,507 lines of system models bridging the two (counted at 2026-08-03 by the categorized line-count census; category definitions live in the ledger). On a routine day, six to eight agents ran in parallel and landed on the order of 200 commits — about 1,000 a week, sustained. I inspected almost none of the code. That one fact is the engineering problem this book exists to answer: how a fleet producing more code than any human can read stays trustworthy.

5.3.1 It started in a meeting

The project began, fittingly, in a committee meeting about the very deadline described in the last chapter. In early 2026 an accreditation committee was discussing the ADA compliance date. Most of the faculty in the room did not know a mandate existed; those who did had made little progress against it. While the meeting droned on, I did what an engineering professor does when bored by a conundrum: I ran a small experiment. I opened a chat model, fed it screenshots from one of my own slide decks, and asked it to transcribe what it saw. It worked. The model looked at a rendered slide and recovered real semantic content from the image — the core technical problem of document accessibility, solved well enough to build on.

That single positive result was the decision point. It converted a research question into an engineering project. What it did not do defines the gap the rest of the timeline had to close: the model did not export a corrected file, did not add reading order, did not validate against any standard, and understood nothing about the institution the file lived in. A flash of capability is not a system.

The first plan was small and conventional. Draft a one-week project — automated alt-text for the equations in instructional materials — and hand it to an undergraduate or two over Spring Break. I posted the offer to twenty of the department's best students. Not one replied; they had, reasonably, planned to have fun. So I decided to supervise agents instead of undergraduates. I bought a subscription, opened an empty Git repository, and told myself one month should be plenty.

5.3.2 Six stages, then a seventh

The build moved through a recognizable sequence of stages — recognizable only afterward. Each was driven by a concrete pressure: a colleague's request, a user's complaint, a deadline, a failure. And each enlarged both the product and the machinery that kept the product trustworthy. Table 5.3-1 lays out that sequence; the sections after it read the evidence it left behind.

The two-line summary of the whole sequence: the product grew, and the support apparatus around it grew faster. Measured in source size, that apparatus — testing, modeling, orchestration, documentation, and governance, plus the tooling that runs them — ran about 3.0 times the size of the production code, and the test suite alone was roughly the size of the product. A ratio like that reads as gold-plating until you recall that at 200 commits a day no human reads the diffs. The apparatus carries the repeatable review burden no human could perform per diff (more in the built system); the architectural and semantic judgment it cannot perform stays with me. It is the price of the velocity.

5.3.3 The velocity curve

Figure 5.3-1 plots commits per week: a steep rise as the fleet gained capability, then a dip that coincides with the hardening interval, when the work record shifts toward mechanisms and validation.

The repository's own activity records the shape of the effort. Commits per week rose steeply through the MVP stages — feasibility, PowerPoint, formats, service — then fell during hardening. Read against the commit classification, the dip is not a slowdown but a change of character: from producing features to producing the mechanisms that let features be produced safely. The bar heights mark the interval; the classification, not the height, tells you what changed. Read that way, the curve is my own shift from coder to architect, drawn as a line — an interpretation of the coded record, not a measurement the bars make on their own.

The pace was extreme, and the human cost is part of the record — set down in the field note below, not celebrated here. The methodological point is the durable one: a fleet run this hard needs machinery that holds the line when its operator cannot, because the operator's attention is exactly what runs out first.

Field note — the unhealthy pace is not the method

The pace left marks on the days. Sleep dropped to four or five hours a night; to stay ahead of the subscription rate limits I ran up to four top-tier plans in round-robin and scheduled my own body across them. Once, to unlock a third account when two were saturated, I needed a two-factor code from a student off at a corporate retreat, and a second student at the next table walked it over — three-factor authentication, I called it. The real lesson was plainer: that 2FA code was not authentication so much as another unit of synthetic labor coming online. I worked at the kitchen counter while the family slept; during a campus network outage I sprinted between two buildings so the agents would not stall waiting on me. This pace is evidence about the experiment, not a recommended operating method. A production discipline should reduce dependence on an operator behaving this way — the whole point of the machinery in the chapters that follow is to let the line be held without it. Keep the detail for what it shows about the system, not as something to emulate.

The clean stages read like a plan. They were not one. Each is a line drawn afterward through a run of dated, ordinary moments — a context handoff mid-task, a rate-limit ceiling hit at 2 a.m., agents left orchestrating unattended, a build bill that made a design choice for me, a network outage the fleet had to survive. Each exposed a property of the system or a mechanism it still lacked. Figure 5.3-2 plots the real days behind the clean stages.

Read the curve as a decision, not an accident. In the prototype the apparatus was smaller than the product — 0.85×, below parity — because there was barely a product to govern. By mechanization it had crossed production to 2.48×, and by hardening it peaked at 3.68×: roughly three and a half times the code it guarded. The governance was built first, then the features leaned on it. The support ratio is the Governed Engineering Environment measured against the product it governs.

The final-window dip to 3.06× is not a retreat. Support did not fall — it grew another 21% in absolute terms; production simply grew faster, up 45%, as feature work resumed on the finished environment. A falling ratio here signals a stabilized foundation being spent, not an abandoned one. Across the whole span the apparatus crossed from sub-parity to about three times the product while production itself grew roughly eighteen-fold — larger than production across every mature window, built out ahead of the features that leaned on it.

One honesty note keeps the number from overclaiming. The census fails loud over seven primary source roots, so the counting-surface gap is zero at every window — no file the tool was asked to count slipped past it. About a fifth of the raw tree sits outside the census by design (root configuration, assets, non-primary test corpora, this book itself), and that side-tree share is roughly flat across the mature windows, so the curve tracks a real shift in how effort was spent, not an artifact of the category map moving under it. Lines of code remain a coarse proxy for effort; the ratio is a revealed preference across one repository and one fleet, not a controlled comparison of governed against ungoverned work.

5.3.5 The shape of the churn

Under the ratio sits the raw motion of the code: lines added and lines deleted, week over week, on the two paths that carry the product — web/, the Python service and worker, and backend/, the C# tool and rule engine. Mechanization — the wave where the fleet learned to build the environment — is where both paths move hardest; backend/ alone rewrote itself in that window. Two constructs stay apart here: repository motion is added and deleted lines, what git counts; churn in the theoretical sense is effort spent undoing and reconciling rather than advancing. Then the deletions collapse and both paths turn net-additive, window after window — consistent with stabilization after mechanization and hardening, though the case cannot isolate how much of the decline the governing apparatus caused rather than ordinary product maturation.

One caveat keeps the numbers honest, and a measurement now bounds it. These counts come from git's own line accounting, so generated bundles and vendored trees ride along with hand-authored source. Partitioning each changed line by path shows how much that inflates the totals. On backend/ the answer is none: zero generated lines. On web/ it is 14.6% of added lines, almost all in the mechanization window (24.5% there), when the TypeScript-to-JavaScript build output and wire-contract codegen were still tracked; once the compiled output was git-ignored the generated share dropped to 1-2%. Across both paths combined it is 5.2%, because backend/ dominates and carries none of it. So read the bars as a churn signal, not a hand-authored source count — the inflation is bounded and time-localized, and the curve is predominantly non-generated tracked source everywhere else.

For a stretch of the build, nearly every unwelcome surprise came from deployment, and they shared a shape. The unit tests stayed green; the lints passed. What broke was never a statement in a file but a behavior of the running system — a cold start a hundred times its warm latency, a gate budget sized for a regime that no longer held, two services contending for a core, a dependency graph that woke in the wrong order. None of these lived in the statics. They lived in the dynamics, invisible to any check that reads one layer alone, because each coupling ran across five layers at once: the code, the lints that guard it, the tests that exercise it, the infrastructure-as-code that places it, and the deploy pipeline that sequences it.

That is the case for testing the dynamics, not only the statics — and for the harder move underneath it. A one-off fix to a cold start clears today's surprise and leaves the next to be found the same expensive way, on the next real deploy. The durable answer turns each discovered dynamic into a mechanism that watches it: a measured cold-start number fed to a model that recomputes the worst-case path, a budget derived rather than guessed, an ordering asserted rather than assumed. A finite review of a diff never had a chance against these — the coupling is not on the diff. Only a mechanism watching the whole running dynamic holds the line as the fleet's deploys speed up.

5.3.6 The kinds of work it required

Read this timeline as a catalog of the kinds of work agentic development turned out to need — more useful than any feature list. The code was cheap. Everything around the code was where the judgment went, and the same handful of activities recurred at every stage:

  • Framing the problem. Deciding what "accessible" even meant for a math-dense graduate deck, where the file alone underdetermines the answer.
  • Discovering abstractions. Recognizing PDF as a distinct problem, not "Office with a different extension," and building structured models so structure was compiler-checked instead of passed as anonymous primitives.
  • Deciding local versus structural. For each failure, judging whether it was a one-off bug or the symptom of a missing mechanism — the single most repeated act in the whole record.
  • Building the checks. Standards-grounded validation, content-preservation checks that assert the input survives into the output, hundreds of project-specific lints atop a maxed-out commodity floor, and a tiered regression suite from smoke tests to fuzz campaigns.

Learn more about this governance mechanism: content-preservation checks.

  • Governing the fleet. A dispatch-and-track workflow for parallel agents, isolated worktrees, a pre-commit gate whose passage is proven by a hash-keyed marker file, an agent registry that authoritatively answers "who is in flight," and lifecycle records for every agent that lands or dies.

Learn more about this governance mechanism: agent registry.

  • Operating and deploying. Cloud deployment with staged gates, quota and cost controls, observability channels, audit trails that reconstruct a finished document's history, and runbooks for the failures that recur.

Read as a list, those are the chapters of this book. Every one answers a failure this timeline produced, and the recurring move that produced them all is the same: velocity surfaces a failure; judgment classifies it as local or structural; the structural ones get converted into a durable mechanism that narrows the next agent's room to fail. That move, applied over and over, is what the rest of this book laid out, told slowly enough that you can do it too.

By the end of the study period the system could process Office and PDF files to pass or substantially improve their accessibility scores. A representative graduate slide deck processed in about a minute for about a dollar, under a warm-start service on the study's model mix; a cold start or a heavier deck costs more, and the metrics chapter gives the distribution. The system was patent-pending as of this edition and running as a deployed service used by named internal colleagues on real university documents — not yet adopted or supported as an institutional university service — under the name DocAble. The direct development cost was about sixty thousand dollars, most of it my salary, with a few thousand each in model inference, cloud hosting, and subscriptions. That is the arc. How it was governed is the story the rest of this book has already told.
