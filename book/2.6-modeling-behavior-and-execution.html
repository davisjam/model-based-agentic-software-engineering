Part 2 — Modeling  ::  Chapter 10

2.6 Modeling Behavior and Execution

The structural models of the last chapter hold the system still. They name parts, dependencies, owners, and permitted boundaries; they leave time out. A design that looks correct part by part can fail once the parts run: two workers race on one record, lock order hangs a process, a lifecycle re-enters a terminal state, or a service lands on the wrong execution tier. Those failures live in behavior and execution: what runs, in what order, under whose ownership, and where.

This chapter asks four questions, each one raw code makes you reconstruct in your head:

  • What states can the system enter, and which transitions are legal?
  • What may run concurrently — and what must stay true across every interleaving?
  • Where does each part run?
  • Which execution decisions must remain invariant across environments, rather than vary as configuration?

A behavioral model turns each into a checked fact. The rest of the chapter takes them in that order, one representative model at a time, and closes on the lesson the placement models share: correctness dependencies belong in the graph; host-specific resource limits belong in a profile. Measurement waits for the next chapter, where the question changes from what may the system do? to what does the running system promise, observe, and record?

2.6.1 Reading behavior formally

The models below share a small formal vocabulary. You need it at the level required to use it, not a survey of the field. One inset covers that level.

Inset — Reading behavior formally

A state machine declares a finite set of states and the legal transitions among them. A leased job is queued, leased, running, or reclaimed; a worker taking the lease moves queued → leased, a lease expiring moves leased → queued. Draw it and the illegal edges show themselves — a path back to running from reclaimed is the double-execution bug, visible before the code runs. The state machine is the spine a behavior invariant attaches to.

Behavioral properties ask questions about reachable executions, not about one state. Two shapes cover almost all of them, and each names one operator worth memorizing:

  • Safety — □P ("always P"). Something bad never occurs. "A leased job always has exactly one owner." You settle it by searching the reachable states for any that violates P.
  • Liveness — P ↝ Q ("P leads-to Q"). Some good outcome eventually follows. "A preempted job eventually re-runs"; "a completed worktree eventually tombstones." No single state settles it — it is a claim about the sequence, so it needs a checker that reasons about cyclic runs.

The checker must match the property. Route a liveness property to a safety runtime and the runtime structurally cannot see the violation — it reports green. So the shape is checked too (below).

Bounded model checking explores the executions represented by a model up to a bound k. For a safety property it either finds a counterexample within that bound or establishes that none exists within the explored model and bound. It is stronger than sampling a few schedules — a test samples the schedules you thought of; a distributed race hides in the one you did not — but it is not an unbounded proof: behavior requiring k+1 steps, or omitted by the model, remains outside the claim. That is the price of a decidable check.

That is the whole vocabulary these models use: a state machine, an invariant as □P or P ↝ Q, and an exhaustive walk to depth k. The appendix primer adds the operators (◇, Until) and edge cases this Part does not.

2.6.2 A remediation job's lifecycle

Job lifecycle · Behavioral

  • Engineering question — Can a job move only through valid states, and are its terminal states truly terminal?
  • Model — a state machine: the states a job occupies and the table of legal transitions.
  • Property — illegal transitions are unreachable; a failure surfaces as a terminal state, and it never silently loops back into processing.
  • Quality attribute — correctness, reliability.

The inset drew a leased job in miniature. DocAble's own job is the full version, and it answers the first question the chapter promised: can a job move only through valid states, and are its terminal states truly terminal?

A long-running job passes through many stages, and each stage sets a status field. When the status is a bare string any code can overwrite, the lifecycle lives only in the programmers' heads. A job jumps from done back to processing. A failed job gets picked up again. The bugs are not in any one stage; they live in the transitions nobody wrote down. Read off the raw code, the answer is scattered across a dispatcher, a worker, and a merge handler, each holding one arc of a job's life.

The state machine gathers it into one place. A job enters queued; a dispatcher claims it; the claimed job is planned and split into chunks — the unit of work a single worker remediates. It then waits while its chunks run, merges their results, finalizes the output, and reaches completed. That is the whole happy path. The production machine distinguishes more intermediate states; the teaching view collapses the three planning states into one node. One edge matters as much as any of them: every active state can also move to failed, and a failed job does not loop back to try again — it lands in a terminal state and surfaces to the user. Figure 2.6-1 draws the states and that one-way edge into failure.

Two properties are worth stating precisely, because they are what the model buys:

  • Illegal moves cannot happen. Completed → processing is not in the table, so the code cannot express it. One transition function is the only way to change state, and it refuses — loudly — any edge the table does not list.
  • Failure is a dead end, not a detour. A failed job surfaces; it does not quietly re-enter the pipeline. That single decision — failure never requeues — is what breaks the retry storm Part V tells the story of.

Both facts rest on the same shape: a declared transition table plus one guarded operator that is the only writer of state. The one illustrative invariant carries it:

Table 2.6-1. One invariant of the job-lifecycle state machine — an illegal transition cannot be reached and a terminal state does not re-enter processing.
InvariantTemporal shapeHow it is checked
Illegal transitions are unreachable, and a terminal state never re-enters processing□P (safety)The machine exposes one operator to change state, and it raises on any edge absent from the declared transition table — so an illegal move cannot be reached in code. Terminal states carry no outgoing edge into processing. A build-time drift check reconciles the declared table against the running code, so the two cannot silently part.

The model also exposes a boundary between workflow and ownership. Claiming work is not just another status string. When the planner claims a job, a database compare-and-set moves it from queued to remediating only if it is still queued; a second consumer racing for the same job matches zero rows and does nothing. That compare-and-set is where the next model begins.

A behavioral model makes the lifecycle a first-class object instead of an emergent property of scattered assignments. Write the states as an enum, the transitions as a table, and route every change through one guarded function. Now the system is in a legal state is something you can assert, and this transition is impossible is something the runtime holds for you. The move that pays: name the states explicitly, and make the transition table the single writer.

State machines are ordinary computing machinery; what is less common in the industrial cases examined here is treating application lifecycles themselves as explicit, checked engineering models. Siemens uses rich behavioral models for physical systems, but that is a different engineering question. The discrete application-lifecycle model is therefore a MAGE emphasis rather than a common pattern in the cases examined here.

Authority surface — A job moves only through legal transitions, and a terminal state never re-enters processing. The model makes that obligation explicit; Part III asks which mechanism should hold it and at which boundary.

2.6.3 Ownership over the lifecycle, a specialization

The state machine says what states a job may occupy. It does not say who holds a job while it sits in one, or how many may hold it at once. Answer that and the concurrency questions this chapter opened with come into focus — races, dropped work, torn writes — scoped tightly to one running job.

Job ownership · Behavioral / Structural

  • Engineering question — Can two workers ever own the same chunk of work at once, and what happens to a chunk when its owner dies?
  • Model — the behavioral machine with an owner and a lease added, over the queue the owner claims from.
  • Property — at most one active owner per chunk; a crash before acknowledgement re-delivers the work rather than dropping it; failure never requeues — the one sanctioned requeue is a planned preemption.
  • Quality attribute — safety, consistency, reliability.

The moment work is claimed by one of many workers, two ugly questions appear. Can two workers grab the same piece at once and do it twice? And if a worker dies mid-task, is the work lost, or does someone notice and re-drive it? Do it twice and you double-charge the customer and corrupt the merge. Lose it and the job hangs forever. Both stay invisible until they aren't.

A chunk is claimed by exactly one worker. The claim is a compare-and-set against the database: set this chunk to being worked on, but only if it is still queued. Exactly one claimant wins the row; a duplicate delivery matches zero rows and quietly stops, having done nothing twice. On top of the claim sits a lease: a claimed task is invisible to other workers until the owner acknowledges it or the lease times out. If the owner crashes before acknowledging, the lease lapses and the task comes back. Figure 2.6-2 separates the two — the lease that recovers from a crash and the claim that grants the one permission to act.

The lease and the claim solve different problems. The lease keeps work recoverable when a worker disappears; the compare-and-set grants one worker permission to perform the side effect. A worker can be slow rather than dead — it finishes the real work, then has its task redelivered because it took too long — and the redelivered claim loses the compare-and-set and acknowledges without repeating the side effect. So a duplicate delivery can occur without producing a duplicate effect. At-most-once effect rests on the durable claim, not on the lease.

Redelivery is a transport and recovery event; permission to perform is a state-transition decision. Conflating them makes an availability mechanism look like a correctness mechanism.

And there is exactly one sanctioned way for work to re-enter the application's queue: a planned preemption, when the platform recycles a container that had not failed. The in-flight work re-enters ahead of fresh work of its size, its place preserved. Broker redelivery after a lease lapses is not the same operation as this application-level requeue: a lapsed lease lets the transport re-deliver a task, while the application's own requeue path stays reserved for preemption. Timeout and crash do not take that requeue path; they surface as failed. That distinction is narrow and deliberate, and something watches to keep it narrow.

Ownership is a lease plus a claim over a lifecycle. When many actors draw from a shared pool of work, represent single ownership explicitly, and put a correctness net under the lease — an atomic claim a duplicate loses. The move: do not trust the lease for correctness; trust the atomic state change the lease is wrapped around.

The underlying pattern is familiar — distributed locks, leader election, and at-most-once effects under at-least-once delivery — but the cases examined here rarely elevate the race itself into an explicit checked model.

Ownership bounds who holds a single job in flight. The models that follow widen the question — from one owned job to what must stay true across every schedule of the machinery around it.

Authority surface — At most one active owner per chunk; a crash before acknowledgement redelivers the work; failure never requeues except a planned preemption. The model makes those obligations explicit; Part III asks which mechanism should hold them and at which boundary.

2.6.4 What must hold across every interleaving

What runs at once, and what stays true across every schedule? Concurrency is where a correct-in-isolation design becomes a source of races, deadlocks, and torn writes, and the answer cannot be a thought experiment about interleavings. It has to be a declared, checked contract. Three models carry it; each compresses to its property here, with the full card in the appendix.

Synchronization model · Behavioral (compressed here; full card in the appendix)

A typed registry of every OS-level lock: which shared resource each guards, and the required acquisition ordering between them. Declaring the ordering turns deadlock-avoidance into a graph check — an ordering lint walks each code path's acquisition sequence against the declared graph, and a held lock reaching for one that must precede it is a finding, so the deadlock is caught before it can hang. Property: no acquisition inverts a declared ordering. Full card: synchronization model

Two ways to make a concurrent write safe (compressed here; full cards in the appendix)

Above the raw locks sit two registries whose distinction is worth naming, because confusing them is how a corruption bug hides inside a load limit.

  • The mediator registry answers how many. It declares which heavy or shared-resource invocations run through a serializer and at what cap; its failure class is resource contention.
  • The single-writer registry answers who. It declares that one function, and only one, may write a given piece of shared state; its failure class is a torn or interleaved write — which a concurrency cap of two would happily permit, because two mediated processes racing on one record is still within cap.

Concurrency control bounds contention; the single-writer registry bounds ownership. Two "safely rationed" writers can still corrupt a shared file: the semaphore rations contention and guards nothing that decides who writes. Full card: concurrency contracts

Formal invariant verification · trunk machinery (compressed here; full card in the appendix)

All three concurrency models hang their invariant tables on one shared method — trunk machinery, not a model of the running system. An invariant's temporal shape routes its exhaustive checker: a safety form (□P) to a state-space search, a liveness form (P ↝ Q) to a temporal model checker. Declare the shape wrong and the safety runtime cannot see the violation and reports green, so a lint checks the declared shape against the checker that ran — the shape is consumed, and a mis-routed invariant is a build failure instead of a false all-clear. Full card: formal invariant verification

2.6.5 Where each part runs

Where does each part run, and which of those placements is a correctness decision? A functionally correct system can still be mis-deployed. Placement is not a detail an agent discovers after the fact in scattered deployment configuration — it is a fact the fleet should read before it deploys.

The deployment-topology model makes it one: a typed statement of where each service runs — its name, its owning layer, its tier class. From that, the deploy scripts reason about a declared topology instead of scattered constants, and a parity check holds the model to reality in both directions. A parity set-diff run model ⊆ reality catches a tier that drifted in the code; run reality ⊆ model it catches a service that shipped with no model row; either mismatch fails the build. The illustrative invariant — every declared service deploys at the tier the model names, and every deployed service appears in the model — is a □P safety property, and the record's schema and derivation direction are in the appendix. 2.6.6 Correctness in the graph, resource limits in a profile

The placement models raise one more question — what resources constrain execution? — and answering it well turns on a single modeling principle, the most portable idea in this chapter: correctness dependencies stay in the graph; host-specific resource limits live in a scheduler profile.

What must be true on every host — B is wrong without A — belongs in the deploy graph. How hard a particular box may be pushed — how many steps run at once, whether a cost gate is worth honoring — belongs in that host's profile, read by a scheduler. Move the stress between hosts and you edit the profile, never the graph: profile edit, not graph edit.

The invariant-DAG execution policy embodies it. Each deploy-graph edge carries a typed intent: CORRECTNESS (B is wrong without A; honored on every host) and COST_GATE (a cheap check gating an expensive step; honored unless the budget is unbounded) stay in the graph, host-identical. The LOAD intent — B contends with A for a scarce box — is banned from the graph and migrated to the scheduler, which reads a per-host profile of a concurrency ceiling and a budget and emits the execution plan. The mapping is a pure function of the profile, small enough to read as a table:

| Host profile | Ceiling | Budget | Emitted plan | |---|---|---|---| | Elastic staging | none (unbounded) | ∞ (unbounded) | fan the whole ready wave out; relax cost gates | | Metered prod | low finite | finite | small concurrency; honor cost gates | | Scarce local box | 1 | finite | serialize; honor cost gates |

Moving stress between hosts edits one row of this table. The one illustrative invariant keeps the graph portable:

Table 2.6-2. One invariant of the invariant-DAG execution policy — a load edge in the deploy graph is banned.
InvariantTemporal shapeHow it is checked
No deploy edge carries the LOAD intent□P (safety)A load-edge lint reads the graph's resident intent set; a LOAD edge — the thing that would fork the graph per host — is a finding. Load rationing is a semaphore in the scheduler, never a graph edge.

The edge-intent schema, the profile record, the scheduler code, and the full invariant set are in the appendix; the model is also walked end to end in this Part's closing join example. Appendix C

2.6.7 What This Model Leaves Open

The policy models the load-and-cost side of execution, but not request latency, cache-hit ratios, or data-movement cost. That omission is deliberate — model only where a failure lives: a model with no recurring decision or named risk to exercise it rots before anyone checks it. Part V reconstructs the cold-start incident that exposed what leaving this question unmodeled could cost.

---

These models make execution explicit: legal states, ownership, interleavings, placement, and host-dependent resource policy. They answer what the system may do and how it is allowed to run. They do not yet answer whether the running system is meeting its quantitative promises, or whether its account of what happened remains true. Those are evidence questions.
