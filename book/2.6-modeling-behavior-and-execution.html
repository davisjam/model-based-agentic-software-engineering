Part 2 — Modeling  ::  Chapter 10

2.6 Modeling Behavior and Execution

The structural models of the last chapter hold the system still. They name the parts, the dependencies, the owners — and they leave time out. A design that is correct read part by part can still fail once the parts run: two workers race on one record, an inverted lock ordering hangs the build, a service correct in every test ships to the wrong tier and pages a user at 3 a.m. Those are not structural bugs. They live in behavior — what runs when, in what order, on which box.

This chapter builds the models that make behavior analyzable. Each answers a question raw code makes you reconstruct in your head:

  • What states can the system enter, and which transitions are legal?
  • What can run concurrently — and what must stay true across every interleaving?
  • What resources constrain execution?
  • Where does each part run?
  • Which deployment decisions are correctness decisions, not configuration trivia?

A behavioral model turns each into a checked fact. The rest of the chapter takes them in that order, one representative model at a time, and closes on the lesson the placement models share: correctness dependencies belong in the graph; host-specific resource limits belong in a profile.

2.6.1 Reading behavior formally

The models below share a small formal vocabulary. You need it at the level required to use it, not a survey of the field. One inset covers that level; the full primer, with the operators these models do not use, is in the appendix. Appendix G

Inset — Reading behavior formally

A state machine is a finite set of states and the legal transitions between them. A leased job is queued, leased, running, or reclaimed; a worker taking the lease moves queued → leased, a lease expiring moves leased → queued. Draw it and the illegal edges show themselves — a path back to running from reclaimed is the double-execution bug, visible before the code runs. The state machine is the spine a behavior invariant attaches to.

An invariant over behavior is a predicate over an order of events, not over one state. Two shapes cover almost all of them, and each names one operator worth memorizing:

  • Safety — □P ("always P"). A bad thing never happens. "A leased job always has exactly one owner." You settle it by searching the reachable states for any that violate P.
  • Liveness — P ↝ Q ("P leads-to Q"). A good thing eventually happens. "A preempted job eventually re-runs"; "a completed worktree eventually tombstones." No single state settles it — it is a claim about the sequence, so it needs a checker that reasons about cyclic runs.

Declaring the shape is not decoration: it routes the checker. Route a liveness property to a safety runtime and the runtime structurally cannot see the violation — it reports green. So the shape is checked too (below).

Bounded model checking settles a safety property the honest way a test cannot. A test samples the schedules you thought of; a distributed race hides in the one you did not. Model checking exhaustively walks every interleaving out to a fixed depth k, and either shows no reachable state violates the predicate or hands back the exact counterexample trace. The limit is the bound: a bug needing k+1 steps, or one the model abstracts away, is out of scope — the price of a decidable check.

That is the whole vocabulary these models use: a state machine, an invariant as □P or P ↝ Q, and an exhaustive walk to depth k. The appendix primer adds the operators (◇, Until) and edge cases this Part does not.

2.6.2 A remediation job's lifecycle

Job lifecycle · Behavioral

  • Engineering question — Can a job move only through valid states, and are its terminal states truly terminal?
  • Model — a state machine: the states a job occupies and the table of legal transitions.
  • Property — illegal transitions are unreachable; a failure surfaces as a terminal state, and it never silently loops back into processing.
  • Quality attribute — correctness, reliability.

The inset drew a leased job in miniature. DocAble's own job is the full version, and it answers the first question the chapter promised: can a job move only through valid states, and are its terminal states truly terminal?

A long-running job passes through many stages, and each stage sets a status field. When the status is a bare string any code can overwrite, the lifecycle lives only in the programmers' heads. A job jumps from done back to processing. A failed job gets picked up again. The bugs are not in any one stage; they live in the transitions nobody wrote down. Read off the raw code, the answer is scattered across a dispatcher, a worker, and a merge handler, each holding one arc of a job's life.

The state machine gathers it into one place. A job enters queued; a dispatcher claims it; the claimed job is planned and split into chunks — the unit of work a single worker remediates. It then waits while its chunks run, merges their results, finalizes the output, and reaches completed. That is the whole happy path. The production machine distinguishes more intermediate states; the teaching view collapses the three planning states into one node. One edge matters as much as any of them: every active state can also move to failed, and a failed job does not loop back to try again — it lands in a terminal state and surfaces to the user.

Two properties are worth stating precisely, because they are what the model buys:

  • Illegal moves cannot happen. Completed → processing is not in the table, so the code cannot express it. One transition function is the only way to change state, and it refuses — loudly — any edge the table does not list.
  • Failure is a dead end, not a detour. A failed job surfaces; it does not quietly re-enter the pipeline. That single decision — failure never requeues — is what breaks the retry storm Part V tells the story of.

Both facts rest on the same shape: a declared transition table plus one guarded operator that is the only writer of state. The one illustrative invariant carries it:

Table 2.6-1. One invariant of the job-lifecycle state machine — an illegal transition cannot be reached and a terminal state does not re-enter processing.
InvariantTemporal shapeHow it is checked
Illegal transitions are unreachable, and a terminal state never re-enters processing□P (safety)The machine exposes one operator to change state, and it raises on any edge absent from the declared transition table — so an illegal move cannot be reached in code. Terminal states carry no outgoing edge into processing. A build-time drift check reconciles the declared table against the running code, so the two cannot silently part.

The model also exposes a boundary between workflow and ownership. Claiming work is not just another status string. When the planner claims a job, a database compare-and-set moves it from queued to remediating only if it is still queued; a second consumer racing for the same job matches zero rows and does nothing. That compare-and-set is where the next model begins.

A behavioral model makes the lifecycle a first-class object instead of an emergent property of scattered assignments. Write the states as an enum, the transitions as a table, and route every change through one guarded function. Now the system is in a legal state is something you can assert, and this transition is impossible is something the runtime holds for you. The move that pays: name the states explicitly, and make the transition table the single writer.

State machines themselves are everywhere in computing — TCP guards its illegal transitions, an order that cannot move from shipped back to cart enforces a terminal state, and workflow engines run on explicit transition tables. But the discrete job-lifecycle machine, drawn and governed as a model, is where the industrial cases we surveyed go quiet. The software teams document structure, policy, and measurement; none records a discrete lifecycle of legal transitions like this one. The nearest neighbor, Siemens, builds behavioral models heavily — but as continuous physics: finite-element models and executable digital twins that predict how a physical system responds, sitting upstream of the software rather than bounding a job's lifecycle. Stretching that into a homolog would misread it. The silence scopes the claim rather than crowns it: the discrete-behavior model is one MAGE leans on and the corpus rarely writes down.

Handoff to Alignment — what states a job may occupy → the job-lifecycle state machine → illegal transitions unreachable and terminal states that do not re-enter → correctness, reliability → in Alignment the property acquires authority: the single transition operator that refuses an unlisted edge is the constraint the code cannot route around; the stream of transitions is the sensor; an exhaustive walk of the reachable states is the validator that proves no illegal state is reachable; and the single-consumer claim is a gate checked both in the build and at run time.

Ownership over the lifecycle, a specialization

The state machine says what states a job may occupy. It does not say who holds a job while it sits in one, or how many may hold it at once. Answer that and the concurrency questions this chapter opened with come into focus — races, dropped work, torn writes — scoped tightly to one running job.

This is a specialization of the behavioral and structural views, not a sixth class of model. Add an owner and a lease to the behavioral machine, add the queue the owner claims work from to the structural picture, and you have it. The five classes stay five; the specialization sits beneath two of them.

Job ownership · Behavioral / Structural specialization

  • Engineering question — Can two workers ever own the same chunk of work at once, and what happens to a chunk when its owner dies?
  • Model — the behavioral machine with an owner and a lease added, over the queue the owner claims from.
  • Property — at most one active owner per chunk; a crash before acknowledgement re-delivers the work rather than dropping it; failure never requeues — the one sanctioned requeue is a planned preemption.
  • Quality attribute — safety, consistency, reliability.

The moment work is claimed by one of many workers, two ugly questions appear. Can two workers grab the same piece at once and do it twice? And if a worker dies mid-task, is the work lost, or does someone notice and re-drive it? Do it twice and you double-charge the customer and corrupt the merge. Lose it and the job hangs forever. Both stay invisible until they aren't.

Ownership layers onto the job machine — which is why we present it as a specialization rather than a new class. A chunk is claimed by exactly one worker. The claim is a compare-and-set against the database: set this chunk to being worked on, but only if it is still queued. Exactly one claimant wins the row; a duplicate delivery matches zero rows and quietly stops, having done nothing twice. On top of the claim sits a lease: a claimed task is invisible to other workers until the owner acknowledges it or the lease times out. If the owner crashes before acknowledging, the lease lapses and the task comes back.

Here is the subtle, load-bearing point. The lease alone does not give you exactly-once effects. A worker can be slow rather than dead: it finishes the real work, then gets its task redelivered because it took too long. What saves you is the compare-and-set — the redelivered claim loses the race and acknowledges without repeating the side effect. Exactly-once rests on the claim, not the lease. The lease provides crash recovery; the compare-and-set provides the correctness net.

And there is exactly one sanctioned way for work to re-enter the queue: a planned preemption, when the platform recycles a container that had not failed. The in-flight work re-enters ahead of fresh work of its size, its place preserved. Timeout and crash do not requeue; they surface as failed. That distinction is narrow and deliberate, and something watches to keep it narrow.

The production model runs two dispatch planes and eight queue structures. The teaching view keeps one work queue, one in-flight lease, one claim, and the two edges that matter: crash-before-ack re-delivers, and failure goes to failed, never back to the queue — except preemption.

Ownership is a lease plus a claim over a lifecycle. When many actors draw from a shared pool of work, represent single ownership explicitly, and put a correctness net under the lease — an atomic claim a duplicate loses. The move: do not trust the lease for correctness; trust the atomic state change the lease is wrapped around.

You will recognize the shape elsewhere: distributed locks, leader election, exactly-once processing. Message brokers offer at-least-once delivery and leave exactly-once effects to you; the idempotency key on a payment API is the same compare-and-set trick under another name. Across the industrial cases we surveyed, though, this distributed race is rarely written down as a governed model — the pattern is common knowledge and seldom stated as a checked obligation.

Ownership bounds who holds a single job in flight. The models that follow widen the question — from one owned job to what must stay true across every schedule of the machinery around it.

Handoff to Alignment — who may own a chunk of work → the ownership specialization → at most one active owner, crash-before-ack redelivers, failure never requeues except preemption → safety, consistency, reliability → in Alignment the property acquires authority: the lease-and-acknowledge together with the compare-and-set are the constraint that makes a second owner unexpressible; a stale-work sweep is the sensor that notices a dropped owner; the single-owner rule is the validator; and the preemption path is the one exception the gate is written to allow. The distributed race the surveyed corpus rarely governs is here made a checked fact.

2.6.3 What must hold across every interleaving

What runs at once, and what stays true across every schedule? Concurrency is where a correct-in-isolation design becomes a source of races, deadlocks, and torn writes, and the answer cannot be a thought experiment about interleavings. It has to be a declared, checked contract.

The synchronization model is the first. It is a typed registry of every OS-level lock: which shared resource each guards, and the required acquisition ordering between them. Declaring the ordering turns deadlock-avoidance into a graph check. Here is the one illustrative invariant.

Table 2.6-2. One invariant of the synchronization model — an inverted lock acquisition is caught at author time.
InvariantTemporal shapeHow it is checked
No acquisition inverts a declared ordering□P (safety)An ordering lint walks each code path's acquisition sequence against the declared ordering graph; a held lock acquiring one that must precede it is an inversion, and a finding — the deadlock is caught before it can hang.

Two ways to make a concurrent write safe

Above the raw locks sit two registries that draw a distinction worth naming, because confusing them is how a corruption bug hides inside a load limit. There are two ways to make a concurrent write safe, and they fail differently.

  • The mediator registry answers how many. It declares which heavy or shared-resource invocations must run through a serializer — the test runner, the build tool, a whole-repo lint mutex — and at what cap (1 for a mutex, M for a semaphore). Its failure class is resource contention: too many heavy processes on one box. A coverage lint fails any unmediated call to a mediated class at author time.
  • The single-writer registry answers who. It declares that one function, and only one, may write a given piece of shared state. Its failure class is a torn or interleaved write — which a concurrency cap of two would happily permit, because two mediated processes racing on one record is still within cap. A second discovered writer of that state is a finding.

Concurrency control bounds contention; the single-writer registry bounds ownership. Only naming both keeps a corruption bug from hiding behind a semaphore that was only ever rationing load. This one cost me a day of chasing a corrupted registry file before I saw that two "safely rationed" writers were the whole problem: the semaphore was doing its job and guarding nothing that mattered. The verification backbone

All three concurrency models hang their invariant tables on one shared method, and it is trunk machinery rather than a model of the running system. Formal invariant verification takes an invariant's temporal shape and routes the exhaustive checker for it: a safety form (□P) to a state-space search, a liveness form (P ↝ Q) to a temporal model checker. Get the shape wrong — declare a liveness property as safety — and the safety runtime cannot see the violation and reports green. So a lint checks the declared shape against the checker that ran: the shape is consumed, and a mis-routed invariant is a build failure instead of a false all-clear. Its full construct-and-invariant treatment is in the appendix. Appendix G

2.6.4 Where each part runs

Where does each part run, and which of those placements is a correctness decision? A functionally correct system can still be mis-deployed. Placement is not a detail an agent discovers after the fact in scattered deployment configuration — it is a fact the fleet should read before it deploys.

The deployment-topology model makes it one. It is a typed statement of where each service runs: its name, its owning layer, its tier class. From that, the deploy scripts reason about a declared topology instead of scattered constants, and a parity check reconciles it against the real deploy table. The one illustrative invariant runs in both directions.

Table 2.6-3. One invariant of the deployment-topology model — bidirectional parity holds the model to the real deploy table.
InvariantTemporal shapeHow it is checked
Every declared service deploys at the tier the model names, and every deployed service appears in the model□P (safety)A parity set-diff, run both ways: model ⊆ reality catches a tier that drifted in the code; reality ⊆ model catches a service that shipped with no model row. Either mismatch fails the build.

Neither direction is optional. Check only model ⊆ reality and you miss a service that shipped without a model row; check only reality ⊆ model and you miss a tier that drifted in the code. 2.6.5 Correctness in the graph, resource limits in a profile

The placement models raise one more question — what resources constrain execution? — and answering it well turns on a single modeling principle, the most portable idea in this chapter: correctness dependencies stay in the graph; host-specific resource limits live in a scheduler profile.

What must be true on every host — B is wrong without A — belongs in the deploy graph. How hard a particular box may be pushed — how many steps run at once, whether a cost gate is worth honoring — belongs in that host's profile, read by a scheduler. Move the stress between hosts and you edit the profile, never the graph: profile edit, not graph edit.

The invariant-DAG execution policy embodies it. Each deploy-graph edge carries a typed intent: CORRECTNESS (B is wrong without A; honored on every host) and COST_GATE (a cheap check gating an expensive step; honored unless the budget is unbounded) stay in the graph, host-identical. The LOAD intent — B contends with A for a scarce box — is banned from the graph and migrated to the scheduler, which reads a per-host profile of a concurrency ceiling and a budget and emits the execution plan. The mapping is a pure function of the profile, small enough to read as a table:

| Host profile | Ceiling | Budget | Emitted plan | |---|---|---|---| | Elastic staging | none (unbounded) | ∞ (unbounded) | fan the whole ready wave out; relax cost gates | | Metered prod | low finite | finite | small concurrency; honor cost gates | | Scarce local box | 1 | finite | serialize; honor cost gates |

Moving stress between hosts edits one row of this table. The one illustrative invariant keeps the graph portable:

Table 2.6-4. One invariant of the invariant-DAG execution policy — a load edge in the deploy graph is banned.
InvariantTemporal shapeHow it is checked
No deploy edge carries the LOAD intent□P (safety)A load-edge lint reads the graph's resident intent set; a LOAD edge — the thing that would fork the graph per host — is a finding. Load rationing is a semaphore in the scheduler, never a graph edge.

2.6.6 One honest gap

That policy models the load-and-cost half of execution. The pure-latency half — request latency, cache-hit ratios, data-movement cost — has no dedicated model, and the honest move is to say so rather than invent one to fill the slot. The discipline behind the gap is model only where a failure lives: a model built with no decision, invariant, or named risk to exercise it rots before anyone checks it, because nothing reads it.

The gap has drawn blood once. To cut idle cost, the fleet let its services scale to zero — a change the cost half of the policy welcomed and the latency half could not weigh. The cold-start incident in Metrics is exactly this gap surfacing: a warm per-request floor a fleet-average number hid, run down to a resident-runtime re-architecture that cut it 4,057 → 109 ms. A latency slice here — request latency typed per service, the cold-start penalty an invariant over it — would have weighed that trade before a user waited on it. The gap stays honest; this is what it costs to leave open.

2.6.7 Computing a migration's blast radius

One last behavioral question an agent asks before it changes the ground everything runs on: which mechanisms assume the substrate I am about to replace? The substrate-dependency model makes each governance mechanism declare, as typed metadata, the substrate assumption it bakes in — bound to one substrate, substrate-aware, or substrate-agnostic. Joining those declarations against the topology model on the migration target turns a hopeful grep into a computed blast radius: a declaration lint requires the stance, so the one mechanism whose assumption is implicit cannot hide from the join.

Worked once, on the plane the fleet actually migrated. When the system retired a Redis poll plane for a push plane, the join answered which mechanisms bake in "workers pull from a Redis sorted set"? before the change landed — the queue-depth metric, the stale-job sweep, the cancellation cleanup, the priority-preservation requeue, each declaring "bound to the poll plane" and each surfacing in the table. A substrate-agnostic mechanism that reads only durable Postgres truth declares its stance and correctly does not appear. The typed schema and the join code are in the appendix. Appendix G

2.6.8 What the system promises about time and cost

The structural models say what the system is made of. The behavioral models say what it does when it runs. Neither says what it costs to run, how fast it answers, or whether the running system is still inside those bounds today. That is a measured question, and it needs its own model.

Be clear about which measured question. The execution policy earlier in this chapter rations load — it decides how hard a given box may be pushed, and moves that decision to a scheduler profile. This model asks something the scheduler does not: is the system staying inside the throughput, latency, and cost bounds it promised? One decides a plan; the other checks a fact.

A system that is never measured makes no promise it can keep. It is fast until it is slow, cheap until a runaway job costs a fortune, healthy until the queue backs up. Worse, the numbers that would explain a bad day were never recorded, so every incident is diagnosed from scratch. A configuration constant does not help here: a wall-clock kill at ten minutes tells you when a process will be stopped. It does not tell you whether the expected worst case is approaching ten minutes.

Operational-envelope model · Measurement

  • Engineering question — What does the system promise about latency, cost, and throughput — and is it inside those promises now?
  • Model — a typed statement of the measured quantities that must stay bounded: end-to-end latency, per-job wall-clock, per-call output cost, and queue depth by work-size class, each paired with the ceiling it may not cross.
  • Property — every admitted unit of work runs within its declared resource ceilings; a breach stops the work rather than shipping a degraded result.
  • Quality attribute — performance, scalability, efficiency.

The measured envelope is itself modeled, not left to intuition. A small set of quantities carries the story: the warm end-to-end latency, tracked as measured rows the deploy smoke appends — around twenty-four seconds; a per-job wall-clock ceiling near ten minutes; a hard cap on how many tokens a single generation call may spend; a size classification of each incoming document; and a credit balance — not a per-file page cap — that throttles admission. Each is a promise; each has a bound.

What the model lets you establish is that the running system does not silently leave the envelope. It earns that through a sensor and a gate.

  • The sensor is instrumentation. The pipeline emits phase timings, per-segment timers, server-clock start/finish pairs, and worker heartbeats; a deploy-time budget smoke drives a fixture through the whole pipeline and appends the measured end-to-end row. The promised numbers are compared against measured ones, not hoped for.
  • The gate is admission. Before work begins, an admission gate sizes the job, classifies it, and debits the account's credit balance; a job whose account cannot cover it is refused at the door rather than started and abandoned mid-flight. A second gate refuses a different way: in the staging environment, a fidelity check fails an individual repair pass rather than shipping a regression — it emits a signal and exits with a distinct code the surrounding system reads as this pass did harm; stop.

One illustrative invariant carries the shape:

One quantity, honestly, has no production gate. The per-chunk cost model exists. It is instrumented. It can predict when a chunk's worst-case time would breach the job ceiling. But in production it only reports — it does not split the work or refuse it. The single hard cost cap in the system lives in an offline benchmark harness, not on the live path. We keep that gap in view rather than paper over it. It is a real modeled and sensed, but not yet governed case, and it teaches something the tidy version would hide: a model does not automatically earn blocking authority. Earning it is a later, separate decision. Part III returns to exactly this.

A measurement model names the quantities you promise to hold and the bound on each. Its authority is a gate that reads the sensed quantity and refuses to advance when the bound would break. The move: decide what the measurement must drive — an admission decision, a stop decision — and sense one level below the surface number so the data can act, not merely report.

Two things in this Part are called measurement, and they answer different questions. This model measures the running system against its resource bounds — is it fast enough, cheap enough, inside the envelope now? A later chapter, Keeping the Models in Sync, measures a model against reality — has the map drifted from the territory it claims to describe? One watches the system; the other watches the model. Keep them apart.

This is the service-level-objective, capacity-model, and admission-control tradition. The mature ones publish an error budget and act on it; the immature ones graph the number and never wire it to a decision — exactly the rung DocAble's cost cap still sits on. Three teams show the same move realized on materially different resources:

Worked Examples

Spotify. Spotify gates a merge on measured evidence. An automated merger admits a change only once its verifiers and monitoring have generated the evidence that it is safe, so the measurement is the admission criterion: the system reads a signal about the change and refuses to advance until the signal clears. The measured quantity is verification evidence; the ceiling is "not yet proven safe"; the gate is the merge itself. Same function as the envelope model — measure, then refuse to advance on a breach — realized as an evidence-gated merge.

Zenseact. Zenseact treats context as a scarce resource and rations it against a measured budget. A deterministic router measures how much context a task needs and holds it under a ceiling, cutting what each step carries by a large, measured fraction rather than a guessed one. The measured quantity is context size; the ceiling is the routing budget; the gate is the router that will not hand a step more than its share. A different resource, a different mechanism — but the same move: measure the envelope, ration against the bound.

DocAble. DocAble measures a latency-cost-throughput envelope and gates against it in two places. An admission gate refuses a job whose account cannot cover its measured size before any work starts; an in-pass fidelity gate fails a remediation that regresses quality rather than shipping it. The measured quantities are wall-clock, cost, and queue depth by size class; the ceilings are the job budget and the account balance; the gates are admission and per-pass fidelity. Same function again, realized as a resource-and-quality envelope enforced at the door and at each pass.

Takeaway. Measuring a resource envelope and refusing to advance on a breach is one engineering move; an evidence-gated merge, a rationed context budget, and a latency-cost admission gate are three realizations of it — strong evidence the abstraction is real, not one team's habit.

Handoff to Alignment — what the system promises about latency and cost → the operational-envelope model → every admitted unit of work stays within its declared resource ceilings → performance, scalability, efficiency → in Alignment the property acquires authority: the instrumentation that emits the measured quantities is the sensor supplying evidence; a validator evaluates a run against its declared obligation; the admission gate that refuses over-budget work at the door and the per-pass fidelity gate that fails a quality regression are the gates that act on a breach. One bound stays modeled-but-not-yet-gated — the production cost cap — and Alignment inherits closing it as declared, not hidden, work.

The measured budgets that compose into this envelope — the per-job and per-call ceilings, the size-class weights — are detailed in the model's appendix reference. Each answered one question in isolation.
